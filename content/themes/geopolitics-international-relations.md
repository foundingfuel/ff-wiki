# Geopolitics & International Relations

---

## PART 1 — THE OPENING

Something has broken in the mental model most Indian executives carry into the boardroom. It goes like this: geopolitics is the government's problem. Business finds a way. Tariffs come and go. Supply chains adjust. The world stays open because openness serves everyone.

That model is now a liability.

The separation between commerce and security that made globalisation work — the assumption that a dollar of trade would always outweigh a point of rivalry — has collapsed. What has replaced it is something harder and stranger: a world in which every economic flow, every technology decision, every data routing choice is being read through a national security lens by governments in Washington, Beijing, Brussels, and Delhi simultaneously. The interdependence that India spent three decades building into is being weaponised by the very powers that built it.

Indian practitioners are caught in the middle of this — and most of them don't know it yet. They see the US-China rivalry as a bilateral story. They see geopolitics as risk to hedge, not structure to navigate. They treat strategic autonomy as a philosophical position India's diplomats hold, not a constraint that runs through their own supply chains, their funding rounds, their data architectures, their hiring pipelines.

The conventional understanding fails because it assumes the old rules still operate underneath the new noise. They don't. The rules have changed. Trade is now geopolitics. Technology is now sovereignty. The neighbourhood is now a theatre. And India — which has spent the post-Cold War decades perfecting the art of not choosing — is being forced, transaction by transaction, to choose anyway.

This page will not give you a framework for tracking geopolitical risk. It will give you the principles that actually govern how the new order works — and what that means for every decision you make on Monday.

## PART 2 — PRINCIPLES

---

### Principle 1

> The interdependence that built your business is now the instrument of someone else's coercion.

**What This Means & Why**
Globalisation was sold as a mutual-benefit machine: trade ties nations together, economic integration reduces conflict, everyone wins. That logic held long enough to restructure the world economy. But the same architecture of integration — financial nodes, semiconductor supply chains, internet infrastructure, payment rails — turns out to be an extraordinary weapon when one party decides to use it as one. Control a chokepoint and you can punish any country that passes through it. The network that created prosperity now creates vulnerability. Most people miss this because they still think of interdependence as symmetrical. It never was.

**The India Reality**
India's digital economy runs on foreign cloud infrastructure, foreign-designed chips, and foreign-owned platforms. DigiLocker sits on a foreign cloud. A large share of India's logistics runs on Google Maps. India's fintech stack was built with US and Chinese capital. The chokepoints in India's own digital infrastructure are, in many cases, not in India.

**The Evidence**
In "The US-China war for tech supremacy is splitting the world in two" (2020), G Venkat Raman introduced the concept of "weaponized interdependence" — drawn from political scientists Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman — arguing that power in the international economic order flows to whoever controls the critical nodes: financial communications, supply chains, and the internet. He showed that the US used its control of dollar clearing and semiconductor design to punish Huawei in ways no military action could have achieved. This was not an accident of power — it was a strategy.

**The Implication**
Audit your company's critical dependencies — cloud provider, chip architecture, payment infrastructure, data storage location — and identify which of those sit in a foreign jurisdiction whose government has demonstrated willingness to weaponise access.

### Principle 2

> Strategic autonomy is not a foreign policy doctrine — it is a supply chain problem.

**What This Means & Why**
When Indian officials speak of strategic autonomy, it sounds like diplomacy: India will not be bullied into choosing sides. But the real test of autonomy is not at the UN General Assembly. It is in the degree to which India can actually sustain an independent position when the cost of that position is extracted from its energy imports, its technology access, its export markets, or its defence procurement. A country that imports 80% of its crude through a single chokepoint, sources its frontier chips from one allied ecosystem, and has its data on foreign servers has constrained its own autonomy before a single diplomatic cable is sent.

**The India Reality**
The Gulf Cooperation Council is India's largest trading partner bloc at $178.56 billion in FY 2024-25. More than 80% of India's crude needs are imported, with a large portion flowing through the Strait of Hormuz. Every $10 rise in crude oil prices shaves roughly half a percentage point off GDP growth. India's strategic autonomy in the West Asia theatre is a function of that arithmetic, not of its diplomatic traditions.

**The Evidence**
In "The US–Israel War on Iran: The Risks to India's Strategic Hedging" (2026), Vivek Y. Kelkar argued that "the very region that enables India's strategic autonomy is also the one most capable of constraining it when instability erupts." The Iran war scenario demonstrated that India's doctrine of "de-hyphenation" — maintaining simultaneous relationships with Israel, Arab Gulf states, and Iran — works in peacetime but becomes structurally impossible when one of those relationships demands a position. India had built a doctrine on the assumption that it would not have to choose. The infrastructure said otherwise.

**The Implication**
Map every strategic dependency your organisation or institution carries — energy sources, technology stack, export markets — and ask whether the country controlling that dependency has demonstrated willingness to use it as leverage.

### Principle 3

> China is not a near-peer competitor on technology. That framing is already out of date.

**What This Means & Why**
The phrase "near-peer competitor" implies China is catching up, trailing slightly, aspirationally equivalent. It bakes in an assumption of Western primacy that the evidence no longer supports in key domains. In AI, in electric vehicles, in solar manufacturing, in 5G infrastructure, in battery technology, China is not catching up — it has arrived, and in several areas it leads. The habit of dismissing Chinese innovation as derivative, state-subsidised, or theft-dependent is a cognitive error that has already misled Western policymakers and is now misleading Indian ones. Understanding what China can actually do — and what it cannot — is a precondition for thinking clearly about the rivalry.

**The India Reality**
India's R&D spending has never crossed 1% of GDP, hovering around 0.8-0.9%. In China, 70% of R&D spending comes from business. In India, the ratio is inverted — government does most of the spending. That structural difference in who drives innovation has compounded over decades into a vast capability gap in applied technology.

**The Evidence**
In "The Chinese innovation machine" (2016), Rishikesha T. Krishnan documented that China's R&D investment at $162 billion represented approximately 2% of GDP, with business driving the majority of that spending. He mapped a three-stage model — from "fit for purpose" products for the local market, to world-standard products, to global leadership — and argued that Chinese firms were moving through these stages faster than any prior industrialising country. By 2020, G Venkat Raman, in "The US-China war for tech supremacy is splitting the world in two," was saying plainly: "Beijing's technological capabilities are no more based on the imitation of Western technological products. It would be a grave error to dismiss China as merely a 'near-peer competitor.'"

**The Implication**
Wherever your business competes with Chinese products or platforms — in India or in export markets — benchmark against the actual current product, not the Chinese product you knew five years ago.

### Principle 4

> Technology is politics. There is no technical decision that is not also a geopolitical one.

**What This Means & Why**
Choosing a cloud provider, selecting a chip architecture, deploying a particular AI model, routing data through a particular jurisdiction — each of these is a political act in the current order, whether or not the person making it intends it to be. Governments have made this explicit: the US restricts GPU exports based on AI safety frameworks; the EU's GDPR has become de facto global law through what scholars call the "Brussels Effect"; China has built a digital ecosystem that excludes foreign competition by design. The practitioner who thinks of these as purely technical choices is operating on a model of the world that no longer exists.

**The India Reality**
The US restricts GPU exports to India because India has not created a Frontier AI Safety Framework equivalent to what the UAE built to unlock access. The UAE got its GPU allocation. India did not. A governance gap — not a capability gap — is the binding constraint on India's AI ambitions at the frontier.

**The Evidence**
In "Technology is politics" (2019), N S Ramnath made the argument explicit, using the Huawei-5G controversy as the lens: the question of which country's infrastructure carries a nation's data is inseparable from the question of which country has intelligence access to that nation. Henry Paulson's warning — that if the US forces countries to choose between itself and China, some will choose China, and the US might end up isolating itself — was cited as the highest-stakes version of this logic. By 2025, Sundeep Waslekar in "The Global Politics of AI" extended this: "AI has become the frontier of new geopolitics, dividing the world."

**The Implication**
Before signing any technology infrastructure contract — cloud, AI, connectivity — have your legal and strategy teams assess which government's jurisdiction that infrastructure falls under and what access rights that government claims.

### Principle 5

> Russia punches far above its economic weight, and the mechanism is worth studying.

**What This Means & Why**
Russia's GDP is smaller than that of Guangdong province or New York State. By conventional measures of national power — economic size, trade volume, demographic trajectory — Russia should be a regional power with global pretensions that exceed its reach. Yet from Syria to Mali to Ukraine, Russia has repeatedly extended its influence at costs that dwarf what the US spends to achieve far less. The mechanism is worth unpacking, because it reveals something important about how power actually works in the current fragmented order: speed, narrative, opportunism, and the willingness to accept risk that rivals will not.

**The India Reality**
India's relationship with Russia — maintained through a decade of Western pressure — has delivered real benefits: discounted crude oil, continued defence supply chains, diplomatic cover at the UN. The relationship is not sentimental. It is a calculated read of Russia's continued utility as a counterweight, precisely because Russia punches above its weight and India needs counterweights.

**The Evidence**
In "From Russia with Love" (2021), Samuel Pepys documented in granular detail how Russia had extended influence across Africa, the Middle East, and Central Asia by moving faster, accepting lower returns, and exploiting the vacuums left by Western withdrawal. "Where the US and China pour billions to buy a little goodwill and influence, Russia walks in and takes a controlling interest at virtually no cost. Moscow punching far above its weight may well turn out to be the biggest geopolitical surprise of the 21st century." By 2022, Sundeep Waslekar's quarterly reviews for Founding Fuel had documented Russia's influence reaching levels not seen since the 1970s, despite — or because of — the Ukraine war isolating it from Western financial systems.

**The Implication**
When assessing a country's geopolitical relevance for your business decisions, weight speed of action, narrative control, and risk appetite alongside GDP and trade volume — Russia's playbook shows these can outperform economic scale.

### Principle 6

> The world is dividing into blocs — but not cleanly, and the middle ground is shrinking.

**What This Means & Why**
The post-Cold War assumption was that the world had outgrown blocs: one global economy, one (mostly) shared internet, one dominant financial architecture. That world has ended. What has replaced it is not a clean bipolar division but a messier fragmentation — a US-inclined bloc, a China-Russia bloc, and a third grouping of countries (India, Vietnam, Brazil, Gulf states, much of Africa) that would prefer not to choose. The problem is that the space for not choosing is being compressed by the logic of both blocs. Friend-shoring, technology decoupling, sanctions enforcement, and standard-setting wars all force choices. The middle ground is not disappearing, but it is getting more expensive to occupy.

**The India Reality**
India sides with the US on security; with China on climate; with neither on Ukraine; with Russia on energy; and with the Global South on trade equity. This is not confusion — it is a deliberate strategy of calibrated non-alignment. But the strategy requires constant management, and its costs are rising as both blocs develop sharper tools for enforcing loyalty.

**The Evidence**
In "Briefing 1: How a fragmented world will impact trade & corporate strategy" (2024), Vivek Y. Kelkar mapped the emerging structure: "There's one bloc that's US-inclined, another led by China and Russia, and a likely third that would ideally like to straddle these two blocs with countries like India, Vietnam and Brazil." He argued that friend-shoring "will not fracture the global system into exclusive trading zones, but it will create barriers in critical industries such as semiconductors, biotechnology, artificial intelligence, green mobility and new forms of energy" — precisely the industries India is trying to build.

**The Implication**
For every new market entry, partnership, or technology platform decision, explicitly map which bloc's infrastructure and standards you are plugging into, and model what happens to that decision if the blocs harden further.

### Principle 7

> China's state-directed capitalism is coherent and effective on its own terms — and it is not converging toward Western norms.

**What This Means & Why**
A generation of Western investors and policymakers bet that China's economic integration into the global system would produce political liberalisation. It did not. What it produced instead was a state that learned to use market mechanisms as instruments of state power — carving out domestic markets for national champions, directing credit to strategic sectors, using consumer data as a national asset, and protecting its digital ecosystem from foreign competition with the same discipline it brings to border security. The bet on convergence was not just wrong — it was costly. And continuing to analyse China through that framework — as if liberalisation remains the destination — produces decisions that are consistently surprised by what China actually does.

**The India Reality**
Chinese smartphones and apps were ubiquitous in India for years, building a playbook for serving Indian consumers and accumulating data on Indian users' health, finance, communication, and movement patterns. India's post-Galwan ban on Chinese apps was a recognition, belated, that the data had strategic value. The question of what was done with that data before the ban is still unanswered.

**The Evidence**
In "The Uber-Didi deal is more about China than about Uber and Didi" (2016), Haresh Chawla dissected how China disposed of Uber's ambitions in China: not through market competition, but through a state-designed architecture in which the government "carves out markets for [entrepreneurs] to play in, provides a cushion of state debt and benign regulations and allows them to build scale rapidly" — then ensures that foreign competitors cannot access those same privileges. "China has demonstrated again and again that it will not allow foreigners to control its vital assets — and what's more vital than its entire digital ecosystem?" This was not protectionism. It was a system.

**The Implication**
When considering any business engagement with China — investment, partnership, market entry, supply chain — assume that the Chinese state has a strategic interest in the outcome of that engagement, and design your terms accordingly.

### Principle 8

> Nuclear risk is the most underappreciated existential threat to business continuity — and it is growing.

**What This Means & Why**
Business continuity planning accounts for pandemics, cyberattacks, regulatory change, currency crises, and supply chain disruption. It does not account for nuclear exchange. Yet the probability of nuclear use — not full strategic exchange, but limited tactical nuclear deployment — has risen materially since 2022. The Doomsday Clock, maintained by the Bulletin of Atomic Scientists, reached 90 seconds to midnight in 2023. The nuclear non-proliferation review conference failed in 2022. The test ban treaty collapsed. These are not abstract signals. They represent the erosion of the institutional architecture that kept nuclear risk contained for seven decades.

**The India Reality**
India sits between two nuclear-armed states with unresolved territorial disputes — Pakistan and China — and has its own nuclear arsenal. The India-Pakistan border has been the site of multiple near-miss escalations. Any scenario in which tactical nuclear weapons are used anywhere in India's extended neighbourhood changes every calculation about investment, supply chain location, and talent concentration in Indian cities.

**The Evidence**
In "Nuclear War: A 100 seconds to midnight" (2022), Sundeep Waslekar argued that "humanity was one miscalculation away from nuclear annihilation" — quoting UN Secretary-General António Guterres — and documented the collapse of arms control infrastructure in granular detail. By "Is the world increasingly becoming a dangerous place to live?" (2023), Waslekar had updated this to note that "the existential threat to human civilization stems from the growing nexus between artificial intelligence, nuclear weapons, and biotechnology." The nightmare scenario is not deliberate nuclear war — it is an AI-assisted miscalculation that escalates faster than human decision-making can interrupt.

**The Implication**
Include low-probability, high-consequence nuclear and AI-enabled escalation scenarios in your board-level risk reviews — not because they are likely, but because their impact on your business would be total and irreversible.

### Principle 9

> Conflicts no longer have endgames — and open-ended instability is now the structural condition, not the exception.

**What This Means & Why**
Every major conflict of the past decade — Ukraine, Gaza, Yemen, Syria, and now Iran — shares a feature that strategic analysis consistently underweights: none of the parties has a credible theory of how the conflict ends. There are military objectives, political demands, economic pressures, and diplomatic manoeuvres — but no pathway to resolution that all parties could accept. This is not a temporary condition awaiting a breakthrough negotiation. It is the new normal. The institutions and norms that previously provided off-ramps — the UN Security Council, arms control treaties, multilateral diplomacy — have either been captured by the parties to the conflict or hollowed out by years of neglect. What replaces them is prolonged ambiguity: frozen conflicts, provisional arrangements, and the permanent management of instability rather than its resolution.

**The India Reality**
India's own border dispute with China has been in a state of prolonged managed ambiguity since Galwan in 2020. The 1993 Border Peace and Tranquillity Agreement accepted formulations on the Line of Actual Control that "did not conclusively reject the Chinese version of the LAC" — a deliberate choice made because the alternative was worse. This is not a failure of diplomacy. It is a feature of a world in which clean resolutions are rarer than managed tensions.

**The Evidence**
In "A World Without Endgames" (2026), Vivek Y. Kelkar argued that "the absence of credible endgames — rather than the presence of active conflict alone — is emerging as the defining feature of the global order." He documented the Iran war as a case study: "there is escalation — but no discernible political, military or economic endgame." The implication was structural: planning for a world in which conflicts resolve is now less useful than planning for a world in which they persist indefinitely at varying temperatures.

**The Implication**
Build your three- to five-year strategic plans around scenarios of persistent instability in key geographies rather than assuming conflicts will resolve — and make resilience to sustained disruption, not recovery from acute shocks, the design principle.

### Principle 10

> India's neighbourhood policy has been neglected in the pursuit of global profile, and the costs are now arriving.

**What This Means & Why**
There is a version of Indian foreign policy ambition that prioritises the G20 presidency, the Global South leadership narrative, the Indo-Pacific strategic architecture, and the bilateral relationship with Washington — while underfunding the political and economic relationships with Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Myanmar, and the Maldives. The assumption is that the neighbourhood is stable enough to manage on autopilot while India plays the bigger game. That assumption has repeatedly proven wrong. When neighbourhood states tip toward China — or toward internal instability — India discovers that its global profile does not compensate for local relationship failure.

**The India Reality**
India's support for Sheikh Hasina in Bangladesh was so unequivocal that it "looked away as she brutally cracked down on the opposition." When Hasina fell, India's position in Dhaka was suddenly precarious. Meanwhile, India had made "inadequate investments in political relationships and economic integration with neighbours in the past decade" — the assessment of former Foreign Secretary Shyam Saran, cited without contradiction.

**The Evidence**
In "Fire in the 'hood" (2024), Dinesh Narayanan documented the pattern in granular detail across Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, the Maldives, and Myanmar. He cited Shyam Saran's judgment that "the neighbourhood has suffered relative neglect with the pursuit of a high global profile" — and showed how, in each case, the vacuum created by Indian neglect was filled by Chinese economic engagement, Pakistani intelligence activity, or internal instability that spilled across borders. The article made the case that India's Neighbourhood First policy had become a slogan without a strategy.

**The Implication**
For businesses with operations, supply chains, or market exposure in South Asia, treat neighbourhood political stability as a strategic input that requires active investment — not a background condition you can assume.

### Principle 11

> The "splinternet" is not coming — it is here, and it is reshaping every digital business model.

**What This Means & Why**
The vision of one global internet — one set of protocols, one (mostly) common layer of platforms — has fractured. What exists now is a set of overlapping but increasingly divergent digital ecosystems: the US ecosystem with its platform giants and GDPR-adjacent regulation; the Chinese ecosystem with its state-designed alternatives to every Western platform; the EU's regulatory perimeter that effectively forces global companies to adopt European standards or exit; and a growing number of countries — India included — building local data localisation requirements, platform regulations, and digital public infrastructure that fragment the space further. This is not a political problem that will be solved by diplomacy. It is a structural feature of the new order.

**The India Reality**
India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, its data localisation requirements for financial data, its UPI architecture as a sovereign payments layer, and its push to build domestic alternatives to foreign cloud infrastructure all represent India's own contribution to the splinternet — not as a victim of fragmentation, but as a participant in it.

**The Evidence**
In "FF Daily #309: Splinternet and the new rules of the game" (2021), Founding Fuel documented that "there is a global tendency towards fragmenting the internet much more than it has been fragmented in the past," citing Daphne Keller of Stanford's Cyber Policy Center. G Venkat Raman, in "The US-China war for tech supremacy is splitting the world in two" (2020), had earlier described the endpoint: "the internet world will be divided into two worlds. Some refer to this as 'one world, two systems.'" By 2021, the "Brussels Effect" article by G Venkat Raman documented that more than 120 countries were taking GDPR as the template for their own technology regulation — a third force in the fragmentation.

**The Implication**
Audit your digital product architecture for its ability to operate under three distinct regulatory and platform regimes simultaneously — US, EU, and China-aligned — because building for one and retrofitting for the others will be more expensive than designing for fragmentation from the start.

### Principle 12

> China's foreign policy is built on constructed narrative and deliberate deception — and the West was complicit in missing this for decades.

**What This Means & Why**
The CPC's foreign policy toolkit includes something that strategic analysis consistently underweights: the systematic construction of false narratives, the cultivation of victim identity to extract concessions, and the deployment of deception as a first-order instrument rather than a last resort. This is not a cultural quirk. It is a documented policy. What makes it effective is that it exploits the good faith of counterparties who assume that diplomatic communication is broadly honest. The West — motivated by greed, willful blindness, and a liberal theory of international relations that assumed economic integration would produce cooperative norms — missed this for decades. India, which had a longer and more direct experience of Chinese policy, was largely ignored when it raised the alarm.

**The India Reality**
Mao told India's Vice President S. Radhakrishnan: "If India and China stood together for twenty years, no one would be able to make us go on different paths." Twenty years became five, when China attacked India in 1962. The gap between declared intent and executed policy in China's India relationship is not an aberration — it is a pattern with a sixty-year history.

**The Evidence**
In "The practice of Chinese foreign policy" (2023), Jabin T. Jacob documented the CPC's "great skill in constructing false narratives, including its playing victim of Western imperialism as a way of extracting concessions." He argued that "the West — the US and the Europeans — for their greed, willful blindness and hypocrisy in policies towards China ignoring India's experiences with China and its warnings until quite recently" had enabled this. The article made the case that deception is "often seen as another fine art in the practice of Chinese foreign policy" — not an edge case but a strategic instrument.

**The Implication**
In any negotiation with Chinese counterparties — commercial, regulatory, or diplomatic — treat the gap between stated intent and structural incentive as a primary variable, and invest in understanding what the Chinese party actually needs rather than relying on what they say they want.

### Principle 13

> India is a Global South aspirant leader — but the gap between aspiration and capacity is real and visible to the countries it wants to lead.

**What This Means & Why**
India's G20 presidency produced genuine diplomatic achievement — the New Delhi Declaration, the Africa Union's inclusion, a consensus built across 120 governments. But the gap between convening power and transformative power is wide. Countries across Africa and the Global South have resentment toward the West and growing scepticism about China. That creates a genuine opening. But filling that opening requires sustained economic engagement, financing capacity, institutional presence, and a model that others want to adopt. India has diplomatic ambition. It does not yet have the instruments to match it.

**The India Reality**
Indian diplomats discovered, during G20 consultations, "huge resentment among developing countries, especially in Africa, against the West" — and simultaneously growing scepticism about China's BRI as debt-laden and poorly designed. The conditions for Indian leadership were present. But "what is not clear is whether India has the wherewithal to take on that mantle."

**The Evidence**
In "Is India too late to the Global South party?" (2023), Pramit Pal Chaudhuri documented both the opportunity and the constraint. India had the diplomatic reach — 120 governments consulted. It had the narrative — "Modi instead talks about a 'new multilateralism.'" But the article found that India "avoids calling itself a Global South leader" precisely because the responsibility that comes with the title requires resources India has not committed. The contrast with China's BRI — whatever its failures — is a contrast in ambition of execution, not just ambition of rhetoric.

**The Implication**
If your business is positioning in African or Southeast Asian markets on the back of India's diplomatic profile, verify that the infrastructure, financing, and institutional relationships India is claiming actually exist on the ground before building your market entry strategy around them.

### Principle 14

> AI is not a technology race — it is a sovereignty race, and most Indian institutions have not entered it.

**What This Means & Why**
The AI contest between the US and China is being fought at multiple levels simultaneously: compute infrastructure, training data, scientific talent, standard-setting bodies, and regulatory frameworks. India participates at some of these levels and is absent from others. The level that matters most — frontier model development, scientific AI, the competition for control over the next generation of discovery — is one India is largely watching from the sidelines. The reason is not capability alone. It is governance: India has not built the regulatory frameworks that would unlock access to frontier compute, attract frontier talent, or position itself as a trusted partner in the AI infrastructure stack.

**The India Reality**
The US restricts GPU exports to India because India has not established a Frontier AI Safety Framework. The UAE created one and got its GPU allocation. India has not. Meanwhile, India's AI policy is "made by MeitY and Niti Aayog" with uncertain involvement from the National Security Council — a governance structure that treats AI as a technology ministry problem rather than a national security priority.

**The Evidence**
In "India's AI Gambit: Exploring Our Strategic Options" (2025), Sundeep Waslekar documented that India "has built a strong, consensus-driven foundation in AI policy — but remains on the sidelines of the global AI arena, a spectator rather than a key player." The article, and the subsequent masterclass "India's AI Gambit" (2025), recorded former diplomat Pankaj Saran's judgment: "AI is the new battleground of global dominance... Like the space race or nuclear era, AI is driving a new binary contest — between the US and China." India's position: observing the contest and leading on inclusion, access, and data protection — all necessary, none sufficient for the actual race.

**The Implication**
If your organisation is building AI capabilities, explicitly assess whether you are building toward the frontier of the actual race — scientific AI, compute sovereignty, proprietary training data — or building on someone else's infrastructure in ways that will require renegotiation as the geopolitical constraints tighten.

### Principle 15

> The US is strategically incoherent — and that incoherence is now a structural feature, not a temporary condition.

**What This Means & Why**
American strategic incoherence takes a specific form: the US wants outcomes — a free and open Indo-Pacific, supply chain resilience, allied solidarity, technology leadership — without committing the instruments required to achieve them. It walked away from the Trans-Pacific Partnership. It has repeatedly backslid on the Indo-Pacific Economic Framework. It applies tariffs without a coherent theory of what they are supposed to achieve. It demands allies choose sides and then destabilises those same allies with unpredictable trade policy. This is not incompetence in execution — it is a genuine structural tension between American ambitions and American domestic politics. The result is that partners and competitors are both planning around US unreliability, not US leadership.

**The India Reality**
India has calibrated its relationship with the US with enormous care — the Quad, defence procurement, technology partnerships, intelligence sharing. But every one of these commitments was made in a context where US strategic consistency could be assumed. That assumption has degraded. India's response — maintaining the Russia relationship, hedging on trade deals, moving slowly on full alignment — reflects a rational read of American reliability.

**The Evidence**
In "Briefing 3: The US - Europe - Russia - China - India dynamic" (2024), Vivek Y. Kelkar was direct: "The US remains unwilling to commit to trade policy as a broad foreign policy tool. The backsliding over IPEF, and the reluctance to enter pacts like the TPP, are stark pointers." By 2025, in "Trump's Tariff Gambit and the Global Trade Dilemma," Kelkar was noting that Trump's tariff regime "raises fundamental questions about whether the US has a cohesive geopolitical strategy... The signals are mixed, the strategy murky." Justin Logan of the Cato Institute supplied the sharpest summary: "The tariff policy had a ready-fire-aim quality to it."

**The Implication**
Do not build your export or partnership strategy around the assumption of sustained US policy consistency — build it to be resilient to the full range of US policy outcomes, from deep engagement to transactional disruption.

### Principle 16

> Historical aberrations tend to revert — and the past two centuries of Western dominance may be one of them.

**What This Means & Why**
The mental model that treats Western — and specifically Anglo-American — dominance as the natural state of global order is a historical artefact of approximately 200 years' duration. For the 1,800 years before that, the world's two largest economies were China and India. The current rebalancing of global economic and political weight toward Asia is not a disruption of the natural order — it is a reversion toward it. This does not make the transition smooth, predictable, or free of violent contest. But it changes the frame for thinking about where the world is going: not toward a restored Western hegemony, not toward Chinese dominance, but toward a multipolar order more similar to what existed for most of recorded history than to anything the post-war institutions were designed for.

**The India Reality**
India's civilisational self-understanding — as an ancient civilisation temporarily eclipsed by colonial interruption — is not just cultural pride. It is a strategic frame that shapes how Indian diplomats and policymakers think about the long game. The current moment is not India catching up to a Western standard. It is India re-entering a global order in which Asian civilisations are the default, not the exception.

**The Evidence**
In "The West in an era of Asian dominance" (2018), CKGSB Knowledge featured Kishore Mahbubani's argument that "for 1,800 of the past 2,000 years, the world's two largest economies have always been China and India. The last 200 years have been a major historical aberration." Mahbubani also offered his sharpest critique: "Fukuyama's essay did a lot of brain damage to the West. He put the West to sleep precisely at the moment when China and India were waking up." The article treated the Asian century not as a prediction but as an ongoing reversion that had already begun.

**The Implication**
When building 10-year strategic plans, weight scenarios of genuine multipolarity — in which no single power dominates and India operates as a major autonomous actor — at least as heavily as scenarios of continued American hegemony or Chinese ascendance.

### Principle 17

> Trade deals are not transactions — they are sovereignty decisions, and the costs often sit in the fine print.

**What This Means & Why**
The standard frame for evaluating a trade deal is market access: what tariffs drop, what sectors open, what exports grow. This frame misses the structural dimension. Trade deals embed regulatory standards, intellectual property frameworks, data governance norms, and investment protection clauses that constrain future policy choices. A country that signs a trade deal with a more powerful partner in a moment of pressure is not just opening its markets — it is accepting a regulatory architecture designed by and for the more powerful party. The sovereignty cost is real, diffuse, and accumulates over time. It rarely appears in the headline announcement.

**The India Reality**
India's 20-year tax holiday for foreign data-centre operators — part of its trade accommodation with the US — illustrates the pattern. "The platforms, intellectual property, and profits will largely remain offshore. The costs — energy demand, water stress, and pressure on local grids — will be borne domestically." India traded future regulatory space for present-tense trade peace.

**The Evidence**
In "India's Trade Deals and the New Meaning of Strategic Autonomy" (2026), Vivek Y. Kelkar argued that "India did not so much choose this negotiation as accept that refusing accommodation would have carried higher geo-economic costs." The article documented, line by line, how the concessions India made — on data localisation, on digital trade norms, on data-centre taxation — were not the result of Indian strategic preference but of structural pressure from a more powerful partner. "Autonomy today is less about refusal and more about calibration. India has bought time — but at the cost of signalling a willingness to align with Washington's priorities."

**The Implication**
When evaluating any bilateral trade or investment agreement that your sector will operate under, commission a second-order analysis of what regulatory and policy flexibility the agreement forecloses — not just what market access it opens.

### Principle 18

> Geopolitical knowledge inside most Indian businesses is two decades out of date — and the cost of that lag is accelerating.

**What This Means & Why**
Most Indian businesses, even large ones, have no internal capacity for geopolitical analysis. They rely on newspaper headlines, diplomatic briefings, and the assumptions embedded in their founders' worldview — which was typically formed in the 1990s or early 2000s, when globalisation was expanding, China was a factory not a rival, the US was unquestionably dominant, and the neighbourhood was relatively stable. That worldview is now systematically misleading. The businesses that are navigating the current order well are the ones that have built genuine analytical capacity — people who understand how the US-China rivalry works, what bloc alignment means for their supply chains, how AI governance decisions will constrain their technology choices, and what the fragmentation of the global financial system means for cross-border capital flows.

**The India Reality**
India's business press covers geopolitics as a subset of politics — as background noise that occasionally erupts into relevance. Founding Fuel's consistent argument is that geopolitics is now the foreground condition of every business decision. The gap between what Indian practitioners know and what they need to know is not academic. It is operational.

**The Evidence**
In "Masterclass: Geopolitics and Global Trade - How It Impacts India" (2024), Founding Fuel opened with the observation that "nearly everything, especially economic flows, have been securitised — flows of goods, capital, people, data and especially technology — are being refracted by the political class, the strategic class and regulatory class in Washington through the prism of national security rather than public good." The masterclass was designed precisely because the audience — senior Indian practitioners — did not have this framework. The demand for it confirmed that the knowledge gap was real and large.

**The Implication**
Hire or contract at least one person with a genuine international relations or geopolitical analysis background — not a current affairs reader, but someone who can model how structural power dynamics will evolve over a five-year horizon — and integrate their analysis into your strategy process, not your risk register.

## FF's View

**The Core Conviction**

FF's single most important argument on geopolitics and international relations is this: the separation between business strategy and geopolitical strategy is over, and Indian practitioners who have not yet internalized this are operating on a map that no longer matches the territory.

This is not a claim about turbulence or uncertainty, the ambient vocabulary of corporate risk departments. It is a structural claim. The global order that Indian businesses were built to navigate — one in which trade flows followed comparative advantage, in which technology crossed borders freely, in which a company could source from China, sell to the US, and operate from India without those three facts creating political problems — has been dismantled. Not disrupted. Dismantled.

What replaced it is a world where, as G Venkat Raman argues in "The US-China war for tech supremacy is splitting the world in two" (2020), "weaponized interdependence" is the operating principle. Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman's concept — that the distribution of power rests on control over critical nodes: financial communications, supply chains, and the internet — has moved from political science seminar to lived business reality faster than most Indian executives have registered. In "Masterclass: Geopolitics and Global Trade - How It Impacts India" (2024), Vivek Y. Kelkar states it with surgical precision: nearly everything that used to be an economic flow — goods, capital, people, data, technology — has been "securitised," filtered through a national security prism in Washington, Beijing, Brussels, and increasingly Delhi.

The implication FF draws, and draws consistently, is uncomfortable: neutrality is no longer a passive option. India's famous "strategic autonomy" — the doctrine of not choosing sides — was once an act of refusal. It is increasingly, as Kelkar argues in "India's Trade Deals and the New Meaning of Strategic Autonomy" (2026), "less about refusal and more about calibration." You can still play both sides, but only if you are doing it actively, with intelligence, with traded concessions, and with a clear-eyed understanding that both sides know you are doing it. The old non-alignment — dignified abstention — is gone. What remains is managed exposure, and managing it requires understanding the game at a level most Indian business education has never taught.

That is what this page is for.

**What India Means for This Theme**

India's relationship to geopolitics is structurally unlike any other large economy's. It is not that India faces more pressure than others — it is that India faces pressure from every direction simultaneously, with no clean alliances that resolve the tension.

Consider the geography. India shares a land border with China, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, and Myanmar. Three of those relationships are live strategic problems. Its extended neighbourhood includes Afghanistan (a base state for terror, as FF argued in "FF Daily #425: The Afghan front" in 2021), Sri Lanka, and the Maldives — all of which have oscillated between Indian and Chinese influence within the last decade alone. In "Fire in the 'hood'" (2024), Dinesh Narayanan documents the collapse of India's Neighbourhood First policy in practice, quoting former Foreign Secretary Shyam Saran: "The neighbourhood has suffered relative neglect with the pursuit of a high global profile." India looked away as Sheikh Hasina cracked down on opposition in Bangladesh because the bilateral relationship was too convenient to disturb. The result was a neighbourhood that increasingly resented Indian hegemony and found Chinese infrastructure money a useful lever.

The Indian Ocean is a second dimension. Kanti Prasad Bajpai, in "Is the US in strategic retreat in the Indian Ocean region?" (2023), makes the case that the US is not, in fact, retreating — but that the relevant dynamic is US-India complementarity, not US unilateralism. American and Indian naval and air power, working together, would be devastating to Chinese positions at Djibouti, Gwadar, and Hambantota. India's geography is its primary strategic asset in the Indo-Pacific equation. But the same geography that makes India valuable to Washington is also the geography that makes it exposed: any disruption in the Persian Gulf ripples directly into Indian energy costs, remittance flows, and diaspora welfare.

The Gulf is not an abstraction for India. As Anindya Dutta writes in "A War India Didn't Choose—and a Market It Can't Escape" (2026), the Gulf Cooperation Council is India's largest trading partner bloc at $178.56 billion in FY 2024-25. More than 80% of India's crude needs are imported, and a large fraction flows through the Strait of Hormuz. Each $10 rise in crude shaves roughly half a percentage point off GDP growth. When Vivek Y. Kelkar writes in "The US–Israel War on Iran: The Risks to India's Strategic Hedging" (2026) that "the very region that enables India's strategic autonomy is also the one most capable of constraining it when instability erupts," this is not rhetorical symmetry. It is the structural condition India navigates every day.

There is a Bharat dimension here that English-language geopolitical commentary almost never engages. The millions of Indian workers in the Gulf — blue-collar, semi-skilled, absent from corporate India's strategic imagination — are themselves a geopolitical factor. Their remittances underpin household economies across Kerala, UP, Bihar, and Rajasthan. Their safety and return in any Gulf crisis is a domestic political problem as much as a foreign policy one. Indian strategic autonomy is not just about what the foreign minister says at the UN General Assembly. It is about whether the government can absorb a sudden return of three million workers from a war-disrupted Gulf without a social and economic implosion. No framework imported from Western strategic studies has ever been built with this constraint in mind.

India's technology vulnerability adds a third layer. In "Algorithmic War is upon us" (2019), FF documented how Chinese smartphones and apps became ubiquitous in India, giving Chinese AI systems access to Indian health, financial, and behavioral data at scale. By the time this was recognized as a problem and app bans were imposed in 2020, years of data accumulation had already occurred. In "India's AI Gambit" (2025), Purushottam argues that "we have allowed our complete data ecosystem to be pillaged by Big Tech" — pointing to DigiLocker hosted on foreign cloud and Indian logistics running on Google infrastructure. This is not a complaint about corporate practice. It is a description of a national security vulnerability that accumulated during the period when Indian policymakers treated geopolitics as someone else's department.

The India-China border adds the sharpest and most immediate dimension. The 1993 Border Peace and Tranquillity Agreement, as "India's negotiations with China: The boundary question" (2022) reveals, accepted a formulation on the LAC that "did not conclusively reject the Chinese version" because the alternative was continued confrontation at a moment when India was also fighting Pakistan-sponsored terrorism. The LAC itself, as FF notes, was "a Chinese creation." India negotiated under pressure in 1993, got locked into ambiguity, and has lived with that ambiguity ever since — including in the Galwan confrontation of 2020. As "FF Insights #648" (2022) recalls, Mao told Radhakrishnan that if India and China stood together for twenty years, "no one would be able to make us go on different paths." Twenty years became five, when China attacked India in 1962. The shadow of 1962 is not nostalgia — it is the reason India's China policy will always be constrained by institutional memory of miscalculation.

And then there is the Global South aspiration. India spent enormous diplomatic capital consulting over 120 governments before the G20 New Delhi summit, discovering, as Pramit Pal Chaudhuri writes in "Is India too late to the Global South party?" (2023), huge resentment among developing nations against both the West and, increasingly, China. India avoids the phrase "Global South leader" — Modi speaks of "new multilateralism" — precisely because claiming the mantle raises the question of whether India has the institutional wherewithal to deliver on it. The aspiration is real. The gap between aspiration and capacity is equally real. FF names both, without flinching from either.

**Where Global Best Practices Break Down**

The dominant frameworks for geopolitics and business strategy — as developed by McKinsey, Harvard's geopolitical intelligence units, the European corporate-diplomatic traditions, the World Economic Forum's stakeholder capitalism model — all share a founding assumption: that the rules of the international order are sufficiently stable that companies can treat them as background conditions. Geopolitics, in this framing, is weather. You adjust for it but you don't restructure around it.

This assumption has been wrong for a decade. FF's coverage has documented its unraveling across every dimension of business life.

The most important imported framework that breaks down for India is the idea that supply chain resilience can be achieved through diversification within existing trade architectures. The McKinsey prescription — reduce China concentration, build in redundancy, map your supply chain to the nth tier — is sensible as far as it goes. What it does not account for is that the alternative production locations (Vietnam, Mexico, Bangladesh, India itself) are not neutral territory. As Vivek Y. Kelkar documents in "Briefing 2: India and the Indo-Pacific" (2024), Vietnam and Mexico function as "connector economies" — supply chain pass-throughs for Chinese intermediate goods that are then finished and exported as non-Chinese. The US has "seen through the game" but has not consistently acted to close the arbitrage. This means that diversification strategies built on Vietnamese assembly or Mexican final-mile production may be buying time, not buying safety.

For India specifically, the standard prescription is worse. India lacks significant trade and competitive advantages vis-à-vis ASEAN, as Kelkar states directly in "Briefing 2" (2024). This is not a policy problem with a straightforward fix. It is the accumulated consequence of decades of industrial policy that prioritized domestic protection over export competitiveness. The McKinsey recommendation to "build manufacturing capability and integrate into global supply chains" is correct as a destination. It does not address the decades of institutional change required to get there, or the fact that the window for relatively frictionless integration is closing as the blocs harden.

The second major framework failure is the assumption that multilateral institutions can be relied upon to manage disputes. The WTO's dispute resolution mechanism is effectively non-functional. The UN Security Council is blocked on every significant conflict by great-power veto. As Sundeep Waslekar documents in "Is the world increasingly becoming a dangerous place to live?" (2023), the nuclear non-proliferation review conference failed in 2022 and the test ban treaty collapsed in the same year. Shyam Saran, quoted in "Masterclass: The World in 2025 - Part 2" (2025), frames this with maximal clarity: the international order is breaking down "because the very architects of that order are taking a wrecking ball to it." When the US — the primary architect of the post-1945 order — decides that the order no longer serves US interests, no amount of faith in multilateralism can substitute for that anchor.

The third imported framework failure is the EU's regulatory approach being treated as a scalable global model. The "Brussels Effect" — described in "State Vs Big Tech: The Brussels Effect" (2021) as a condition where more than 120 countries draw inspiration from GDPR — is real as a phenomenon. But it breaks down when applied to the US-China tech contest. The EU can set standards that American and Chinese companies comply with because they want access to the European market. But the EU cannot resolve the fundamental strategic question of which technology stack — American or Chinese — becomes the global default. For India, which has to make decisions about 5G infrastructure, AI governance, and semiconductor sourcing that will lock in dependencies for decades, the EU regulatory model is a useful reference for consumer protection but an inadequate framework for strategic technology choices.

The fourth failure is HBR's treatment of purpose-driven business as a geopolitical softener. The idea that companies can build political goodwill through ESG, corporate social responsibility, and stakeholder commitments breaks down completely in a securitized environment. Disney's "Authentically Disney, Distinctly Chinese" localization strategy at Shanghai — cited approvingly in "Shanghai Disney's Chinese track and fighting obesity with taxes" (2016) as a model for MNC adaptation — looks very different after 2020. The question is no longer whether your content is locally adapted. The question is whether your data flows, your ownership structure, and your supply chain geography can survive geopolitical pressure from either side. Purpose-washing does not answer that question.

**Against Management Orthodoxy**

The received wisdom of business schools and consulting firms on geopolitics runs roughly as follows: identify your political risk exposures, diversify your concentration, build government relations capacity in key markets, monitor regulatory environments, and factor country risk into your cost of capital. Engage a geopolitical intelligence provider. Attend Davos. This is essentially risk management dressed as strategy, and it assumes the rules of engagement are stable enough to manage against.

FF's evidence base contradicts this orthodoxy at every level.

First, the orthodoxy says: diversify your supply chain to manage geopolitical risk. FF's evidence says: the places you diversify to may themselves be geopolitically compromised. In "Briefing 1: How a fragmented world will impact trade & corporate strategy" (2024), Kelkar documents how connector economies — Vietnam, Mexico — have absorbed Chinese production without actually reducing Chinese economic and technological influence in those supply chains. The diversification is often cosmetic. The underlying exposure persists in the form of Chinese intermediate goods, Chinese technology components, and Chinese capital.

Second, the orthodoxy says: engage with the multilateral trading system. FF's evidence says: that system has ceased to function as designed. Kelkar's "Trade Is the New Geopolitics—and India Must Learn to Play It" (2026) makes the point that "trade and economics now turn on caprice; new trade orders are inevitable." The WTO was built to adjudicate disputes within an agreed rule-set. When the primary power no longer believes the rules serve its interests, the dispute resolution machinery becomes irrelevant. Companies that have built supply chains, pricing models, and market access strategies on WTO stability are running on a faulty assumption.

Third, the orthodoxy says: identify where geopolitical risk is highest and reduce exposure there. FF's evidence says: the locus of risk has shifted to technology architecture, and most companies have not updated their risk maps. G Venkat Raman's "The New Perimeter of National Security" (2025) documents how digital platforms are no longer just commercial infrastructure — they are strategic infrastructure with national security implications. Indian fintech companies discovered this in regulatory encounters that had nothing to do with their business models and everything to do with data flows and platform dependency. The risk is not in a geography. It is in a technology stack.

Fourth, the orthodoxy says: build government relations. FF's evidence says: the relevant relationship is not just with your own government but with the technology and trade governance processes happening in Washington, Brussels, and Beijing simultaneously. As Venkat Raman documents in "US-China Tech war: The US is weaponizing its global influence" (2022), the US "foreign product rule" — extending export controls extraterritorially — affects non-American and non-Chinese firms as much as Chinese ones. An Indian semiconductor company, or an Indian firm that sources components anywhere in the global supply chain, is affected by US export control decisions it had no voice in making and may not even be aware of.

Fifth — and most subversively — the orthodoxy says: there is a best practice that applies globally, and your job is to learn and implement it. FF's consistent position is that India's conditions are structurally different enough that global best practices are often misleading guides. The China model, the EU model, the Silicon Valley model — each requires translation into Indian conditions that is more radical than "adaptation." Sometimes it requires rejection.

**The Entrepreneurial Mindset Beyond Startups**

The entrepreneurial lens — the willingness to act under uncertainty, to treat the environment as something to be shaped rather than merely accommodated, to build options rather than optimize for a single scenario — is as relevant to a policymaker navigating India's AI governance choices as it is to a startup founder choosing a technology stack.

FF's coverage of geopolitics is full of moments where the absence of an entrepreneurial mindset at the institutional level produced strategic paralysis. India's response to the Taliban's return to power in Afghanistan is the starkest example. While Russia, China, and Iran had already opened diplomatic channels with the Taliban — treating the new reality as a constraint to be worked with — India "declined as a matter of principle," as "FF Daily #425: The Afghan front" (2021) documents. The principled position is understandable. The strategic cost was real: India lost ground in a country where it had invested significantly in development projects, and left itself with no working channel when it needed one most. An entrepreneurial approach would have recognized that engaging the Taliban did not require endorsing them, and that being present in the room is better than being morally correct and absent.

The same logic applies to India's AI governance choices. Waslekar's analysis in "India's AI Gambit: Exploring Our Strategic Options" (2025) documents that India has built a "strong, consensus-driven foundation in AI policy" — but remains a spectator in the actual arena where AI standards are being set. The problem, as Waslekar notes, is partly structural: AI policy is handled by MeitY and Niti Aayog, without clear National Security Council involvement. This is an institutional design problem that an entrepreneurial leader — in any ministry, any corporate, any think tank with access to decision-makers — could begin to address by simply creating the conversations across those silos.

The connector economy framework applies entrepreneurially to Indian business. India lacks significant trade advantages vis-à-vis ASEAN, as Kelkar documents. But the fragmentation of global supply chains creates opportunities for India that did not exist in a world of smooth comparative advantage. The entrepreneurial question is not "where is India's natural advantage?" — the analyst's question — but "what can India do that no connector economy currently does, that both sides of the US-China divide would value?" That question requires the same customer development instinct, the same willingness to experiment with positioning, that distinguishes a successful startup from a well-funded failure.

For large organizations — Tata, Mahindra, Reliance, HDFC — the entrepreneurial lens on geopolitics means treating geopolitical position as a competitive asset to be built, not just managed. Haresh Chawla's analysis in "The Uber-Didi deal is more about China than about Uber and Didi" (2016) shows how Chinese state capitalism explicitly creates market structures that reward its champions. India's large conglomerates have the scale to engage in analogous positioning — not through state direction, but through deliberate cultivation of strategic roles in supply chains, technology partnerships, and market access that give them geopolitical leverage. This requires thinking ten years out, not two quarters.

**On Transformation: Process, Not Just Destination**

The standard account of geopolitical transformation is event-driven: a war starts, a sanction is imposed, a summit produces a communiqué, and the world reorganizes. FF's reporting reveals a very different mechanism — slow, structural, often invisible until suddenly legible.

China's innovation transformation is the clearest case. The arc from copycat economy to genuine technological competitor did not happen through any single policy decision or technological breakthrough. As Rishikesha T Krishnan documents in "The Chinese innovation machine" (2016), it was the cumulative consequence of sustained R&D investment — $162 billion at 2% of GDP, with 70% coming from business rather than government — compounded over decades. By 2020, when Venkat Raman wrote "The US-China war for tech supremacy is splitting the world in two," the transformation was complete enough that he could state without qualification: "Beijing's technological capabilities are no more based on the imitation of Western technological products." The endpoint was sudden in its clarity. The process was thirty years in the making.

The same structural process logic applies to global order fragmentation. The "Splinternet" — the fragmentation of the internet into geopolitical blocs — did not begin with any single decision. It began with China's Great Firewall, extended through Russia's sovereign internet experiments, was accelerated by US export controls on Huawei, and deepened through GDPR and India's data localization requirements. By the time FF was writing "FF Daily #309: Splinternet and the new rules of the game" (2021), Daphne Keller of Stanford could observe a "global tendency towards fragmenting the internet" as an empirical reality, not a hypothesis. Practitioners who waited for the decisive moment missed it. The decisive moment had been accumulating for years.

The mechanism FF's reporting consistently reveals is: structural conditions change slowly, then suddenly become visible in a crisis that looks like a surprise but was not. The Galwan confrontation of 2020 — Indian and Chinese soldiers in hand-to-hand combat on the LAC — looked like a sudden escalation. It was the consequence of thirty years of ambiguity in the 1993 BPTA, as "India's negotiations with China: The boundary question" (2022) documents. The boundary agreement that India accepted under pressure in 1993 because the alternative was unacceptable contained within it the conditions for every subsequent confrontation. The crisis was structural, not accidental.

For Indian practitioners, the transformation lesson is: the changes that will matter most in your industry, your market, or your regulatory environment over the next decade are already underway. The securitization of data is not a future event — Indian companies are already subject to extraterritorial US export controls and GDPR-inspired data regulations. The fragmentation of the technology stack is not a hypothetical — choices being made now about cloud providers, AI vendors, and semiconductor suppliers are locking in dependencies that will be extremely expensive to reverse. The window for strategic optionality is not closed, but it is narrowing.

**The Human and Cultural Dimension**

Geopolitics is often discussed as if it were a contest between abstract entities — states, blocs, alliances. FF's coverage consistently retrieves the human and cultural dimension that this abstraction erases.

The Obama-Manmohan Singh conversation, quoted in "FF Daily #233: When Barack Obama met Manmohan Singh" (2020), is one of the most striking moments in FF's geopolitics coverage precisely because it is human rather than strategic. Manmohan Singh told Obama: "In uncertain times, Mr. President, the call of religious and ethnic solidarity can be intoxicating. And it's not so hard for politicians to exploit that, in India or anywhere else." This is not a line from a strategic doctrine. It is a practitioner's honest acknowledgment of the emotional substrate of politics — the fact that identity mobilization is not a bug in democratic systems but a feature that any leader must contend with.

Charles Assisi's "India versus Pakistan is not cricket, but war" (2017) retrieves the same human dimension in a different register. The image of MS Dhoni holding Sarfraz Ahmed's baby — offered as evidence that the ordinary human reality of India-Pakistan relations is disconnected from its political framing — is a deliberate counter to the strategist's tendency to treat bilateral relationships as abstractions. The colonial legacy that "sowed the seeds of discord among the brown natives" is not merely historical context. It is a lived emotional inheritance that shapes how Pakistani and Indian publics respond to political signals in ways that no rational choice model can capture.

Rishikesha Krishnan's "The dragon mellows" (2015) offers a different kind of human observation: the moment when Chinese academics who had previously ignored India began seeking meetings with Indian business school faculty once India's GDP growth rate overtook China's. This is not grand strategy. It is the human reality of status and recognition — the way national trajectories reshape interpersonal dynamics in institutions that are nominally about knowledge rather than power. It tells you something about Chinese elite psychology that no official document reveals: that status competition operates at every level, and that India's rise is registered in China through these small, telling social adjustments.

Sundeep Waslekar's nuclear risk writing operates in a register that most strategic analysis refuses: the register of horror. "Those who die instantly will be fortunate. The rest of us will suffer from radiation, environmental impact, blackout of sunrays, disease of flora and fauna, and a slow and torturous death," he writes in "Nuclear War: A 100 seconds to midnight" (2022). This is not a strategic assessment. It is a moral witness to what the strategic contest, if it miscarries, actually means. FF publishes it because the human stakes of geopolitical failure need to be felt, not just calculated.

The Afghan diaspora in India, the Gulf workers from UP and Kerala, the Indian students in Chinese universities, the Indian businesspeople who built relationships with Chinese counterparts before 2020 and then watched them become politically toxic — these human figures populate FF's geopolitical coverage and give it a texture that pure strategic analysis lacks. When FF argues that India should engage China with "pragmatism rather than pride or fear," as Venkat Raman writes in "The US-China war for tech supremacy is splitting the world in two" (2020), it is partly because pride and fear are themselves human phenomena — emotional states that degrade strategic judgment.

**The Systems View**

The systems view that emerges from FF's cumulative geopolitics coverage is one of interlocking vulnerabilities — where a disruption in one node propagates through multiple systems in ways that no single analyst, government, or company has modeled adequately.

The semiconductor supply chain is the clearest example. Shishir Prasad writes in "The Tariff Trap: Why Economic Weapons Misfire in the Age of Silicon" (2025) about the "quantum entanglement of global manufacturing" — the impossibility of cleanly decoupling semiconductor production when every chip contains components, processes, and intellectual property from multiple jurisdictions. US export controls on advanced chips to China are designed to impede Chinese AI development. But they also affect American chip companies' revenues, which affects their R&D capacity, which affects the long-term US technological position they were designed to defend. The instrument undermines the goal it is supposed to serve.

The Iran scenario, analyzed in depth in "The US–Israel War on Iran: Energy, Power and Regional Disruption" (2026) and "How Iran Is Turning the Global Economy Into a Battlefield" (2026), reveals a second-order effects problem of comparable magnitude. A conflict involving Iran does not just affect Iranian oil exports. It activates the Houthi Red Sea axis simultaneously with Hormuz pressure — Kelkar's "dual chokehold" — affecting global energy flows, insurance rates, shipping routes, and therefore every sector of the Indian economy that depends on imported inputs or export shipping. It threatens India's IMEC and INSTC connectivity projects. It strains India's "de-hyphenation" approach — the effort to maintain discrete relationships with Israel, Arab Gulf states, and Iran simultaneously — by forcing a visible choice.

The AI-nuclear nexus is the most alarming system-level interaction in FF's coverage. Waslekar identifies in "Is the world increasingly becoming a dangerous place to live?" (2023) "the growing nexus between artificial intelligence, nuclear weapons, and biotechnology" as the existential threat to human civilization. This is not three separate risks. It is one compounded risk: AI applied to nuclear command and control reduces human decision time in ways that make accidental escalation more likely; AI applied to biotechnology enables the development of biological agents that previously required state-level resources; and these risks interact in scenarios that the arms control frameworks of the Cold War were never designed to address.

The Belt and Road Initiative's failure mode illustrates a different systems dynamic. In "Is Africa swapping out Europe for China?" (2023), Kelkar documents how BRI projects left recipient nations with "extensive and out-of-control debt," partly because "projects were often uncoordinated" and "economic gains to the recipient nations were opaquely defined." China's strategic infrastructure play created dependency, but the dependency has generated resentment that now threatens to erode the political relationships that justified the investment. The Chinese state-capitalism model is effective at building infrastructure at speed. It is less effective at building the political legitimacy that makes that infrastructure sustainable. This is a systems failure: the instrument achieves its first-order goal (infrastructure presence) while undermining its second-order goal (stable political influence).

For India, the systems view reveals a specific vulnerability cluster: energy dependency concentrated in the Gulf, technology dependency concentrated in US and Chinese platforms, manufacturing dependency on Chinese intermediate goods, and border security requiring enormous continuous defense expenditure — all operating simultaneously. The cost of maintaining a brigade on the Siachen glacier, as Sandeep Unnithan's reporting cited in "WFH Daily #189: The Indian Army in Ladakh" (2020) documents, is Rs 5 crore per day. This is not a strategic abstraction. It is a resource drain that directly competes with every rupee that could be invested in education, health, or technology capability — the investments that would reduce these vulnerabilities over time. The systems view reveals a trap: the vulnerabilities require expensive maintenance that prevents building the capacity to reduce the vulnerabilities.

**The Evolution of FF's Thinking**

The earliest Founding Fuel geopolitics coverage — roughly 2015 to 2017 — is primarily analytical and comparatist. The China innovation pieces by Krishnan and the CKGSB Knowledge contributions are rigorous assessments of a specific question: is China genuinely innovating, or is it still fundamentally a copycat economy? The answer FF develops over this period is unambiguous: the innovation is real, the scale is extraordinary, and Western dismissal is strategically dangerous. But the frame is economic and technological. Geopolitics appears as context, not as the central concern.

By 2019, with N S Ramnath's "Technology is politics," the frame has shifted. The claim is no longer "China is innovating at scale" but "technology choices are political choices, and treating them as purely commercial is a category error." This is a significant conceptual upgrade. The earlier coverage documented a phenomenon. The 2019 framing provides a framework for understanding what the phenomenon means — and what it requires of practitioners.

The 2020-2021 period — the pandemic years, the Galwan confrontation, the app bans, the beginning of the US-China tech decoupling — accelerates this shift dramatically. Waslekar joins as a regular voice; his Doomsday Clock framing and his "world dividing into two camps" analysis give FF's geopolitics coverage an urgency and a systems-level perspective it had previously lacked. The quarterly world-events summaries that begin in 2021 institutionalize geopolitics as a recurring editorial priority, not an occasional feature.

What FF believed in 2016 that it no longer holds: the Uber-Didi analysis by Haresh Chawla in 2016 treated China's state-directed capitalism as primarily a domestic story about market design and entrepreneurial energy. By 2022-2024, the same dynamics are analyzed explicitly as instruments of geopolitical power — the state creates market structures not just to support domestic champions but to exclude foreign competitors from data flows and platform infrastructure. The analysis has not reversed; it has deepened to reveal the strategic logic underlying what earlier looked like economic pragmatism.

What has sharpened: FF's skepticism of India's strategic posture has become more specific and more pointed. In 2020, the argument was "India should engage the world confidently rather than protectionist." By 2024-2026, the argument is more granular: India's Neighbourhood First policy has failed in practice, India's AI governance has left it a spectator rather than a player, India's data ecosystem has been compromised by foreign platforms, and India's strategic autonomy doctrine is being tested by structural dependencies that rhetoric cannot resolve. The earlier framing was normative — India should do better. The later framing is diagnostic — here is specifically what has gone wrong and why.

What is genuinely new in the 2024-2026 coverage is the trade-as-geopolitics frame. The realization that tariff regimes, free trade agreements, and supply chain architecture are now the primary instruments of geopolitical power — not just adjuncts to it — represents a conceptual evolution that positions FF to address practitioners with more operational specificity. Kelkar's "Trade Is the New Geopolitics—and India Must Learn to Play It" (2026) and "Tariffs Are the Noise. Realignment Is the Signal" (2026) are direct attempts to make this operational for Indian business.

**Key Voices**

**Sundeep Waslekar** (Strategic Foresight Group) is FF's most sustained and distinctive geopolitical voice. What he brings that others don't is the long-run existential frame — nuclear risk, AI-bio-nuclear nexus, the comparison of the 2020s to the 1920s — combined with a practitioner's knowledge of mediation and multilateral process (he has direct experience with conflict mediation across multiple regions). He is at his sharpest in "Nuclear War: A 100 seconds to midnight" (2022), where he brings the horror of nuclear escalation into visceral proximity, and in "India's AI Gambit: Exploring Our Strategic Options" (2025), where his critique of India's governance gap on AI is both technically grounded and institutionally specific.

**Vivek Y. Kelkar** has become FF's primary voice on trade, economic architecture, and Indian strategic positioning from 2024 onward. His distinctive angle is structural economics applied to geopolitics — he tracks trade flows, supply chain dependencies, and institutional architecture with the granularity of an economist and the strategic imagination of a security analyst. He is at his sharpest in "Briefing 1: How a fragmented world will impact trade & corporate strategy" (2024) and "Trade Is the New Geopolitics—and India Must Learn to Play It" (2026), where his three-bloc framework gives practitioners an organizing structure that is both analytically rigorous and operationally useful.

**G Venkat Raman** has been FF's most consistent voice on the US-China technology contest. His signal contribution is taking the academic framework of "weaponized interdependence" and making it readable and applicable for business practitioners. He is at his sharpest in "The US-China war for tech supremacy is splitting the world in two" (2020), where he synthesizes the Farrell-Newman framework, Kai-Fu Lee's data empire analysis, and the Splinternet trajectory into a single coherent picture of where the contest is heading.

**Jabin T. Jacob** contributes the most analytically clear-eyed China coverage in the FF corpus. Where other China analysts either celebrate China's achievements or catastrophize its threat, Jacob forensically documents how Chinese foreign policy actually operates — deception, narrative construction, weaponized victimhood — without reducing China to a caricature. "The practice of Chinese foreign policy" (2023) is his sharpest FF contribution, and it is essential reading for any Indian practitioner who deals with Chinese counterparts.

**Rishikesha T Krishnan** laid the analytical foundation for FF's China innovation coverage in the 2015-2016 period. "The Chinese innovation machine" (2016) remains one of the most data-dense and analytically precise pieces in the entire FF geopolitics corpus — its comparison of Chinese and Indian R&D investment structures is still the most economical statement of why India's innovation deficit relative to China is structural rather than cultural.

**Shyam Saran** (former Foreign Secretary of India) appears primarily in masterclass formats and year-end reviews rather than bylined pieces, but his framing — that the Western-dominated international order is being dismantled by its own architects, and that India must navigate a hybrid order with no reliable anchor — is arguably the most important strategic framework in FF's recent geopolitics coverage. His clearest articulation comes in "Masterclass: The World in 2025 - Part 2" (2025).

**Haresh Chawla**'s "The Uber-Didi deal is more about China than about Uber and Didi" (2016) remains one of the most cited single pieces in FF's geopolitics body of work — not because of its Uber analysis but because of its account of how Chinese state capitalism creates market structures that serve strategic as well as commercial goals. It is the piece that established the "China designs markets for its champions" framing that has recurred throughout FF's subsequent coverage.

**Dinesh Narayanan** has contributed the most grounded and reported neighbourhood coverage — "Fire in the 'hood'" (2024) and "The Year the World Learned to Live Without America" (2025) — bringing field-level observation to bear on structural arguments about Indian strategy.

**What FF Is Skeptical Of**

FF is consistently skeptical of linear projections about China. Martin Jacques's prediction of Chinese dominance — "already looking hopelessly naive and out of date," as "FF Insights #708: China's demographic disaster" (2022) notes — is the canonical example of a projection that extrapolated a trend without adequately weighting the structural constraints (demography, debt, political economy, institutional rigidity) that complicate it. FF does not dismiss China's rise — it has documented it more carefully than most Indian media. But it consistently pushes back against the idea that the trajectory is frictionless.

FF is skeptical of India's demographic dividend narrative as it is typically deployed. The chest-thumping about having more young people than China, as "FF Insights #708" (2022) argues, "may be misplaced" — because a young population is an asset only if it is educated, skilled, and employable in the economy that exists, not the economy of thirty years ago. India's demographic dividend could as easily manifest as a social crisis if the institutional investments to realize it are not made.

FF is skeptical of US-China détente being structural. The Biden-Xi San Francisco summit of 2023 was read by much commentary as evidence of stabilization. Venkat Raman's "Does the current US-China detente signal a thawing of relations?" (2023) calls it "a tactical pause, forced by immediate geopolitical compulsions." The structural drivers of US-China competition — technology supremacy, Taiwan, Indo-Pacific military posture, values competition — are unchanged. The tactical pause does not resolve them.

FF is skeptical of the "Western values versus authoritarian capitalism" binary as a complete account of the global contest. China's state-directed model is not simply authoritarian and therefore brittle. As Chawla's 2016 analysis showed, it is purposive — designed to achieve specific economic and technological outcomes — and has been remarkably effective at doing so. The Western framing that authoritarianism and innovation are incompatible was empirically wrong for thirty years, and FF said so while others were still repeating the mantra.

FF is skeptical of travel bans as effective pandemic responses — a position that sounds niche but illustrates a broader methodological skepticism of security theater in policy. "By the time a travel ban is instituted, the virus has already gone," as "FF Daily #538: Do travel bans help?" (2021) argues. The same logic applies to geopolitical interventions more broadly: the gesture that signals resolve rarely achieves the strategic outcome it is supposed to produce.

FF is skeptical of India's Neighbourhood First policy as anything other than a slogan. The evidence Narayanan assembles in "Fire in the 'hood'" (2024) — inadequate investment in political relationships, unequivocal support for authoritarian leaders, neglect of economic integration — is a forensic case that the policy existed primarily as rhetoric.

**What FF Celebrates**

FF celebrates intellectual courage in geopolitical analysis — the willingness to say uncomfortable things about India's strategic position rather than defaulting to nationalist pride or bureaucratic euphemism.

It celebrates China's genuine innovation achievements with the same clarity that it documents China's strategic deceptions. Krishnan's detailed account of Chinese R&D investment, the three-stage innovation model, the "sea turtles" returning from Silicon Valley — all of this is written with genuine admiration for a national project executed at scale, without endorsing the political system that executed it.

FF celebrates India's strategic autonomy as a doctrine — with the important caveat that the celebration is conditional on the doctrine being actively and intelligently managed rather than passively invoked. The ideal it holds up is a confident, globally engaged India that uses its position between the blocs to extract maximum advantage for its own development, rather than allowing its non-alignment to be a cover for strategic drift.

It celebrates Kishore Mahbubani's civilizational framing — the return of Asia to its historical centrality after two centuries of Western aberration — as an antidote to the provincial self-congratulation of both Western triumphalism and Indian nationalism. The 1,800 years of two large Asian economies statistic, as cited in "The West in an era of Asian dominance" (2018), reframes the present moment not as a disruption but as a restoration.

FF celebrates the practitioners who bring technical depth to strategic questions: Waslekar on nuclear risk with real mediation experience, Kelkar on trade architecture with economic rigor, Jacob on Chinese foreign policy with disciplinary training in international relations. The quality of thinking FF holds up is analysis that is simultaneously expert enough to be credible and clear enough to be actionable.

It celebrates India's digital public infrastructure — Aadhaar, UPI, potential AI governance frameworks — as genuine contributions to the global conversation about alternatives to both US Big Tech and Chinese state surveillance. As Ramnath argued in "AI as a global public good, a digitised world, and why philosophy matters" (2019), India has the standing and the evidence base to advocate for AI as a global public good. That this advocacy has not yet happened at the required scale is a disappointment, but the potential is real and FF treats it seriously.

**The Historical Lens**

FF draws on history at three levels: the long civilizational arc, the medium-term pattern of systemic transitions, and the short-term precedent of specific policy choices.

At the civilizational level, Mahbubani's framing dominates: the last 200 years of Western dominance are the aberration, and the current period is a return to historical equilibrium. This framing is cited approvingly in "The West in an era of Asian dominance" (2018) and gives FF's coverage a patience that purely event-driven analysis lacks. Short-term fluctuations — the US-China trade war, the Russia sanctions, the India-China border skirmishes — are legible as episodes in a much longer story of civilizational repositioning.

At the medium-term level, Waslekar's recurring comparison of the 2020s to the 1920s is FF's most alarming historical frame. As he argues in "Masterclass: The World in 2024" (2024): the breakdown of multilateral institutions, the rise of nationalist leaders, economic volatility, and great-power competition all echo the interwar period that preceded the most destructive conflict in human history. Mussolini and Hitler became popular in the context of democracies under economic stress and institutional failure. The comparison is not a prediction — it is a warning about the conditions that produce the worst outcomes, conditions that FF's evidence suggests are present in more than sufficient measure.

Will and Ariel Durant's "everything repeats itself" — invoked in "Life is unfair. Deal with it" (2017) by Charles Assisi — operates as FF's deepest methodological assumption about history: that the specific configurations of technology, power, and human psychology that produce certain kinds of crises recur, even when the surface details are entirely different.

At the short-term precedent level, the 1993 BPTA negotiation is the most analytically useful historical case in FF's coverage. "India's negotiations with China: The boundary question" (2022) shows how a decision made under acute pressure — accepting ambiguous LAC language to avoid confrontation while simultaneously managing Pakistan-sponsored terrorism — locked India into thirty years of strategic ambiguity that produced the Galwan confrontation. The lesson is not that the 1993 decision was wrong given the constraints of 1993. The lesson is that provisional solutions to structural problems tend to calcify into permanent vulnerabilities. This is a lesson that applies directly to the provisional arrangements India is currently making on data sovereignty, technology dependence, and trade architecture — arrangements that, without deliberate review, will become the structural constraints of 2044.

Lee Kuan Yew appears in FF's historical lens not primarily as a model for Singapore's development success, but as a practitioner who thought clearly about difficult problems that polite discourse avoids. His assessment that "we should learn to live with the Pakistan-terror nexus for a long time," cited in "FF Daily #471: Lee Kuan Yew on reining extremism" (2021), is valued precisely because it is the kind of unsentimental assessment that official Indian diplomacy cannot make publicly. FF uses Yew as a calibration instrument: how does this look from the perspective of someone who has no illusions and no domestic audience to manage?

---

**Unresolved Tensions**

The deepest unresolved tension in FF's geopolitics coverage is between two simultaneously true claims: that India must engage the world confidently and openly, and that India's engagement has repeatedly left it structurally vulnerable.

The confident engagement argument — made most directly in "WFH Daily #116: Why India should bat for globalisation now" (2020), citing Kelkar, Mashelkar, and Rajadhyaksha — is that economic isolation at a turning point in the global system would be a strategic mistake. India needs trade, investment, ideas, and innovation. It cannot afford to wall itself off. This is true.

The structural vulnerability argument — made across the data sovereignty pieces, the India-China border analysis, the Neighbourhood First failure documentation — is that India's engagement has been insufficiently strategic. It has allowed data ecosystems to be colonized, neighbourhood relationships to atrophy, AI governance to fall behind, and trade dependencies to deepen without building countervailing capabilities. This is also true.

These two truths point in different directions and FF has not resolved the tension between them. The "engage but strategically" formulation is available, but it is less a resolution than a restatement of the problem. What does strategic engagement actually look like when the most powerful country in the world is strategically incoherent, when the rules-based order is being dismantled by its architects, and when India's institutional capacity to execute complex strategic moves is genuinely limited?

A second unresolved tension is between the celebration of India's strategic autonomy — its famous ability to maintain relationships with all sides — and the growing evidence that this autonomy is being bought at escalating cost. The Russian oil purchases that demonstrate Indian independence also constitute dependence on Russian energy and goodwill. The refusal to endorse Western sanctions language maintains diplomatic space but creates pressure from Washington. The "de-hyphenation" approach in West Asia maintains relations with Israel, Arab Gulf states, and Iran simultaneously — until the day it can't. FF documents all of this honestly but does not resolve the question of when calibrated ambiguity becomes unsustainable.

A third tension is between FF's skepticism of China's narrative construction — Jacob's forensic account of Chinese victimhood performance, deception, and false narrative — and FF's simultaneous insistence that India must engage China pragmatically rather than emotionally. If China is systematically deceptive, what does pragmatic engagement actually look like? How do you negotiate with a party whose core foreign policy tool, as Jacob argues, is deception? FF holds both positions without reconciling them.

**The Missing Voices**

FF's geopolitics coverage is weighted toward strategic, economic, and technological analysis produced by a relatively narrow range of contributors — senior policy practitioners, economists, think-tank analysts, and business journalists, almost all urban and English-speaking.

Missing is the perspective of the Indian small manufacturer who is directly affected by supply chain fragmentation — the Surat textile producer who lost Chinese fabric suppliers, the Chennai auto-component maker navigating US export controls, the Bengaluru software firm caught between US data localization requirements and Indian client data storage rules. These practitioners have ground-level intelligence about how geopolitical shifts translate into operational reality that no strategic analyst possesses.

The Gulf diaspora — the millions of Indian workers in the GCC region whose remittances underpin household economies across several states — is referenced as a statistic in the Iran coverage but does not appear as a voice. Their experience of geopolitical instability — the visa restrictions during the 2020 pandemic, the periodic diplomatic tensions that affect their contracts, the Gulf transition away from hydrocarbon dependence that threatens their livelihoods — is a missing dimension in FF's geopolitics coverage.

Regional India — non-English-speaking, non-metropolitan — is almost entirely absent from FF's geopolitical analysis. The farmers in Punjab who export basmati rice to Pakistan's informal market through third-country routes, the fisherfolk in Tamil Nadu who navigate the India-Sri Lanka maritime boundary, the traders in Manipur whose commerce flows through Myanmar — these are actors in India's geopolitical reality whose perspectives are not represented in FF's coverage.

Africa appears in FF's coverage primarily as a theater for US-China competition — "Is Africa swapping out Europe for China?" — rather than as a set of actors with their own strategic logic, economic priorities, and institutional capacities. The African Union's Agenda 2063, African technology entrepreneurship, African approaches to digital governance — these would substantially change the analysis if they were present.

Dissenting voices within China — the economists and analysts who argue that Xi's political consolidation has damaged Chinese economic dynamism, the business people who quietly resist the BRI logic — are referenced in FF's coverage but not given direct voice. This is partly a structural constraint (access), but it leaves FF's picture of Chinese internal dynamics less complete than its picture of Chinese external behavior.

---

**What This Means For You**

The one belief you are probably carrying that the evidence says is wrong: that geopolitics is something that happens to you, a weather system you can track and accommodate but not fundamentally engage with. The securitization of technology, data, and trade — documented across FF's entire evidence base — means that every significant business decision you make about supply chains, technology platforms, data architecture, and market access is now simultaneously a geopolitical decision. You are making these choices whether or not you have a framework for making them. The absence of a framework does not make you neutral. It makes you exposed.

The one thing to stop: treating your technology stack as a vendor relationship. Where your cloud infrastructure sits, which AI systems you are training your business processes on, which payment rails your transactions flow through, which messaging platforms your teams use — these are no longer purely commercial decisions. As Purushottam argues in "India's AI Gambit" (2025), India's data ecosystem has been "pillaged" partly because the people making infrastructure decisions treated them as procurement decisions. You cannot afford to do the same. Stop renewing technology contracts without a deliberate assessment of the geopolitical dependencies they create.

The one thing to start: a systematic mapping of your organization's exposure to the three structural vulnerabilities FF's evidence identifies — technology stack dependency (which jurisdiction owns your data, your AI, your core infrastructure), supply chain geography (where your critical inputs originate and which political relationships those origin points depend on), and market access fragility (which of your revenue streams depend on bilateral relationships that are under active geopolitical pressure). This is not a consultant's exercise. Do it yourself, in a room with your operations head, your technology lead, and your CFO, in two hours, on a whiteboard. You will find things you did not know you were exposed to. Then you can start building the options that strategic autonomy actually requires — not as doctrine, but as deliberate, maintained, resourced capacity.

The conviction to carry out of this page: in the world that is actually forming, the companies and institutions that will have strategic options in 2035 are the ones that are building them now — not waiting for the geopolitical weather to clear before deciding where to stand.
