# Policy, Governance & Regulation

---

## PART 1 — THE OPENING

Here is the problem nobody states plainly: India has more policy than it can implement, more regulation than it can enforce, and more governance ambition than it has state capacity to deliver. The gap between what is written and what actually happens — between the circular and the outcome, the scheme and the school, the law and the lived reality — is not a bug in the system. It is the system.

The conventional understanding fails in two directions at once. The optimist sees liberalisation, digital infrastructure, GST, UPI, and concludes that India is reforming steadily, that the arc bends toward order. The pessimist sees corruption, regulatory capture, and judicial delay and concludes nothing changes. Both are wrong in the same way: they treat policy as something that governments make and citizens receive, rather than as a contested, often accidental process shaped by incentives, information failures, state capacity limits, and the political economy of who benefits from stasis.

What this page gives you is something neither camp offers: a practitioner-level account of how Indian policy actually works — why good laws produce bad outcomes, why regulatory architecture that was built to stop crime ends up stopping enterprise, why technology is neither the solution nor the enemy but an amplifier of whatever institutional quality already exists, and why the distance between Delhi's policy announcements and the lives of 900 million people in Bharat is not a communication problem but a structural one.

If you are building a business, designing a programme, advising a ministry, or trying to understand why your most rational strategy keeps hitting invisible walls — this is the map you need.

## PART 2 — PRINCIPLES

---

### Principle 1

> The problem with Indian regulation is not that it is too much or too little — it is that it was designed to prevent crime, not enable enterprise.

**What This Means & Why**
India's regulatory architecture was built layer by layer since the License Raj, and each layer was constructed around the assumption that the entity being regulated was a potential criminal, not a potential creator of value. The result is a system where compliance costs fall hardest on the most legitimate actors — the small manufacturer who actually tries to follow the rules — while sophisticated actors learn to navigate the discretion that enforcement officers possess. Most people assume regulation is neutral friction; in India, it is a selective tax on good faith.

**The India Reality**
The business regulatory environment affects small enterprises most of all because their owners are hands-on operators — every hour diverted to a government functionary is an hour stolen from production. India's manufacturing sector, the one that was supposed to absorb the demographic dividend, bears this burden most acutely.

**The Evidence**
In 'Want "Make in India" to succeed? Improve the ease of doing business' (2015), Arun Maira argues that India's regulatory framework is "based on a jail principle; intended to stop criminality, not encourage enterprise" — and that the cost falls not on large firms with compliance departments but on the small entrepreneur whose only competitive advantage is time. The logic of "Make in India" collapses if the regulatory environment treats every manufacturer as a suspect.

**The Implication**
Before recommending any new regulation, map which actor bears the compliance cost and whether that actor is the one the regulation was designed to discipline.

### Principle 2

> State capacity is not a soft constraint — it is the binding one. India can write any law; it can implement very few.

**What This Means & Why**
The failure mode that recurs across Indian governance is not bad policy design — it is policies designed for a state that does not yet exist. India imports regulatory ambition from the West without importing the institutional infrastructure that makes Western regulation work: independent agencies, data systems, trained enforcement officers, and a culture of rule-following in both government and business. When the state attempts more than its capacity supports, the gap between law and enforcement does not just create inefficiency — it creates discretion, and discretion creates corruption.

**The India Reality**
Tabarrok and Rajagopalan's paper *Premature Imitation and India's Flailing State* names this precisely: Indian elites take cues on what is "normal and desirable" from Western elites, but imitation is premature because India lacks the state capacity to enforce the edicts of a developed country. The analogy is a Ferrari on Bengaluru roads — not wrong as a vehicle, wrong for the infrastructure.

**The Evidence**
In 'When the solution you imitate doesn't match ground realities' (2019), Founding Fuel cites both the Tabarrok-Rajagopalan framework and the extended case of the solar cooker — a technology championed by Indian policymakers for political rather than scientific reasons, implemented without testing, and whose failure "set back applied science by at least a decade." The "cooker affair," as it became known, is the archetype for what happens when policy ambition outruns implementation honesty, as Arun Mohan Sukumar documented in 'The cooker affair: A cautionary tale of using science for political ends' (2020).

**The Implication**
Before designing any new policy intervention, explicitly assess it against Vijay Kelkar and Ajay Shah's four-variable test from *In Service of the Republic*: number of transactions, level of discretion, stakes for private persons, degree of secrecy — and only proceed if the state can actually manage those variables at scale.

### Principle 3

> Good economic outcomes and bad governance coexist in India for exactly as long as it takes for the political consequences to arrive — typically 25 years.

**What This Means & Why**
Economic decisions are made on four-year electoral cycles but their political consequences arrive on generational ones. This is why Indian policymakers consistently underestimate the political cost of exclusionary growth — not because they are short-sighted, but because the feedback loop is so long that the people who made the decision are rarely the ones who face the consequences. The assumption that growth is its own political legitimacy has been proved wrong repeatedly; what it produces instead is a deferred reckoning.

**The India Reality**
The 1966 rupee devaluation changed the internal politics of Congress and created the political conditions for the Emergency. The 1991 liberalisation created a new middle class that, ten to fifteen years later, became the political base for the BJP. Economic decisions do not just have economic consequences — they restructure who has power, who is excluded, and who eventually demands redress.

**The Evidence**
In 'Economy and Electorate: The Road to 2024' (2022), Founding Fuel cites Niranjan Rajadhyaksha's argument that "the relinking with the global economy led to the creation of a new emerging middle class — which 10-15 years later became the political base for the BJP." The same piece documents how demonetisation's political consequences were misread in real time — framed as a masterstroke when its actual impact on BJP's 2017 UP sweep was, according to practitioner Anil Swaroop, negligible.

**The Implication**
When evaluating any major economic policy, extend your consequence horizon to fifteen years and ask who will be newly powerful and who newly excluded at that time horizon — not at the next election.

### Principle 4

> Technology amplifies existing institutional quality. Where governance is strong, it magnifies the benefit. Where it is weak, it magnifies the harm.

**What This Means & Why**
The persistent error in Indian technology policy — from e-governance to fintech to AI — is to treat technology as a substitute for institutional quality rather than a multiplier of it. The fantasy is that a digital system will bypass the corrupt official, reach the deserving beneficiary, and deliver outcomes that the state has failed to deliver for decades. The reality is that the digital system inherits all the incentive failures, data gaps, and power asymmetries of the analogue one, and often makes them less visible rather than more correctable.

**The India Reality**
India's most celebrated technology achievement — Aadhaar — demonstrates both sides of this principle simultaneously. Where the institutional context was strong (financial inclusion, direct benefit transfer in states with capable implementation), the outcomes were transformational: account ownership among women in India's poorest 40 percent rose by more than 30 percentage points between 2014 and 2017. Where institutional context was weak — midday meal programmes in states with poor administrative follow-through — biometric authentication produced exclusion rather than inclusion.

**The Evidence**
In 'Fantasy sports & future of sports; Facebook & hate speech; Contact tracing' (2020), N S Ramnath cites Kentaro Toyama's Law of Amplification — "technology's primary effect is to amplify human forces" — to explain why contact-tracing apps in India succeeded or failed based almost entirely on non-technological factors: trust in government, last-mile health worker capacity, and willingness to report. The Aarogya Setu analysis reached the same conclusion: "the most important factors in fighting the [pandemic] are in fact outside the app."

**The Implication**
Before deploying technology as a governance solution, map the institutional preconditions that technology requires to amplify rather than distort — and fix those first.

### Principle 5

> Consent is not a meaningful privacy protection. Accountability for harm is.

**What This Means & Why**
The consent model of data protection — which requires users to agree to terms before their data is collected — fails for three structural reasons: consent fatigue (people agree to everything), information asymmetry (users cannot understand what they are agreeing to), and the fundamental imbalance between a platform's legal team and a user's five seconds of attention. The consent framework places the burden on the weakest party and relieves the strongest of accountability. The shift that actually matters is from consent to accountability: does the data controller bear liability for harm regardless of what the user agreed to?

**The India Reality**
India is becoming data-rich before it becomes economically rich — an unprecedented historical situation that no governance framework in the world was designed to address. The 400 million people at the bottom of the pyramid who previously had no identity document are now generating data trails without any framework to protect their interests or any comprehension of what is being extracted from them.

**The Evidence**
In 'Consent isn't enough to protect data. We need accountability: Rahul Matthan' (2017), N S Ramnath documents Matthan's argument that the rights model — which shifts burden from data subject to data controller and focuses on harm regardless of consent — is structurally superior to the consent model. The Equifax case is deployed as counter-evidence against the argument that private enterprises protect data better: the company spent $500,000 lobbying to kill accountability rules, then sold shares after a breach but before disclosure.

**The Implication**
In any data partnership, contract, or product design involving Indian users, replace consent clauses with liability clauses — make the data controller responsible for harm, not just compliant with a disclosure process.

### Principle 6

> Corporate boards in India are not governance mechanisms. They are legitimacy mechanisms — and the difference destroys value at scale.

**What This Means & Why**
A governance mechanism makes decisions, challenges management, and bears accountability. A legitimacy mechanism endorses decisions already made, provides social cover for the powerful, and diffuses accountability so thoroughly that no individual is ever responsible. Indian corporate boards, with honourable exceptions, function as the second kind. The evidence is not episodic — the Tata-Mistry battle, the Chanda Kochhar case, the NSE yogi scandal — it is structural: boards staffed with loyalists, conflicts of interest undisclosed to shareholders, and a culture where challenging the promoter or CEO is treated as a breach of relationship rather than a fulfilment of fiduciary duty.

**The India Reality**
India's corporate governance failures are not occasional scandals — they are the predictable output of a system where promoter control, relationship banking, and regulatory capture have coexisted for decades. The banking sector compounds this: public sector banks operate under an "architecture of fear" where business decisions are viewed through the lens of criminality, making both bold lending and honest admission of failure equally impossible.

**The Evidence**
In 'Why the worst may not be over for Chanda Kochhar' (2019), Indrajit Gupta asks whether the bank board's eventual action absolved it of "not discharging its fiduciary responsibilities to its shareholders earlier and kow-towing to a powerful CEO." The question is rhetorical — the board's prior silence was not accidental but structural, a function of how Indian boards are composed and what they believe their role to be.

**The Implication**
If you sit on a board, or advise one, treat every major decision through the lens of the shareholder or stakeholder who is not in the room — not the promoter or CEO who is.

### Principle 7

> India is a graveyard of successful pilots. The problem is never the idea. It is always the system that the idea must travel through.

**What This Means & Why**
The pattern is consistent enough to be a law: an intervention works spectacularly at a small scale, attracts international attention, and then fails to scale because the conditions that made it work — a dedicated champion, a sympathetic local government, a community with unusual social capital — cannot be replicated across India's diversity. The conclusion most people draw is that scale is hard. The conclusion that should be drawn is that India's governance architecture was not designed to enable replication, and that designing for scale from the beginning requires a completely different approach than designing for proof of concept.

**The India Reality**
India's federalism creates 28 simultaneous experiments in governance, which should be an advantage — the best practices should propagate. They rarely do, because the incentive structures that allow a state to innovate do not include any mechanism for sharing that innovation with rival states, and because the central government has historically preferred to design national schemes rather than adopt state innovations.

**The Evidence**
In 'Purpose-Driven Networks: Sustaining large-scale movements of change' (2019), Kiran Karnik states the pattern directly: "India is a classic case of 'graveyard of successful pilots'. There are countless examples of pilot projects or micro-level efforts which work very well, but fail to scale." The observation is not novel — what is notable is that it applies with equal force to technology pilots, health programmes, financial inclusion schemes, and skills initiatives.

**The Implication**
Before launching any pilot, design the scale pathway explicitly — map the institutional actors who must change behaviour for the intervention to move from 10,000 to 10 million, and either secure their commitment or redesign the intervention so that it does not require it.

### Principle 8

> The informal economy is not a transitional phase. It is the permanent architecture of Indian labour — and every policy that ignores it fails the majority.

**What This Means & Why**
India's informal sector employs 93 percent of the total workforce. This is not a development failure awaiting correction — it is the structural reality within which any labour, health, financial, or social policy must operate. Policies designed for formal employment relationships — contributory insurance, statutory benefits, regulated working conditions — leave nine out of ten Indian workers unprotected by design. The political economy explanation for this is that the formal-sector worker is organized, vocal, and visible to policymakers; the informal worker is none of these things.

**The India Reality**
The migrant labour crisis exposed by the 2020 lockdown was not a surprise to anyone who had studied India's employment structure — it was a structural revelation made visible by an extreme event. The workers who walked hundreds of kilometres home were not edge cases; they were the representative case. Their complete invisibility to the policy apparatus that mobilized within hours for formal-sector relief tells you everything about whose interests Indian governance is actually designed to serve.

**The Evidence**
In 'The used and discarded workers of India' (2020), Arun Maira documents the structural abandonment of India's migrant workforce: "The workers were used and discarded. All Indians with compassion for their fellow citizens must ask, who will benefit if employers are given even more freedom to hire and fire?" The piece connects this directly to India's growth model: "India had the lowest employment elasticity of growth in the world — that is the numbers of jobs created with each unit of GDP growth."

**The Implication**
Design any labour, health, or social policy with 93 percent of the workforce as the primary user, not 7 percent — and test it against the specific constraints of workers who have no employer relationship and no fixed address.

### Principle 9

> Transparency, not enforcement, fixes public sector leakage. Sunlight is not a metaphor — it is a mechanism.

**What This Means & Why**
The instinct of governance reformers when facing leakage and corruption is to demand stronger enforcement: more inspectors, harsher penalties, better audits. This instinct is wrong in contexts where enforcement agencies are themselves subject to the same incentive failures as the agencies they are meant to discipline. What Uganda's capitation grant experiment demonstrated — and what India's RTI experience partially confirms — is that when information about resource flows is made public, communities themselves become the enforcement mechanism, and the cost of doing so is a fraction of what an enforcement apparatus would require.

**The India Reality**
India's public sector leakage problem is not primarily a detection problem — it is an information problem. Beneficiaries, communities, and local governments often know where resources are being diverted but have no legitimate mechanism for acting on that knowledge. The RTI Act was supposed to change this, but its implementation has been systematically weakened by the very institutions it was designed to constrain.

**The Evidence**
In 'How Uganda fixed leakages with transparency, and why it matters to India' (2017), N S Ramnath documents the finding that publishing capitation grant fund flows in local newspapers raised school receipt rates from 13 percent to over 90 percent — without any change in enforcement, staffing, or criminal sanctions. The mechanism was purely informational: communities that knew the money had been released would demand it, while communities that didn't know had no way to demand anything.

**The Implication**
Map one programme or scheme where you have visibility into the resource flow but the end beneficiaries do not, and make that flow public in a format that the beneficiaries can actually access and use.

### Principle 10

> Every regulatory intervention has a second-order consequence that arrives later, hits different people, and is never attributed to the original policy.

**What This Means & Why**
The habit of single-stage thinking in policymaking — "this intervention will produce this outcome" — is what Kaushik Basu calls the inability to do "two-stage thinking." Real systems respond to interventions in ways that are logical but unanticipated, and the people who bear the cost of these second-order effects are rarely the people who were consulted when the policy was made. The rat bounty in Hanoi produced rat farming. Suspending labour laws to attract investment produced a race to the bottom on worker rights. Regulating flash sales to protect small retailers produced constraints on consumer choice.

**The India Reality**
India's policymaking culture is particularly prone to this failure because of what Vijay Kelkar and Ajay Shah call the "cult of jald baazi" — the notion that a problem can be understood in a few days and solved in a few weeks. Speed without second-stage thinking produces interventions that create the next problem faster than they solve the current one.

**The Evidence**
In 'FF Daily #467: How people respond to incentives' (2021), Founding Fuel documents Kelkar and Shah's framework for this phenomenon: "It is dangerous to look at the world, come up with a design of an intervention, and hope that it will have a narrow impact as expected." The rat bounty story, the narrow Amsterdam houses, the steps-counting farms — all illustrate that people optimize against the measure of the policy, not the intent of it.

**The Implication**
For any policy or programme you are designing, run a structured pre-mortem: ask what rational actors will do in response to this intervention, and trace the consequences two steps beyond the intended outcome.

### Principle 11

> GDP growth that does not create jobs is not economic success — it is a delayed social crisis.

**What This Means & Why**
The political economy of India's growth model has relied on treating GDP as the measure of success and employment as a lagging indicator that will eventually follow. It has not followed. India's employment elasticity of growth collapsed from 0.44 in 1999-2004 to 0.01 in 2004-2009 — effectively jobless growth. The people who were supposed to benefit from the growth were watching from the outside, their aspirations rising with every smartphone, every visible success story, and every year in which their own situation did not change. This is the formula for political instability.

**The India Reality**
By 2050, another 280 million people will enter India's jobs market, making it the most "job stressed" country in the world. Between 1991 and 2013, the working-age population increased by 300 million but only 140 million could be employed. The demographic dividend that Indian policymakers celebrate is arithmetically guaranteed to become a demographic catastrophe without structural changes to the employment elasticity of growth.

**The Evidence**
In 'Solving India's problem of jobless growth' (2016), Arun Maira frames this as a "5C problem: a Complicated Condition Created by Combinations of Causes" — arguing that conventional management approaches that decompose the problem into parts and solve them separately make it worse, because jobless growth is a systems problem, not a sectoral one. The UNDP data he cites — 300 million new workers, 140 million jobs — is the arithmetic behind every agrarian distress story, every engineering graduate in a call centre, and every Jat reservation demand.

**The Implication**
Evaluate any major business or policy decision not just for its direct economic output but for its employment elasticity — how many jobs does it create per unit of value generated, and is that number rising or falling?

### Principle 12

> The purpose of the economy is to serve human well-being. When that purpose inverts — when people are asked to serve the economy — governance breaks down.

**What This Means & Why**
This sounds philosophical until you trace its practical consequences. When the purpose of governance is to maximize GDP, the natural outcome is policies that benefit capital over labour, growth metrics over health metrics, and the already-integrated over the structurally excluded. The institutions that result — the planning commission, the finance ministry, the corporate regulator — are optimized to track and improve the number rather than the reality behind the number. The people whose well-being cannot be captured in a number become invisible to the apparatus.

**The India Reality**
India's growth model over three decades lifted more people out of poverty than any comparable intervention in history. It also produced what Arun Maira, in 'India@100: A vision for transformative change' (2022), calls a growth that "wasn't inclusive — too many millions are excluded because of failure in the education and the employment systems." The contradiction is real and unresolved: the same model that reduced extreme poverty also widened the gap between those with access to the new economy and those without it.

**The Evidence**
In 'Who do economists serve, really?' (2020), Maira cites Stiglitz, Sen, and Fitoussi's *Measuring What Counts* alongside Banerjee and Duflo's *Good Economics for Hard Times* to make the argument that "economics is too important to be left to economists" — because economists searching for keys where the light is, not where they are lost, produce policy that optimizes the measurable at the expense of the important.

**The Implication**
In any policy or strategy review, replace GDP growth as the leading metric with at least two measures of human well-being — employment quality, health outcomes, or educational attainment — and treat GDP as a lagging indicator, not a leading one.

### Principle 13

> Importing regulation from developed countries without importing the institutional infrastructure that makes it work is not reform. It is theatre.

**What This Means & Why**
The GDPR is a functioning regulation in the EU because it is backed by independent data protection authorities with real enforcement budgets, a legal culture of compliance, and companies that fear reputational cost. When India drafts data protection legislation that structurally resembles GDPR, none of those preconditions exist. The law passes. The institutions that would enforce it are underfunded, understaffed, or politically compromised. The companies that would comply calculate whether the cost of compliance exceeds the cost of non-compliance. Usually it does not. The regulation exists on paper; the protection does not exist in practice.

**The India Reality**
India's GST is the canonical example. Vijay Kelkar designed a clean, rational framework. What was implemented had too many slabs, was confusing, and ran contrary to all of Kelkar's recommendations — the result of infighting between the Finance Ministry and IRS bureaucrats over institutional turf. The design was imported; the institutional conditions for it were not created. As Founding Fuel documented in 'FF Insights #623: The economic landscape' (2022), "it boomeranged."

**The Evidence**
In 'Is banning facial recognition the right option for India too?' (2019), N S Ramnath observes that "India hasn't had a good record in passing regulations/laws with respect to technology. The Manmohan Singh government did not pass the Aadhaar Act for five years. And the Narendra Modi government passed it as a money bill, which created its own problems." The pattern is consistent: the technical form of the regulation arrives; the institutional substance does not.

**The Implication**
Before advocating for any regulatory reform drawn from another country's experience, map the three or four institutional preconditions that make that regulation effective in its home context — and assess honestly whether those preconditions exist or can be created in India.

### Principle 14

> Technology is politics. Every technical choice is a political choice — about who controls data, who sets standards, and whose rules everyone else must follow.

**What This Means & Why**
The fiction that technology is neutral — a tool that works the same way regardless of who wields it or in whose interest — is the most dangerous idea in contemporary governance. The design of a platform determines who profits from it. The architecture of an identity system determines who can be excluded from services. The training data of an AI model determines whose experience is treated as normal and whose is treated as an anomaly. These are not technical questions. They are governance questions that have been outsourced to engineers and venture capitalists by default because nobody with political authority claimed them.

**The India Reality**
The geopolitics of 5G made this visible even to those who had been wilfully blind: the choice of telecommunications infrastructure is simultaneously a choice about whose surveillance apparatus you will operate inside, whose standards you will depend on, and whose regulatory requirements will shape your domestic tech industry. India's AI policy — made by MeitY and Niti Aayog without meaningful National Security Council involvement, according to sources cited in 'India's AI Gambit' (2025) — illustrates how the political dimensions of technology choices are still being underweighted.

**The Evidence**
In 'Technology is politics' (2019), N S Ramnath states the argument as a title and demonstrates it through 5G and Huawei: "Nothing demonstrates it as well as the geopolitics around 5G." The choice between suppliers is simultaneously a choice about data flows, technical standards, and the architecture of surveillance — a governance decision masquerading as a procurement decision.

**The Implication**
In any significant technology procurement or policy decision your organisation or ministry is making, explicitly map the governance implications — who controls the data, who sets the standards, who can cut off access — before evaluating the technical specifications.

### Principle 15

> Criminal sanctions in economic law are a tool of harassment, not justice, when state capacity is low and prosecutorial discretion is high.

**What This Means & Why**
The logic of harsh penalties — that higher expected costs deter economic crime — requires that enforcement be accurate, that prosecutors be honest, and that the target of enforcement actually be guilty. When those conditions fail, which they do routinely in India, harsh criminal penalties become a tool by which enforcement agencies extract rents from legitimate businesses, by which political adversaries are pursued selectively, and by which entrepreneurial risk-taking is chilled precisely when it is most needed. The problem is not that criminals go unpunished; it is that non-criminals face criminal risk.

**The India Reality**
India has more criminal offences in economic law than it did in the 1970s — a perverse inversion of liberalisation's intent. The "Angel Tax" — Section 56 of the Income Tax Act, which treats startup funding as taxable income — is Haresh Chawla's named example in 'No, there is nothing called Angel Tax' (2019): a country that needs more entrepreneurs has a provision that harasses founders precisely when they need support. "There is no such thing called Angel Tax" in any rational governance framework; it exists because of an archaic provision designed to prevent money laundering that was never adapted for a startup ecosystem.

**The Evidence**
In 'FF Insights #672: Justice bulldozed?' (2022), Founding Fuel cites Kelkar and Shah's *In Service of the Republic*: "Criminal sanctions have the harshest effect in terms of reducing the freedom of individuals. Under conditions of low state capacity, criminal penalties put supreme power in the hands of enforcement agencies. Harsh punishments go with wrongful raids, arrests, prosecutions and even convictions."

**The Implication**
In any regulatory design or policy advocacy work, push explicitly for civil penalties over criminal sanctions in economic law, and reserve criminal liability for cases where harm is demonstrably intentional and the evidentiary standard is genuinely high.

### Principle 16

> India's institutions are built on intent, not on systems — which is why they are brilliant in the hands of exceptional individuals and dysfunctional in the hands of ordinary ones.

**What This Means & Why**
The organisation that works spectacularly when the right person leads it and collapses when they leave is not an organisation — it is a person with an org chart attached. India has many such organisations. The problem is not a shortage of exceptional individuals; it is that the institutional design does not capture their insights, embed their practices, or transfer their capabilities. Every time India produces a remarkable public servant, a transformative regulator, or an honest police commissioner, the system treats them as an exceptional case rather than as a template for redesign.

**The India Reality**
The Delhi Metro, often cited as India's governance success story, is inseparable from E. Sreedharan's personal authority and discipline. Founding Fuel's coverage of the Sreedharan mythology pushes back precisely here: "All complexity in the world for him is reduced to an engineering problem," which is the logic of a brilliant individual, not the logic of an institution. The person who remains "totally unsung in the Delhi Metro story" — the institutional designer whose systems would survive the departure of any individual — is the person India consistently fails to produce or celebrate.

**The Evidence**
In 'FF Daily #329: How to build iconic institutions' (2021), Founding Fuel cites the Pratap Bhanu Mehta/Ashoka University resignation as "yet another example of the difficulties of building institutions" — and frames institutional resilience as a design challenge, not a personnel challenge. The piece on India's engineering college boom, 'An engineering college boom built on a foundation of dysfunction' (2021) by M. Rajshekhar, documents the converse: when perverse incentives — black money laundering via land and education revenues — become the structural reality, no exceptional individual can overcome the system.

**The Implication**
For any institution you lead or advise, identify the three decisions that currently depend on the judgment of a single person and design systems, protocols, or distributed authority structures that would produce acceptable outcomes in that person's absence.

### Principle 17

> The real test of a platform — corporate or governmental — is whether the economic value of everyone who uses it exceeds the value of the company that created it.

**What This Means & Why**
The platform framing has become so common that it has lost its discipline. Everything is now a platform. But the original insight — that a genuine platform creates more value for its participants than it captures for its owners — has a sharp implication for governance: a platform that extracts more value than it creates is not a platform, it is a monopoly with network effects. This applies with equal force to UPI (which has created enormous value for all participants) and to Zomato's relationship with restaurants (which periodically extracts more value than it creates, triggering political and regulatory backlash).

**The India Reality**
India's digital public infrastructure — Aadhaar, UPI, India Stack — was explicitly designed on the platform principle: "the economic value of everybody that uses it exceeds the value of the company that creates it." This is what makes it structurally different from both the American model (where private platforms capture the surplus) and the Chinese model (where the state captures it). The Account Aggregator framework's "no data stored by intermediary" design is a direct expression of this principle — the consent manager gets no value from the data it routes.

**The Evidence**
In 'For a platform, how much control is too much control?' (2019), Founding Fuel quotes Harsh Vardhan on Zomato: "In any partnership, if a key player tries to hog more value than he is creating in the game, the market will eventually punish him." The piece uses TCP/IP's founding design principles — no central control, no optimization for a specific application — as the governance model for what a genuinely open platform looks like.

**The Implication**
For any platform business or public digital infrastructure you are involved with, measure the ratio of value created for participants to value captured by the platform owner — and treat a declining ratio as an early warning of regulatory or market backlash before it arrives.

### Principle 18

> India's manufacturing story has not failed because of bad policy. It has failed because of the wrong sequence: policy before institutions, ambition before infrastructure, announcement before implementation.

**What This Means & Why**
Make in India was launched in 2014 with the ambition of raising manufacturing's share of GDP to 25 percent by 2025. A decade later, it stands at 14 percent. The failure is not primarily a policy failure — the incentives exist, the PLI schemes exist, the rhetoric is consistent. The failure is sequential: you cannot build a manufacturing economy before you have the power supply, the logistics, the skill base, the financial infrastructure, and the dispute resolution mechanisms that manufacturing requires. Every Asian economy that successfully industrialised built institutions first and announced ambitions second.

**The India Reality**
India's electronics industry illustrates the sequence failure precisely. The domestic electronics industry in FY2017 was worth $47 billion; imports were $86 billion — almost twice the domestic industry. The failure traces not to the 2014 Make in India announcement but to import substitution ideology and license raj policies from the 1960s that prevented India from building the component ecosystem that electronics manufacturing requires. As N Dayasindhu documented in 'Removing the impedance for a vibrant electronics industry in India' (2020), the institutional deficit is decades deep.

**The Evidence**
In 'Trendspotting 2026: India Cannot App Its Way to Prosperity' (2026), Haresh Chawla states the structural failure plainly: "Every Asian success story — South Korea, Taiwan, China, Vietnam — built its middle class by moving workers from farms to factories. India is caught in a skip of this essential manufacturing phase." The observation is not a critique of India's digital achievements but of the assumption that digital services can substitute for the institutional development that manufacturing drives.

**The Implication**
For any manufacturing investment decision, evaluate the institutional infrastructure in the specific location — power reliability, logistics quality, local supplier ecosystem, dispute resolution speed — before evaluating the policy incentives, which exist everywhere but work almost nowhere.

---

### Principle 19

> AI governance is being made by the ministries that want to use AI, not by the institutions that understand its risks — and this is the most dangerous regulatory gap India currently has.

**What This Means & Why**
The pattern of AI governance globally is that the agencies responsible for promoting AI adoption — trade ministries, technology departments, economic planning bodies — are making the rules, while the agencies responsible for security, safety, and existential risk are not meaningfully involved. This is not surprising; it follows the logic of institutional incentives. But the consequences are structurally different from previous waves of technology governance, because the risks of AI — particularly frontier AI — are qualitatively unlike the risks of previous technologies: they are non-linear, potentially irreversible, and operate on timescales that make the 25-year political consequence cycle irrelevant.

**The India Reality**
India's AI policy is made by MeitY and Niti Aayog. The National Security Council's level of involvement is uncertain at best. The US restricts GPU exports to India partly because India has not engaged with frontier risk frameworks — which means India's development-focused AI ambitions are constrained by a security architecture it has not yet built. The gap between India's stated AI ambitions and its actual AI governance infrastructure is wide and widening.

**The Evidence**
In 'India's AI Gambit' (2025), Founding Fuel documents the specific governance gap: "The problem with Indian AI policy is, it's made by MeitY and Niti Aayog... I don't know how much the National Security Council is involved." Sundeep Waslekar, in 'Oppenheimer's Lesson for the Age of AI' (2025), quotes a senior policymaker who told him there was "no reason to worry" about catastrophic AI risks — citing this as representative of a dismissiveness toward frontier risk that Nobel laureates who built the technology do not share.

**The Implication**
If you are advising any institution — government, corporate, or civil society — on AI deployment, build a governance structure that separates the deployment decision (which can be made by the user organisation) from the risk assessment (which must include people who are not invested in the deployment succeeding).

### Principle 20

> Democratic institutions are not self-maintaining. They require active, continuous investment — and that investment must come from civil society before it is demanded from the state.

**What This Means & Why**
The assumption that democratic institutions, once established, perpetuate themselves through their own logic is contradicted by every historical case of democratic backsliding. Institutions require constituencies — people and organisations whose interests are served by the institution functioning well, who will defend it when it is threatened, and who will provide the political cost of attacking it. When those constituencies are absent, weak, or captured, institutions erode without drama: not through coups or crises, but through the quiet accumulation of small decisions that each seem defensible in isolation.

**The India Reality**
The 2024 Lok Sabha results are treated in FF's coverage as a partial corrective — "hope flickers to life," in the framing of '13 Most Memorable Moments: Lok Sabha Elections 2024' (2024), describing the election as checking "unbridled authoritarianism." But a corrective delivered by elections alone, without the deeper institutional reinforcement of media independence, judicial autonomy, and electoral commission integrity, is a corrective that can be reversed. The Pratap Bhanu Mehta/Ashoka University episode, the media capture documented across multiple FF pieces, and the constitutional literacy workshops Vinita Gursahani Singh runs in rural India with marginalised communities — all point to the same underlying condition: democratic institutions in India are contested, not settled.

**The Evidence**
In 'We, the People of India' (2025), Vinita Gursahani Singh frames constitutional literacy as active civic obligation — "We, the People of India" not as a founding phrase but as a present-tense claim requiring continuous renewal. The piece documents hundreds of workshops with rural and marginalised communities as a practitioner's response to the institutional deficit, grounded in the conviction that the Constitution is a living resource, not a historical document.

**The Implication**
Identify one democratic institution — media, judiciary, electoral commission, civil society organisation — whose independence matters to the functioning of your sector, and make a concrete, non-symbolic commitment to its financial or reputational support this year.

## FF's View

**The Core Conviction**

FF's single most important argument on policy, governance, and regulation in India is this: India's governance failures are not primarily failures of intent, design, or even corruption in the conventional sense. They are failures of implementation — and implementation failure is itself a structural condition, not an accident. The state's ambitions consistently exceed its capacity to execute. The gap between what India's policy apparatus promises and what it can actually deliver is not a temporary lag. It is the permanent character of a state that has never resolved the contradiction at its foundation: an aspiration for developmental outcomes borrowed from advanced democracies, applied through institutions that lack the administrative muscle those outcomes require.

This is an uncomfortable position. It is easier to blame specific politicians, parties, or bureaucratic cultures. FF does not do this. The evidence points consistently elsewhere — toward a state that, as Tabarrok and Rajagopalan argued in a paper cited in 'When the solution you imitate doesn't match ground realities' (2019), engages in "premature imitation": copying the regulatory architecture of developed countries without the enforcement institutions those architectures presuppose. "It's like driving a Ferrari on Bengaluru roads," NS Ramnath wrote in that same piece. The vehicle is impressive. The road cannot support it. The problem is the road, not the driver's intentions.

This conviction runs against the grain of both the technocratic optimist camp — which believes that better-designed policies will solve the problem — and the accountability camp, which believes that corruption is the core failure. FF holds both camps are partly wrong. Design matters, yes. Corruption is real, yes. But neither is the primary variable. State capacity — the ability to consistently, accurately, and fairly execute policy across India's staggering diversity — is the binding constraint. Everything else is downstream of it.

The practical implication is severe. When policymakers, practitioners, and commentators evaluate a new law, a new digital infrastructure, or a new regulatory intervention, the first question is not "is this a good idea?" The first question is: "does the Indian state have the institutional capacity to implement this without creating new forms of harm?" Most of the time, that question is not asked. FF has been asking it for a decade.

**What India Means for This Theme**

India changes every rule about how policy and governance work. This is not a cliché. It is a structural claim, and the evidence in FF's reporting supports it in ways that are specific enough to be useful.

Start with scale and diversity. The standard frameworks of governance — whether from development economics, public administration theory, or management consulting — assume a degree of institutional uniformity that India does not have. Kerala has infant mortality rates comparable to European countries; Uttar Pradesh has rates comparable to Sub-Saharan Africa. These are not policy gradations. They are, as Rishikesha T Krishnan noted in 'Affordable healthcare: India's big opportunity' (2017), evidence that India is not a single country in any policy-relevant sense. A healthcare intervention that works in Trivandrum will not work in Gorakhpur — not because people are different, but because the state institutions delivering the intervention are different, the data infrastructure supporting them is different, and the social systems within which health behaviour is embedded are different.

This matters for every domain FF covers. India is simultaneously a country where digital payment infrastructure has become globally admired — UPI processing more transactions than Visa in some months — and a country where 93 percent of the workforce operates in the informal economy, largely outside any regulatory framework. As Haresh Chawla noted in 'Trendspotting: 2020 can make or break' (2019), India has "two internet markets" — the transacting middle class and the content-consuming mass. Policy designed for one routinely ignores the other.

The Bharat dimension changes the calculation fundamentally. When you move beyond India's English-speaking, urban, formally employed population, the assumptions baked into most policy frameworks collapse. Data protection regulation assumes data literacy and institutional access to grievance mechanisms that do not exist for a subsistence farmer in Bihar. Competition law assumes formal market participation that most Indian workers and entrepreneurs never have. Labour codes assume formal employment relationships that represent perhaps fifteen percent of actual work in India. As Vineet Kaul, Vivek Patwardhan, and Satish Pradhan documented in 'India's Labour Codes: The Shift from Protecting Jobs to Formalising Work' (2025), India's dual labour market — a small formal sector with statutory protections, 85–90 percent of the workforce outside the legal frame — means that the most important policy question is not what the codes say. It is what happens when the inspection system meets real employment relationships that the codes were not designed to cover.

India's history also changes the frame. The regulatory architecture was built, as Dinesh Narayanan noted in 'Breaking the stalemate' (2015), on a "jail principle" — designed to stop criminality, not encourage enterprise. This is not an ideological claim. It is a historical one. The License Raj was not built by people who wanted to make life difficult. It was built by people who were trying to prevent the concentrated accumulation of private power that had defined colonial India and to direct scarce resources toward national development. The intellectual architecture was coherent. What it produced, over decades, was a state apparatus that defaulted to restriction, that treated the regulator's role as enforcement rather than enablement, and that accumulated criminal sanctions in economic law at a pace documented by Vijay Kelkar and Ajay Shah in 'In Service of the Republic' and cited repeatedly across FF's coverage. As noted in 'FF Insights #672: Justice bulldozed?' (2022), India now has more criminal offences in economic law than in the 1970s. In a low-state-capacity environment, criminal penalties hand "supreme power in the hands of enforcement agencies." The result is not rule of law. It is selective enforcement — which is a different thing entirely.

The post-1991 story is complicated in ways that mainstream coverage usually misses. PV Narasimha Rao's reforms, as Charles Assisi showed in 'The Narasimha Rao they don't want you to know of' (2016), were not the confident stroke of a visionary liberalizer. They were a minority government, without popular mandate for reform, led by a man with no charisma for direct popular appeal, making changes under conditions of economic crisis that made inaction impossible. "Reform is best carried out in silence, and opponents best countered singly," Vinay Sitapati quoted Rao saying in 'The man who knew tomorrow' (2016). This is not a blueprint. It is a psychological map of how reform actually happens in India — not through the triumphalist narrative of decisive leaders breaking with the past, but through quiet, incremental, adversarial manoeuvre under constraint.

That psychological map still applies. And it means that practitioners and policymakers who design their strategies around the assumption that rational policy arguments will carry the day, or that good institutions will be built because they are needed, are working from the wrong model.

**Where Global Best Practices Break Down**

The McKinsey playbook for governance reform assumes a state that can execute. The HBR literature on regulatory design assumes institutions that are insulated from political capture. The World Bank's "Ease of Doing Business" framework assumes that reducing the number of regulatory steps produces proportional improvements in business formation and investment. None of these assumptions holds reliably in India, and FF's reporting documents the breakdowns in granular detail.

The most important breakdown is the transplant fallacy. The Indian elite's tendency to import regulatory frameworks from advanced economies — and the policy elite's tendency to reward this intellectual conformity — produces what Tabarrok and Rajagopalan call "premature imitation." The example that runs through FF's Batch 4 coverage is revealing: India's data protection aspirations, modelled on GDPR, were written for a regulatory environment where the state has the capacity to investigate data controllers, impose proportionate penalties, and provide accessible grievance mechanisms to ordinary citizens. India withdrew the Personal Data Protection Bill in 2022 after years of delay. The DPDP Act of 2023 represents India's first major data protection law, but as NS Ramnath noted in 'Who owns your data — you, the creator, or the platform?' (2025), practical ambiguity remains. The question of whether data protection regulation designed for German or French institutional contexts can be implemented by Indian regulatory agencies — with their actual staffing, budget, political independence, and technical capacity — is treated as a detail. It is not a detail. It is the entire question.

The same breakdown appears in labour law. India's four new Labour Codes, documented in 'India's Labour Codes' (2025), represent genuinely comprehensive regulatory reform — consolidating 29 central laws into four, modernising definitions of employment to include gig workers, simplifying compliance. The framework is, by international standards, well-designed. The implementation question is entirely different. As Kaul, Patwardhan, and Pradhan write: "The Codes create the framework; administrative culture will determine the outcome. The acid test is simple: can the country modernise labour governance without slipping back into inspector raj?" The history of Indian labour law suggests that every new layer of compliance creates new opportunities for enforcement discretion — which in a low-capacity, high-discretion system means new opportunities for rent extraction. The best practice has been imported. The institutional soil cannot support it.

The antitrust playbook breaks down too, but differently. The global best practice on platform regulation — informed by American antitrust tradition, challenged by Tim Wu's 'Curse of Bigness' thesis (cited by NS Ramnath in 'Senator, we sell bads', 2018), and institutionalised through GDPR and the EU's Digital Markets Act — is designed for markets where incumbents have the political resources to capture regulators and the technical complexity to obscure their competitive practices. This applies to India, but with a twist: India's own platform companies, as Ramnath noted in 'App store wars' (2020), "have not been averse to using their power against smaller partners." The Paytm-Google battle, the Zomato-restaurant conflicts, the Ola and Uber driver grievances — these are all instances of Indian platform companies behaving in exactly the ways that global antitrust is meant to prevent. The assumption that the problem is only about foreign tech giants breaks down the moment you examine what Indian platforms do with the market power they accumulate.

The nudge theory playbook — beloved of the Thaler-Sunstein school and referenced approvingly in India's Economic Survey — also has specific failure modes in India. NS Ramnath's 'Nudge versus Richard Thaler' (2017) raises the paternalism problem directly: designing default behaviours that are "good for people" without their explicit consent has different political valence in a country where the state's historical relationship to marginalised populations is one of coercion, not benign guidance. The toilet-usage nudge is not politically equivalent to the retirement savings nudge. Context is everything.

**How FF Diverges from Mainstream Media**

Indian business media's dominant failure mode on governance and regulation is what might be called the celebration-then-correction cycle. A new policy is announced. The announcement is covered with enthusiasm — the headline numbers, the ambition, the minister's press conference framing. Implementation problems emerge months or years later, covered as aberrations or execution failures, without systematic connection to the flawed assumptions baked into the original design. The policy is then either quietly abandoned, modified beyond recognition, or continues to exist on paper while being ignored in practice.

FF breaks this cycle at the design stage. The Aadhaar coverage is the clearest illustration. While mainstream business media in 2016–2017 was either enthusiastically cheerleading Aadhaar as a governance revolution or reflexively condemning it as a surveillance state, FF was doing something more difficult: decomposing the question. As NS Ramnath wrote in 'The Aadhaar debate: Is common ground possible?' (2017), the debate needed to be broken into its constituent dimensions — design, implementation, scope, speed, legal framework — because these dimensions have different answers. The Supreme Court's Section 57 ruling, as documented in 'Reverse gear for fintech, identity vs identification, design for innovation' (2018), was "rife with contradictions" and hurt fintech without clearly advancing privacy. Mainstream coverage presented it as either a privacy victory or a bureaucratic obstacle. FF's coverage asked the harder question: what did it actually do to the people it was supposed to protect?

The Paytm coverage is a more recent example. Analyst and media praise of Paytm's business model persisted well after the RBI's concerns about its governance and compliance practices were becoming apparent to careful observers. When the regulatory action finally came, it was covered as a surprise. FF's Indrajit Gupta, in 'Paytm: Emperor's new clothes' (2024), was explicit about where the mainstream coverage had failed: "a case of wilful blindness" from analysts who should have known better. The "regulator doesn't understand fintech" narrative — which mainstream media amplified — was, in FF's reading, covering for a company that had systematically undermined its own regulatory relationships.

On labour, the divergence is starker. The migrant labour crisis of 2020 — the hundreds of thousands of workers walking hundreds of kilometres home after the lockdown was announced with four hours' notice — was covered as a humanitarian story. Occasionally as a logistics failure. Rarely as the structural revelation it was. Arun Maira's 'The used and discarded workers of India' (2020) made the argument that mainstream coverage could not: "The workers were used and discarded." India's growth model had depended on labour that was invisible to policy until it was suddenly, catastrophically, visible. That invisibility was not accidental. It was a feature of a regulatory architecture that had never extended its protections to the 93 percent of the workforce in the informal economy.

The Great Resignation, the labour law suspensions during COVID (documented in K Ramkumar's 'Suspending labour laws means paving a road to nowhere', 2020), the 42 percent of organised manufacturing on contract work (Vineet Kaul in 'The Quiet Drift Toward Precarity', 2025) — these are not separate stories. They are chapters in a single story that mainstream media has consistently declined to tell as a whole.

**Against Management Orthodoxy**

Business schools teach that good policy is good for business — that regulatory clarity, property rights, and rule of law reduce transaction costs, attract investment, and produce growth. This is true at a sufficient level of abstraction. The management orthodoxy fails when it comes to the specific forms good policy and good law take in the Indian context.

The orthodox position on corporate governance — strong independent boards, transparent disclosure, separation of ownership and management — is entirely correct as a principle. The evidence from India suggests it is applied selectively and superficially. The Tata-Mistry battle, covered by Indrajit Gupta in 'The silence of the lambs' (2016) and 'The Tata-Mistry battle royale: No endgame in sight' (2016), was not an aberration. It was a demonstration of what Indian corporate governance actually looks like when stress is applied: boards as rubber stamps, conflicts of interest undisclosed to shareholders, due process absent at every level. The Chanda Kochhar case at ICICI Bank, documented in 'Why the worst may not be over for Chanda Kochhar' (2019), raised the same question Gupta asked: "will this action absolve the bank board of not discharging its fiduciary responsibilities to its shareholders earlier and kow-towing to a powerful CEO?" The management orthodoxy says boards prevent this. Indian evidence says boards enabled it.

The R&D tax break is a more specific case. The orthodox position is that tax incentives for R&D stimulate innovation. India's technology sector has lobbied for these persistently, framing their removal as anti-innovation. Sachin Joneja's 'Is the R&D tax break really an incentive to innovate?' (2015) made the heterodox case: tax breaks reward "exactly the wrong kind of behaviour and at the wrong point in the life of a company." The Xerox PARC case — "a vanity project" — is the canonical illustration. Companies doing genuine innovation at the frontier do not need tax breaks. Companies doing incremental improvements to existing products do not need them either, because incremental innovation is driven by competitive pressure. The breaks largely reward companies that are already doing activities they would have done anyway, while creating bureaucratic incentive to label those activities as R&D. The actual driver of innovation, Joneja argued, is "competitive intensity of the marketplace." The orthodoxy says subsidise the input. The evidence says intensify the competition.

The IPR regime case is even sharper. The orthodox position — enshrined in TRIPS and in India's own intellectual property legislation — is that strong intellectual property rights incentivise innovation by allowing innovators to capture returns from their investments. Arun Maira's 'Intellectual property rights: When it's broken, fix it!' (2016), drawing on Joseph Stiglitz and Boldrin & Levine's 'Against Intellectual Monopoly', made the heterodox argument: "there is considerable evidence that the creation of monopolies for increasing periods and with wider coverage to 'incentivize' innovation does not do so." In the pharmaceutical context — where India's manufacturing capacity is a critical global resource and where drug access for the poor is a life-or-death question — the orthodox IPR framework creates "a means for monopolizing the use of knowledge to accumulate more capital." The vaccine IPR battles of 2021, covered in FF's Batch 6, brought this argument into direct policy relevance.

The "ease of doing business" framework deserves the most sustained critique, because it has dominated Indian regulatory discourse for a decade. The World Bank's EODB rankings have driven Indian policy in ways that are occasionally useful and often distorting. The framework measures the number of regulatory steps, the time to complete them, and the cost. What it does not measure is whether the regulatory environment, once simplified, actually enables the businesses that most need enabling. Arun Maira's 'Want 'Make in India' to succeed? Improve the ease of doing business' (2015) made an argument the EODB framework cannot accommodate: small enterprises — the ones that employ most people and generate most of the dynamism in any economy — bear a disproportionate regulatory burden not because there are too many regulatory steps (though there are), but because "every moment that the owners of small enterprises have to divert to dealing with government functionaries are moments taken away from the functioning of their enterprises." The problem is not just procedural. It is structural — small enterprises have no specialist compliance departments, no lawyers on retainer, no relationships with enforcement officials. The management orthodoxy treats compliance as a cost. For small enterprise owners in India, it is an existential threat to their attention.

**The Entrepreneurial Mindset Beyond Startups**

The policy conversation in India has been captured, to a damaging degree, by the language and priorities of the tech startup ecosystem. Budget 2016's pivot toward "smaller towns and villages, and at manufacturing, social and informal sectors" was noted approvingly by NS Ramnath in 'Mind the gap: Boosting a climate of entrepreneurship' (2016), precisely because it represented a challenge to the narrative that entrepreneurship meant venture-backed tech companies. "While India counts eight unicorns, six of them are domiciled outside India." The unicorn narrative had become self-referential — a story the ecosystem told about itself that bore diminishing relationship to where economic activity actually occurred.

An entrepreneurial lens applied to governance produces different questions than the standard policy questions. The standard policy question is: what regulation should govern this sector? The entrepreneurial question is: what combination of enabling infrastructure, clear rules, and removal of barriers allows the most people to create value? These are not the same question. The enabling infrastructure question — which is what Nandan Nilekani's India Stack work addresses — is fundamentally different from the regulatory compliance question. UPI was not built by asking "how do we regulate digital payments?" It was built by asking "what is the minimum architectural layer that allows anyone to build financial services on top of it?" That is an entrepreneurial question applied to public infrastructure, and it produced something genuinely distinctive.

Applied to the state itself, the entrepreneurial lens points toward what Jaideep Prabhu's work (cited in 'How to make governments smart', 2021) identifies as the key shift: from government as a rule-setter to government as an innovation ecosystem. This requires lateral hiring, genuine learning from private sector practice, and technology deployed in service of specific problems rather than for its own sake. It also requires what Raghuram Rajan, quoted in 'FF Daily #421: The state can do more by trying to do less' (2021), identifies as strategic restraint: "India needs to drop the thick files, and focus more on the thin files. The state can do more by trying to do less." This is, in essence, an entrepreneurial insight about prioritisation and focus applied to the public sector.

The National Monetisation Pipeline case (discussed in FF's Batch 6) illustrates the complexity. Monetisation, as FF noted, is actually harder than privatisation, because it "keeps the government in there as an active player." An entrepreneurial approach would ask whether the government's role as an active participant in these assets creates structural conflicts of interest that undermine the value-creation the pipeline is supposed to enable. The answer, historically and by the evidence of comparable state-as-operator models globally, is yes. The entrepreneurial insight — step back, enable others, capture a share of value rather than trying to capture all of it — is precisely what the bureaucratic instinct resists.

**On Transformation: Process, Not Just Destination**

FF's reporting consistently reveals that governance transformation in India does not follow the linear model of policy reform literature: identify problem → design solution → implement → evaluate → iterate. What actually happens is messier, slower, more politically determined, and more dependent on specific individual agency than any framework comfortably accommodates.

The Aadhaar story is the clearest example. The project took shape under UPA 2, using what NS Ramnath in 'The hyperlocal WhatsApp strategy, policy windows and AI food tasters' (2019) identifies as John Kingdon's "policy windows" — a convergence of problem streams (financial exclusion, leakage from government programmes), policy streams (the biometric identity technology that was mature enough to deploy at scale), and political streams (a government looking for a transformative programme that bypassed the states). The window opened; the architecture was built; the rollout happened faster than the democratic deliberation that should have accompanied it. The Modi government then used it in ways the original designers had not intended, passed it as a Money Bill rather than a full parliamentary debate, and extended it to domains the architecture was not designed for. The transformation is real. It is also contested, legally uncertain, and institutionally incomplete — "poised at a 'Model T' moment," as FF described it in 'India's Model T moment' (2019), but without "the luxury of time for the infrastructure and rules to evolve."

The 1991 reforms tell a similar story of transformation-under-constraint. PV Narasimha Rao's approach — documented by Vinay Sitapati in 'The man who knew tomorrow' (2016) — was not a programme of comprehensive reform. It was a sequence of tactical moves, each defended or obscured from opponents singly, that in aggregate constituted a transformation that no single actor had designed as a whole. This is how major governance change happens in India: not through grand designs but through the accumulation of incremental moves, each contested and compromised, that eventually produce outcomes that were not explicitly chosen by anyone.

The COVID-19 policy response illustrates the failure mode of transformation-by-decree. The lockdown announcement, four hours' notice, nationally applied — produced the migrant labour crisis, the walk-home journeys, the destruction of informal sector livelihoods documented across FF's Batch 4 coverage. The policy logic was not wrong. The implementation logic was catastrophic. "A top-down, centrally-controlled approach won't work — especially in a country as diverse as India that has unique local challenges," as Founding Fuel wrote in 'India is hurting. Why? What next?' (2020). This is not a new insight. But the COVID response demonstrated that the insight had not been institutionalised. The speed of announcement was treated as a virtue. The absence of preparation for the consequences was not.

The mechanism of transformation that FF's evidence supports is neither top-down design nor market spontaneity. It is closer to what Arun Maira repeatedly calls "systems change" — shifts in the underlying architecture of incentives, information flows, and institutional relationships that gradually make different behaviours possible. The UPI transformation worked because it changed the architecture. The Swachh Bharat Mission struggled because changing sanitation behaviour required changing cultural norms, infrastructure, and social conventions simultaneously — and the government's approach, as Amrita Chowdhury noted in 'How Design Thinking can help the Swachh Bharat Mission' (2016), needed to "reframe the problem" and "look at solutions from perspectives of multiple stakeholders" rather than treating sanitation as a hardware installation problem. Transformation requires theory of change. Most Indian policy has programme design without theory of change.

**The Human and Cultural Dimension**

The intellectual framework that FF finds most consistently underrepresented in Indian policy discourse is the human dimension: the motivations, emotions, cultural norms, and social relationships that determine whether policy achieves its intended effects. This is treated as soft by economists, irrelevant by technologists, and too expensive to study carefully by most policymakers. FF treats it as foundational.

Charles Assisi's work is the clearest carrier of this insight across FF's history. His piece on Kerala's anti-alcohol governance — 'The ground beneath India's feet' (2017) — documents what genuine governance-by-nudge looks like: not a decree, but a sustained shift in price signals, social norms, and availability that changed behaviour without prohibition. His piece on the political mind — 'Into the mind of the Indian politician' (2017) — maps the three-circle model of Indian electoral politics: ideologues, aspirationals, and the alienated. Every policy decision is a calculation about which group it affects and how. Understanding this is not cynicism. It is the beginning of realistic policy design.

The engineering college boom story, told by M Rajshekhar in 'An engineering college boom built on a foundation of dysfunction' (2021), is a masterclass in how incentive structures that make sense individually produce collective dysfunction. The colleges were not built to educate. They were built because a college investing Rs 10 crore in a campus would "recover that money in a little over a year." The regulatory framework that was meant to ensure educational quality was captured by the same interests it was meant to regulate. This is not a story about corruption in the simple sense. It is a story about what happens when the formal institutional structure (education regulation) and the informal incentive structure (land value, black money laundering, political connections) are pointing in opposite directions. The formal structure will lose, every time, unless policymakers understand the informal structure.

The data breach story at BigBasket — documented by Charles Assisi in 'Behind the data breach at BigBasket' (2020) — reveals the same gap at the corporate level. The human instinct is to protect reputation rather than disclose harm. The regulatory instinct is to punish disclosure if the company is found to have known about a problem. Together, these incentives produce the worst outcome: delayed disclosure, regulatory confusion, and users who were not protected. "The big question is should we leave such an important decision about user data to the discretion of business leaders?" NS Ramnath asked in 'How to protect data' (2020). The question answers itself, but the answer requires changing the incentive structure, not just lecturing executives about their duties.

The WhatsApp traceability ruling — analysed in 'FF Daily #383: GoI versus WhatsApp' (2021) — illustrates the cultural-political dimension of technology regulation. "Over 100 billion messages pass through the WhatsApp servers every single day." Rahul Matthan described the traceability mandate as technically impossible and a massive civil liberties problem. But the political logic — that WhatsApp had become India's political infrastructure, carrying disinformation, incitement, and electoral manipulation at a scale that traditional media never achieved — was real. The human problem (violent false information leading to mob killings) preceded the regulatory attempt. The regulatory attempt was incompetent. The human problem remains.

Arun Maira's recurring argument about the purpose of economics — "the objective of economics and social progress is to increase people's well-being... Money isn't everything in life," as he wrote in 'Who do economists serve, really?' (2020) — is not a soft observation. It is a challenge to the entire framework of Indian policy evaluation. If welfare, not GDP, is the objective, then the policy instruments are different, the measurements are different, and the political coalitions needed to sustain reform are different. "Economics is too important to be left to economists." India has heard this before. It has not yet institutionalised the alternative.

**The Systems View**

The most consistent analytical commitment in FF's policy coverage is to systems thinking. This is not fashionable complexity theory. It is a specific set of habits: ask about feedback loops, ask about unintended consequences, ask which parts of the system are connected to which other parts, and resist the impulse to solve the visible problem without understanding what solving it will do to the rest of the system.

The jobs crisis is the most important example. Arun Maira's '5C framework' — "a Complicated Condition Created by Combinations of Causes," articulated in 'Solving India's problem of jobless growth' (2016) — is a systems argument: jobless growth is not a single problem with a single solution. It is the emergent property of an economic system in which capital productivity has improved dramatically, labour intensity has fallen, education outcomes have not matched labour market demands, the informal sector has been structurally excluded from the formal growth story, and demographic pressure is accelerating faster than job creation. Intervene in any one of these without understanding the others and you will make the system worse. India's employment elasticity fell from 0.44 in 1999–2004 to 0.01 in 2004–2009 — "close to jobless growth" — during a period of the highest GDP growth in India's history. GDP was the signal everyone was watching. The jobs number was the signal that mattered.

The agriculture-water-climate nexus is another systems story that FF's coverage traces carefully. As documented in FF's Batch 5, 85 percent of irrigated Indian agriculture depends on groundwater. India extracts 230 cubic kilometres per year, 90 percent for irrigation. World Bank estimates that by 2030, 65 percent of Indian groundwater will be over-exploited. The policy response — MSP subsidies for rice and wheat — directly incentivises the crops that are most water-intensive. "Every year, more than a third of the food stocks, mostly rice and maize, in the Food Corporation of India godowns continue to rot." The subsidy creates overproduction. The overproduction goes to waste. The farming practice it incentivises depletes the water table. The depletion will eventually make farming in the most productive agricultural regions impossible. This is a system. The MSP policy was designed without understanding it.

The India Stack story is a systems story of a different kind — a deliberate architectural intervention that was designed as a platform rather than a programme. The key design insight, articulated by Nandan Nilekani and repeatedly referenced in FF's coverage, is what he calls the "inversion of data": from data used to sell things to users, to data that empowers users. The Aadhaar-UPI-Account Aggregator stack was designed on this principle. The "federated data structure" — the bank account knows the Aadhaar number, but the Aadhaar number does not know the bank account — was "deliberately created to avoid profiling and mega data stores," as RS Sharma explained in "'Exclusion does not come from Aadhaar, but from its uses'" (2019). This is a systems design choice embedded in the architecture. It prevents a specific failure mode (centralised profiling) by making it technically impossible rather than just legally prohibited. Understanding the difference between technical impossibility and legal prohibition is essential to understanding why some governance interventions work and others don't.

The telecom duopoly story is a systems warning that has not yet become a crisis, but the structure is visible. As Haresh Chawla noted in 'Trendspotting: 2020 can make or break' (2019), India was heading toward "just two-and-a-half private telecom players." The regulatory decisions that produced this consolidation — spectrum allocation, interconnect pricing, the competitive dynamics that drove Vodafone-Idea to the edge of viability — were each made individually, without a model of what the system would look like when they all interacted. India now has the cheapest mobile data in the world and an industry structure that has no slack for further price reduction. The system produced a benefit and a fragility simultaneously. Policy is only just beginning to grapple with what the fragility means.

The geopolitical systems story is perhaps the most complex. India's strategic autonomy — its ability to maintain independent relationships with the US, Russia, China, and the Gulf simultaneously — is a structural achievement that most medium powers cannot replicate. But as Vivek Y Kelkar documented in 'The US–Israel War on Iran: The Risks to India's Strategic Hedging' (2026), it contains a paradox: "the very region that enables India's strategic autonomy is also the one most capable of constraining it when instability erupts." India's trade dependence on the Gulf — its largest trading partner bloc — and its energy dependence on Russian oil and Iranian gas mean that the strategic autonomy is real but contingent. The system that enables it also constrains it. "Autonomy today is less about refusal and more about calibration," as Kelkar wrote in 'India's Trade Deals and the New Meaning of Strategic Autonomy' (2026).

**The Evolution of FF's Thinking**

The earliest FF coverage of governance and regulation — from 2015 and 2016 — was dominated by two concerns: the jobs crisis and the institutional architecture of economic reform. The framing was primarily economic and structural. Arun Maira's pieces on employment elasticity, manufacturing, and the Make in India programme set the register: these are systemic problems that require systemic solutions, and the Indian state is not currently designed to deliver them.

By 2017–2018, the primary frame had shifted to data and digital governance. The Aadhaar debate consumed an extraordinary amount of intellectual energy at FF, and rightly so — it was the most important governance question of the period. But the quality of FF's engagement evolved during this period in a specific way. The early pieces were trying to find common ground in a polarised debate. The middle period — the Aadhaar Briefing Notes, the privacy pieces, Rahul Matthan's accountability framework arguments — became more technically sophisticated and more willing to identify where the utilitarian defence of Aadhaar was inadequate. The argument that "the government had no other choice but to rush through its implementation, and that one shouldn't let the opponents scuttle a good scheme through the regular democratic processes" — identified in 'Midday meals and Aadhaar: The debate reveals how to make the system robust' (2017) — was treated charitably early on and more critically later.

By 2019–2020, the state capacity argument had become central. The Tabarrok-Rajagopalan paper on premature imitation gave FF a conceptual anchor for an argument that had been building through multiple pieces: India's governance problems are not primarily about bad design or bad intent. They are about a state apparatus whose implementation capacity does not match its regulatory ambitions. The COVID-19 period sharpened this argument to a point it had not previously reached. The migrant labour crisis, the lockdown, the Aarogya Setu contact tracing app — all became evidence for the same thesis. "The deck explains why the most important factors in fighting the [COVID] app are in fact outside the app," Ramnath wrote in 'Aarogya Setu: Why tech is not the most important problem — or the solution' (2020).

Between 2020 and 2022, FF's thinking on Big Tech regulation became more settled. The early coverage had been genuinely open about whether platform companies' business models were compatible with user welfare. By 2022, the accumulated evidence — Facebook's Cambridge Analytica exposure, the Equifax breach, the algorithmic amplification of political extremism — had shifted the analysis. The argument in 'Senator, we sell bads' (2018) — "You can't serve two masters. And the person who doesn't pay you, can't be your master" — had become not just an observation but a prediction: platforms optimised for engagement will consistently undermine the civic infrastructure of the societies they operate in. The Brussels Effect argument, developed by G Venkat Raman in the State vs Big Tech series (2021), gave FF a framework for thinking about how regulation actually globalises — not through coordination or agreement, but through market size and regulatory will.

The most recent period — 2023 to 2025 — has seen FF's thinking on AI governance evolve rapidly. The early AI coverage was curious and occasionally alarmist. The Sundeep Waslekar pieces on 'The Global Politics of AI' (2025) and 'India's AI Gambit' (2025) represent a more settled position: AI governance is critically underprepared; India's AI policy is made by the wrong departments for the wrong purposes; the existential risks are being dismissed by precisely the policymakers who most need to take them seriously. The AutoML-Zero paradigm — AI that creates its own algorithms through trial and error — represents a qualitative shift that current governance frameworks were not designed to address. FF is now tracking this as the next major governance failure in preparation.

One position FF has genuinely updated is the relationship between digital infrastructure and inclusion. The early coverage celebrated India Stack as an unambiguous inclusion win. By 2019–2020, the evidence of Aadhaar-linked exclusions — midday meal denials, pension failures, ghost beneficiaries — had introduced genuine qualification. The argument became more carefully conditional: the architecture is sound, but the uses of the architecture require a legal and institutional framework that does not yet exist. This is a more honest position than either the cheerleading or the condemnation, and it represents real intellectual progress.

**Key Voices**

Arun Maira is the dominant intellectual presence in FF's governance and regulation coverage, particularly across the 2015–2022 period. His distinctive contribution is threefold: a systems thinking framework that he applies with unusual consistency, a moral economy argument that measures policy against human well-being rather than GDP, and a long experience in the Planning Commission and in global institutions (McKinsey, ILO, Planning Commission, WEF advisory bodies) that gives his structural arguments biographical grounding. His sharpest pieces include 'Solving India's problem of jobless growth' (2016), 'Capitalism vs democracy: Consumers vs citizens' (2018), 'The used and discarded workers of India' (2020), 'India@100: A vision for transformative change' (2022), and 'Power to the people' (2021). His limitation — and FF does not always name this explicitly — is that his prescriptions sometimes remain at a level of systemic aspiration that does not fully engage with the political economy obstacles to implementation. The diagnosis is consistently excellent. The pathway from diagnosis to change is sometimes underspecified.

NS Ramnath is FF's primary analytical voice on technology and regulation, and his contributions span the full range from granular case analysis to large-scale structural argument. His Aadhaar briefing notes (2017) remain the most rigorous decomposition of that debate in Indian media. His work on platform economics — from the ICICI-PhonePe standoff (2017) to the Facebook structural critique (2018) to the State vs Big Tech framing (2021) — consistently identifies the right level of abstraction: not "is this platform good or bad" but "what structural feature of this platform's business model produces this outcome?" His work on technology and governance — 'Technology is politics' (2019), 'Aarogya Setu: Why tech is not the most important problem — or the solution' (2020) — applies the Law of Amplification with rigour. He is at his sharpest when he is disaggregating consensus positions that have become too comfortable.

Haresh Chawla brings a practitioner's eye to structural economic analysis with an unusual willingness to name failures directly. His 'Trendspotting' series is remarkable for the consistency of its argument over nearly a decade: India's platform economy is producing market capitalisation without economic substance, and the country is "mistaking a market-cap cycle for national progress," as he wrote in 'Trendspotting 2026' (2026). His 'India Cannot App Its Way to Prosperity' argument — which runs from the e-commerce FDI piece (2016) through to the 2026 Trendspotting — is FF's most sustained challenge to the tech-entrepreneur narrative of Indian economic development. His sharpest pieces also include 'The thinking Indian's guide to Aadhaar' (2018) and 'The Uber-Didi deal is more about China than about Uber and Didi' (2016).

Indrajit Gupta functions as FF's conscience on corporate governance. His consistent argument — that board failure is not an aberration but a structural feature of Indian corporate governance — is documented through case after case: Tata-Mistry, Chanda Kochhar, NSE yogi scandal, Paytm. His pieces on UDAN ('Why UDAN is flying into headwinds', 2019) and infrastructure governance ('Plugging the infra gaps', 2022) apply the same forensic approach to public sector governance. His AI 171 series (2025) demonstrates the same qualities in a new domain: aviation safety governance as a test of institutional capability and transparency.

Sundeep Waslekar's contribution is geopolitical and civilisational in its scope. His quarterly reviews of global order — the world dividing into camps, the triple crisis of institutions/morality/technology, the AI governance failure — provide FF with a long-horizon frame that complements the more immediate policy analysis. His AI pieces — 'Oppenheimer's Lesson for the Age of AI' (2025), 'The Global Politics of AI' (2025), 'Revolution Inside AI' (2025) — are the most technically rigorous non-specialist AI governance writing in FF's catalogue.

Vijay Kelkar and Ajay Shah, through their book 'In Service of the Republic', function as the theoretical anchor for FF's state capacity argument across Batches 4 through 8. Their four-variable framework for assessing state capacity — number of transactions, level of discretion, stakes for private persons, degree of secrecy — is cited in almost every major policy design discussion in FF's recent coverage. Their critique of criminal sanctions in economic law (Batch 8) and the "cult of jald baazi" (Batch 4) represent the strongest institutional economics arguments in FF's evidence base.

Vivek Y Kelkar has become FF's primary voice on strategic and geopolitical analysis in the most recent period. His work on the post-American world order — 'A World Without Endgames' (2026), 'India's Trade Deals and the New Meaning of Strategic Autonomy' (2026), 'The US–Israel War on Iran' (2026) — combines structural realism with a practitioner's attention to the constraints India actually faces. He is notable for his willingness to identify where India's strategic autonomy rhetoric exceeds its strategic capacity — a distinction that most commentary on India's foreign policy is reluctant to draw.

**What FF Is Skeptical Of**

FF is skeptical of techno-utopianism in any form — the recurring belief that the next digital infrastructure, the next platform, the next algorithmic intervention will solve governance problems that are fundamentally about institutions, incentives, and human behaviour. This skepticism is not anti-technology. It is consistently grounded in Kentaro Toyama's Law of Amplification: "technology's primary effect is to amplify human forces." A contact tracing app deployed by a state with inadequate public health infrastructure amplifies inadequate public health infrastructure. A digital payments system deployed into an economy where 85–90 percent of employment is informal amplifies the informal economy's exclusion from formal financial services, unless the architecture is specifically designed to overcome that exclusion.

FF is skeptical of the utilitarian argument that good outcomes justify bypassing democratic process. This argument appears most explicitly in the Aadhaar debate, where supporters argued that the scheme's genuine benefits for the poor justified the speed of implementation and the use of a Money Bill to avoid full parliamentary debate. NS Ramnath identified this in 'Midday meals and Aadhaar: The debate reveals how to make the system robust' (2017) and returned to it repeatedly. The problem is not just procedural. When democratic checks are bypassed, the feedback mechanisms that reveal implementation failures are also bypassed. The exclusions caused by Aadhaar biometric failures — documented in FF's coverage and in the Supreme Court proceedings — were precisely the kind of consequence that democratic deliberation, slower and messier, might have anticipated and designed against.

FF is skeptical of India's demographic dividend triumphalism. "There is much chest-thumping in some policy making circles about India's 'demographic dividend' overtaking China," as noted in 'FF Insights #708: China's demographic disaster' (2022). The demographic dividend is conditional, not structural. It requires that the working-age population entering the labour force has education and skills that match available employment, and that the economy creates sufficient formal employment to absorb them. On current trajectories, India's demographic dividend will become a demographic liability. Employment elasticity of growth near zero, 42 percent of organised manufacturing on contract, the educated unemployed competing for government jobs — the Bihar-UP railway job riots, identified in 'FF Insights #579: Read data the right way' (2022) as "India's first large-scale unemployment riots" — these are not the symptoms of a dividend being collected. They are the symptoms of a dividend being wasted.

FF is skeptical of GDP as a measurement framework for Indian policy purposes. This runs from Arun Maira's earliest pieces through to the most recent trendspotting analyses by Haresh Chawla. "The pursuit of numbers, in the belief that numbers alone indicate accuracy, has become the bane of economics," Maira wrote in 'Who will robots and elephants vote for' (2018). India's growth numbers coexist with the lowest employment elasticity of growth in the world, with annual income of the poorest 20 percent of households plunging 53 percent in 2020–21, with heat stress that could cost India 4.5 percent of GDP by 2030. The number is not wrong. The number is insufficient. A policy framework built on GDP growth as the primary objective will consistently produce growth that does not improve the lives of the people who most need their lives improved.

FF is skeptical of the "regulator doesn't understand innovation" narrative whenever it is deployed by companies with their own compliance problems. The Paytm coverage is the clearest case, but the pattern appears across the fintech series: companies that have benefited from regulatory forbearance and then complain about regulatory scrutiny when their practices attract attention. As NS Ramnath noted in the fintech special reports (2023), "techies have to work a lot more with fuzzies before the benefits reach people at large." The innovation-versus-regulation framing treats compliance as an obstacle to service delivery. The evidence from India's fintech sector suggests that inadequate compliance creates service delivery failures that harm the users the companies claim to serve.

**What FF Celebrates**

FF celebrates the India Stack architecture — Aadhaar, UPI, eKYC, DigiLocker, Account Aggregator — as a genuine policy achievement and a globally distinctive model of digital public infrastructure. This celebration is qualified — as documented throughout the Aadhaar coverage — but it is real. The design choices embedded in India Stack represent an attempt to solve a specific problem (500 million people without usable identity documents, excluded from formal financial services) through a platform architecture rather than a programme architecture. The results are documented: "Between 2014 and 2017 account ownership in India rose by more than 30 percentage points among women as well as among adults in the poorest 40 percent of households," as NS Ramnath noted in 'Lessons from India's tortuous path to make tech work for the people' (2020). Google sent a note to the US Federal Reserve urging it to implement a payment platform similar to UPI. Morocco built its identity system on an open-source platform that emerged out of India. These are genuine achievements, and FF names them as such.

FF celebrates the rare instances of genuine institutional honesty and independence. The RBI memoir excerpt celebrated in 'Not the government's cheerleader' (2024) — Duvvuri Subbarao's account of refusing to subordinate monetary policy to electoral cycles — is held up as a model of what institutional independence looks like under pressure. The Mental Healthcare Bill of 2012, whose "advance directive" provision Keshav Desiraju drafted personally, is described in FF's coverage as genuinely "pathbreaking" — a rare case of legislation designed around the dignity and autonomy of the most marginalised citizens rather than the convenience of the state.

FF celebrates the transparency-as-governance insight from Uganda — publishing fund flows in newspapers raised school receipt rates from 13 percent to over 90 percent, as NS Ramnath documented in 'How Uganda fixed leakages with transparency, and why it matters to India' (2017). The principle is applicable at every level of Indian governance, but it requires a state that is willing to make its own failures visible. The rare Indian institutional experiments that have done this — the Right to Information Act, the MNREGA social audit mechanism, the National Data Analytics Platform — are treated by FF as genuine achievements, however unevenly implemented.

FF celebrates practitioners and institutions that hold the state to account without ideological posturing. Vinita Gursahani Singh's constitutional literacy work — "facilitating hundreds of workshops and trainings, mostly in rural India, and mostly with people from marginalised communities," as described in 'We, the People of India' (2025) — represents a form of civic work that FF consistently values: building democratic capacity at the ground level, outside the elite policy discourse, in the languages and contexts where most Indians actually live.

**The Historical Lens**

History does specific work in FF's policy analysis. It is not deployed as context-setting or as illustration of continuity. It is deployed as evidence — evidence that the present is less exceptional than it believes itself to be, that the institutions being reformed have histories that constrain what reform is possible, and that the failure modes of current policy are recognisably similar to failure modes that India has encountered before.

The 1991 reform story is the most important historical lens in FF's coverage. The standard narrative — Manmohan Singh, the budget, the bold liberalisation — is consistently complicated by the Narasimha Rao pieces. Rao's achievement was not the IMF-mandated stabilisation package. It was the political management of reform in conditions of extreme political fragility: minority government, party that didn't support him, no popular mandate, enemies who had to be defeated singly. The insight this produces is not that reform requires political strength. It is that reform requires political intelligence — the ability to sequence, to manage opponents, to move fast enough that opposition cannot coalesce before facts on the ground have shifted. This intelligence is rare. It is not produced by good policy design alone.

The industrial policy history is equally important. The 1960s policies that shaped India's electronics industry — traced by N Dayasindhu in 'Why Indian technology projects haven't made global impact' (2020) — were not irrational. They were rational responses to the conditions of the time: import substitution, forex conservation, technology transfer requirements. They produced an industry that was structurally dependent on the state and incapable of competing internationally. By FY 2017, electronics imports were almost twice the size of the domestic electronics industry. The historical path dependency is not an accident. It is the compounding consequence of policy choices made in the 1960s that were never systematically reversed.

The pandemic history — traced by Chinmay Tumbe in 'The Covid lessons India failed to learn from its own history' (2021) — reveals a pattern of collective amnesia that recurs across Indian governance domains. "The collective memory of pandemics that had ripped India — in 1920 life expectancy was in the 20s — that entire memory had been lost." The 1918 influenza pandemic killed between 12 and 17 million Indians. The policy lessons — surveillance, stockpiling, community health systems, the relationship between malnutrition and pandemic vulnerability — were not institutionalised. A century later, India faced COVID-19 with institutional gaps that the pandemic of 1918 could have predicted.

The George Fernandes/IBM-Coke comparison to the TikTok ban — deployed by NS Ramnath in 'The political games: IBM, Coke & TikTok' (2020) — is a specific historical use: a precedent that illuminates the political logic of economic nationalism. Fernandes drove IBM and Coca-Cola out of India in 1977. IBM returned in 1992 under the same economic pressures that the 1991 reforms were responding to. The pattern — nationalist exclusion followed by economic reality forcing reintegration — is a recurring one in Indian regulatory history. Whether the TikTok ban follows the same arc depends on variables that the historical analogy illuminates but cannot resolve.

The Harshad Mehta replacement cost theory story — traced by FF in 'FF Daily #536: Stock valuations and lessons from Harshad Mehta' (2021) to a 1956 Rajya Sabha debate — punctures the myth of individual financial genius and reveals something more disturbing: the same valuation frameworks that produce market bubbles have been cycling through Indian financial markets for decades. The institutional memory that might prevent their recurrence is consistently absent.

**Unresolved Tensions**

The deepest unresolved tension in FF's policy coverage is between the legitimate case for state-led development and the accumulated evidence of state capacity failure. This tension cannot be resolved by choosing a side. Both claims are true. India's economic achievements since 1947 — poverty reduction, universal suffrage, the Green Revolution, space programme, nuclear capability, and the India Stack — required state direction and state investment. India's chronic failures — healthcare, education, formal employment, infrastructure — are also primarily failures of the state to deliver on commitments it made and programmes it designed. The question is not whether the state should act. The question is what kinds of action it can reliably execute, and what the political conditions are under which it executes them well. FF has the diagnosis. The prescription remains contested.

The second unresolved tension is between speed and democratic deliberation. India's development challenges are genuinely urgent. The demographic dividend window is not infinite. The 280 million people who will enter the jobs market by 2050, documented in Arun Maira's 'Fixing India's jobs machine' (2016), cannot wait for decade-long policy deliberations. And yet the evidence from Aadhaar, from demonetisation, from the COVID lockdown, from the farm laws — all suggests that speed without deliberation produces implementation failures that harm the people the policy is meant to serve. FF holds this tension honestly. It does not resolve it, because it cannot be resolved. The best governance is fast enough to matter and slow enough to deliberate. Getting that calibration right is the work of a generation.

The third tension is about Big Tech and digital infrastructure. FF celebrates India Stack as a public good alternative to platform capitalism. But FF also documents that the line between public infrastructure and private platform is not stable. The data that Aadhaar enables collecting, the financial flows that UPI makes visible, the behavioural patterns that DigiLocker and Account Aggregator create — all of this is potentially available to a future government that has different values about how state power should relate to individual privacy. The architecture is designed against centralised profiling now. A different political context could change those design choices. FF acknowledges this concern but does not resolve it, because it is genuinely unresolvable in advance. It requires vigilance, not a solution.

The fourth tension is about India's global positioning. FF consistently argues that India should be a "rule shaper, not merely a rule taker" — in AI governance, in digital infrastructure standards, in the post-American world order. But FF also documents that India's institutional capacity to shape global rules is limited. India "lacks the funding to push its ideas beyond a certain point," as Pramit Pal Chaudhuri noted in 'Is India too late to the Global South party?' (2023). India's data sovereignty is "structurally compromised" — DigiLocker is on a foreign cloud service, as noted in 'India's AI Gambit' (2025). The aspiration to global rule-shaping and the domestic reality of infrastructure dependence on the same powers India wants to negotiate with are in genuine tension.

**The Missing Voices**

The most significant absence in FF's governance coverage is sustained, granular reporting from India's rural poor — the people who are most affected by governance failure and least represented in the discourse about it. FF covers them as subjects of analysis: the migrant workers, the smallholder farmers, the informal sector workers, the beneficiaries (or victims) of Aadhaar. It rarely covers them as voices — as people with specific analyses of the systems that govern their lives. This is partly a structural limitation of English-language journalism with an educated urban audience. But it means that the governance analysis is systematically constructed from the outside looking in, even when it is sympathetic. Piyul Mukherjee's piece on Indore's informal workers ('Despatches from Indore: The quest for a secure job in the formal sector', 2020) and the constitutional literacy work of Vinita Gursahani Singh point toward a more grounded approach, but they remain exceptional.

District and state-level administrators — the IAS officers, the district collectors, the block development officers who actually implement national policy — are also largely absent. They appear occasionally as unnamed sources or as the implied targets of policy recommendations. The lived experience of trying to implement contradictory, underfunded, politically pressured programmes with inadequate staff in highly diverse local contexts is not well-represented. This absence has consequences: it makes implementation failure seem more like incompetence and less like the predictable result of systems that were never designed to succeed.

The voices of India's 1.3 billion non-urban residents are under-represented in the AI governance coverage. The AI debate in FF — sophisticated, well-sourced, genuinely rigorous — is a conversation among a small global elite about what AI should do and how it should be governed. The people who will most viscerally experience both the benefits and the harms — the farmers who will use AI-assisted pest detection, the workers who will be managed by algorithmic systems they cannot appeal, the children whose educational futures will be shaped by data systems they did not consent to — are almost entirely absent from the discourse.

India's northeast, its coastal fishing communities, its urban slum populations, its Adivasi communities — these are not constituencies that appear with regularity in FF's governance coverage, except occasionally as abstract subjects of policy. The governance failures that most directly shape their lives — forest rights, coastal fishing regulations, urban land tenure, tribal self-governance — receive less sustained analytical attention than fintech regulation or platform competition policy.

**What This Means For You**

There is one belief you are probably carrying into every governance encounter — every policy design conversation, every regulatory compliance decision, every engagement with state institutions — that the evidence says is wrong. The belief is that the primary variable in governance outcomes is the quality of the policy design or the quality of the people running the institution. If only the regulation were clearer. If only the minister were more competent. If only the board were more independent. The evidence accumulated across a decade of FF's reporting points consistently elsewhere: the primary variable is state capacity — the institutional muscle to implement consistently, fairly, and at scale. A well-designed regulation deployed through an institution without the staffing, data, and incentive structures to enforce it will produce worse outcomes than a modestly designed regulation that can actually be implemented. Update your model from "what is the right policy?" to "what can this institution actually execute?" Those are different questions, and they produce different answers.

Stop presenting fully designed solutions to state institutions and expecting them to implement. This is the most common form of productive-looking work that produces no change. Design thinking workshops for government agencies. McKinsey frameworks deployed in planning sessions. Pilot programmes that work in three districts and are then assumed to be ready for national rollout. India is, as Kiran Karnik documented in 'Purpose-Driven Networks' (2019), "a classic case of 'graveyard of successful pilots.'" The pilot proves the concept works. It does not prove the institution can scale it. Stop stopping at the pilot. Or stop doing pilots without an explicit theory of how the institutional capacity to scale will be built.

Start mapping the incentive structures before you map the intervention. The engineering college boom, the MSP rice-and-water paradox, the Aadhaar biometric exclusions, the Angel Tax harassment — these are not failures of intent. They are predictable outcomes of systems with misaligned incentives. Before any new policy intervention, regulatory proposal, or governance reform, ask the Charlie Munger question that FF deploys in 'WFH Daily #191: The power of incentives' (2020): "If you have been busy solving some organisational problem, how much time have you spent analysing if the right incentives are in place?" Then ask the Kelkar-Shah question: how many transactions does this implementation require, how much discretion does it leave to enforcement officials, what are the stakes for private persons, and what is the degree of secrecy? These are the variables that determine whether a policy will produce what it promises or something worse. They are not typically on the agenda of any design conversation FF has ever reported on. Put them there first.

The conviction to carry out of this page is not that India's governance is broken — that is a description, not an insight. The conviction is this: every governance failure in India is a system that is working exactly as designed, given the incentives actually in place. Change the system; don't blame the people operating within it.
