# Consumer Behaviour & Retail

---

## Part 1 — Opening

Here is what is actually happening in Indian retail right now: companies are building for a consumer they have never met. The metro, English-speaking, digitally fluent, aspirationally western customer — that is the person most product teams, most marketing briefs, and most investor decks are written for. The other 800 million people are a rounding error, a future opportunity, a Bharat slide at the back of the deck.

This is not a diversity failure. It is a strategic one. The consumer who buys on Cash on Delivery because payment-before-delivery feels like a leap of faith is not irrational — they are carrying offline trust logic into an online context. The blue-collar worker who did not know they had a PF account is not financially illiterate — they were never told, by anyone, in a language they understood. The small-town woman making TikTok videos in shorts while her in-laws watch is not a contradiction to be resolved — she is the market signal that FMCG brand managers missed for three years running.

Conventional consumer behaviour frameworks — built in US business schools, tested in Chinese e-commerce, canonised in HBR case studies — treat India as a version of somewhere else with lower prices. They are wrong in ways that cost companies real money. Price sensitivity is real but it is not the whole story. Experience matters but it reaches Jharsuguda differently than it reaches Juhu. Trust is a prerequisite in Middle India, not a nice-to-have. And the stated reason a consumer gives you for their decision is almost never the real one.

This page is built from a decade of Founding Fuel reporting on how Indian consumers actually behave — not how they are supposed to. It will not confirm what your research deck already says.

## Part 2 — Principles

---

### Principle 1

> The reason a consumer gives you for their decision is not the reason they made it.

**What This Means & Why**
People construct post-hoc explanations for choices that were driven by fear, guilt, pride, or the need for control — and they believe those explanations sincerely. This is not deception; it is how human cognition works. Most consumer research fails because it asks people to explain decisions they cannot accurately introspect on. Surveys confirm the story people tell about themselves. Ethnography finds what is actually going on.

**The India Reality**
In India, this gap is wider because stated motives carry additional social weight — what you say you bought, and why, is part of identity performance in a way that is especially pronounced in joint-family and community contexts. The Prius buyer globally signals prosocial status; the Patanjali buyer in India signals cultural allegiance and generational loyalty simultaneously, which no survey will surface cleanly.

**The Evidence**
In 'Why Emotions Are First Principles in Product Strategy' (2025), Shrinath V describes SilverGenie's discovery that the actual buyer of elder care services was not the elderly parent but the adult child — and the emotion driving purchase was not care but guilt. The parent wanted company and independence; the child wanted relief from inadequacy. A product designed around the stated need (elder wellness) would have missed the real purchase trigger entirely. Once SilverGenie built for guilt-relief, not health monitoring, the model worked.

**The Implication**
In your next consumer research sprint, add one question to every interview: "Tell me about the last time you almost bought this but didn't" — the hesitation reveals the real driver faster than any stated preference.

### Principle 2

> Bharat does not adopt technology — it improvises it into something you did not design.

**What This Means & Why**
The dominant mental model of technology adoption treats users as the receiving end of product decisions made upstream. In non-metro India, users routinely reinvent the use case entirely — building on top of infrastructure that was designed for something else. This is not workaround behaviour; it is original innovation. The companies that understand this treat their product as a platform for improvisation, not a finished solution.

**The India Reality**
A sugarcane juice vendor with a QR code, a tailor taking orders via WhatsApp voice notes, CRBTs as the first digital self-expression tool for first-time mobile users in a world where apps were English-first — these are not edge cases. They are the norm in a market where 800 million people encountered digital infrastructure with no prior frame of reference and invented their own rules for it.

**The Evidence**
In 'From Bazaar to Breakthrough' (2025), Shrinath V documents how Cash on Delivery — widely treated as a logistics workaround — was actually a trust mechanism. Users who had no history with online commerce needed a way to restore the sense of control that physical market transactions provided. COD was not an inferior payment method; it was a bridge behaviour carrying offline trust logic into a new context. Companies that eliminated COD to clean up their balance sheets lost customers who were not ready to make that leap.

**The Implication**
Before you deprecate any product feature that seems irrational or operationally inconvenient, spend two weeks understanding which offline trust behaviour it is substituting for.

### Principle 3

> Trust is not a brand outcome in Middle India — it is the entry requirement.

**What This Means & Why**
In metro consumer markets, trust is something you build over time through brand consistency, reviews, and repeat purchase. In Middle India, trust must be established before the first transaction — and it cannot be established through advertising alone. Without a human interface, a vernacular presence, or a community endorser, a new product or service simply does not exist for these consumers, no matter how good the unit economics look on a spreadsheet.

**The India Reality**
Middle India consumers are often encountering organised retail, digital payments, or formal financial services for a second time — after being burned, ignored, or left confused by the first. Blue-collar workers had PF accounts they did not know about, because no one told them in a language or format they could understand. That is not a literacy problem; that is a design failure that destroyed institutional trust for a generation.

**The Evidence**
In 'The Middle India consumer' (2022), Founding Fuel reports that Smartstaff's single most retentive product feature was not their core staffing service but PF balance visibility — showing workers, for the first time, that their pension contributions existed and were accumulating. This became what the article calls "a sticky feature from a trust perspective." Razorpay, in the same report, built trust with small business owners not through product features but through useful content that made them a reliable voice before any sales conversation began. Meesho deployed women entrepreneurs as local trust nodes, using existing community credibility to introduce platform technology.

**The Implication**
Before launching any product in a Tier 3 or Tier 4 market, map the existing trust architecture — who do these consumers already believe, and how do you get that person to stand behind what you are selling?

### Principle 4

> Physical retail is not dying — retailers who did not understand why they existed are dying.

**What This Means & Why**
The e-commerce kills retail narrative confuses correlation with causation. What e-commerce killed was undifferentiated physical retail — stores that competed on price and selection without offering anything a screen could not. Physical retail that delivers experience, curation, community, or sensory engagement is not threatened by e-commerce; it is protected by it, because those things cannot be replicated on a screen.

**The India Reality**
Zudio's expansion into Jharsuguda, Dimapur, Imphal, Pathankot, and Gorakhpur — towns that organised retail had written off — demonstrates that the opportunity in physical retail is not exhausted but misdirected. The consumer in these markets wants the tactile, social, experiential dimension of shopping that e-commerce cannot offer. What was missing was not demand — it was supply designed for actual price points and cultural contexts.

**The Evidence**
In 'Noel Tata's brahmastra' (2023), Indrajit Gupta describes Zudio's model as a deliberate inversion of conventional retail logic — low markup, high inventory turns, fast fashion cycles at price points accessible to the Middle India consumer. Gupta invokes GP Cachon's Wharton typology of "Turns Retailers" to place Zudio in a category that prizes velocity over margin. The result: 120+ locations and expanding, in markets that premium retail had abandoned as unviable. The article also cites MINISO's founder: "I firmly believe that it is the physical economy that will dominate the future as long as the exposed problems can be solved."

**The Implication**
If your physical retail strategy is built around competing with e-commerce on price or selection, stop — redesign around the one thing your store can do that a screen cannot, and make that the reason to visit.

### Principle 5

> Selling on price is a trap you cannot escape once you enter it.

**What This Means & Why**
Price-based competition creates customer relationships that exist only as long as you are the cheapest option. The moment a competitor undercuts you — and someone always does — you have no equity to fall back on. The companies that survive margin compression are those that have built something customers value beyond the transaction: an experience, a community, a story, an identity. Price is what you compete on when you have nothing else.

**The India Reality**
India's consumer market is price-sensitive in ways that are structurally reinforced — import duties, per-capita income distribution, joint-family purchasing decisions with multiple veto points. This makes the price trap especially seductive and especially dangerous. The iPhone SE is a case study in how price sensitivity can be misread: Indian consumers in 2016 did not want a smaller iPhone, they wanted a cheaper one — and the SE's form factor gave them neither what they actually valued nor a price that competed with Android alternatives.

**The Evidence**
In 'FF Daily #463: The price game' (2021), Founding Fuel cites Simon Sinek's framing directly: "Selling based on price is like heroin. The short-term gain is fantastic, but the more you do it, the harder it becomes to kick the habit." The article frames price competition as an addiction that erodes the capacity to build any other kind of competitive advantage — and names it as a structural trap, not a tactical choice.

**The Implication**
Audit your last three pricing decisions: if more than one of them was driven by matching a competitor rather than reflecting the value you deliver, you are already in the trap — identify one non-price reason a customer should choose you and make it visible this quarter.

### Principle 6

> Comfort and nostalgia are not soft marketing levers — they are hard economic forces during crises.

**What This Means & Why**
In downturns, consumers do not simply cut spending — they restructure it. Mass-market comfort brands surge while premium-artisanal challengers stall. Small indulgences — cosmetics, familiar food brands, nostalgic products — spike because they deliver emotional regulation at low cost. Companies that understand this can predict which parts of their portfolio will grow in a recession; companies that do not are surprised every time.

**The India Reality**
India's food economy runs on ghar ka khana as an emotional reference point, not just a consumption behaviour. The nostalgic value of home cooking, as articulated by Indian consumers, surfaces most sharply when people move away from home — and food delivery platforms that understand this (positioning themselves as a substitute for that emotional category, not just a logistics service) have a fundamentally different relationship with their users than those that do not.

**The Evidence**
In 'The mascara effect' (2020), Charles Assisi extends Leonard Lauder's original insight — that beauty purchases rise in downturns as low-cost indulgence — into the Covid moment, noting that comfort brands surged across categories while small-batch premium products that had been gaining share retrenched sharply. Assisi anchors the argument in the Yad Vashem documentation of beauty rituals maintained in Holocaust concentration camps — establishing that the drive to maintain dignity and small pleasure under duress is not a consumer trend but a human constant. The 'On the menu: The changing attitude towards home cooking' (2020) piece by Founding Fuel captures the same force in an Indian key: "Ghar ka khana... is made with emotions. It is unconditional, and in some sense it's free. Bahar ka khana is commerce."

**The Implication**
In your next planning cycle, build a recession scenario explicitly — identify which SKUs or services in your portfolio carry comfort or nostalgia equity, and ensure they have enough margin and distribution to absorb the volume surge when it comes.

### Principle 7

> Behavioural science is not an academic curiosity — it is already in your competitor's product design.

**What This Means & Why**
Loss aversion, the decoy effect, scarcity signalling, expert endorsement, conformity bias, and fear of regret are not concepts for a consumer psychology seminar. They are the design logic behind Zara's inventory model, Sensodyne's advertising, OnePlus's invite-only launch, The Economist's subscription tiers, and Amazon's review architecture. The companies deploying these tools deliberately are not being manipulative — they are being precise about how decisions are actually made.

**The India Reality**
Indian FMCG and retail companies have been slower to systematically embed behavioural science into product and pricing design than their global counterparts, despite operating in a market where social proof (family endorsement, community conformity) and authority bias (doctor recommendations, celebrity yoga teachers) are structurally more powerful than in individualist markets.

**The Evidence**
In 'How Zara, Sensodyne and others use behavioural science to unlock value' (2015), Founding Fuel maps the deliberate deployment of behavioural principles: Zara's scarcity model exploits loss aversion — items disappear fast, so you buy now. Sensodyne's white-coat advertising exploits expert bias. OnePlus's invite system exploits scarcity and conformity simultaneously. The article frames this not as clever marketing but as systematic competitive architecture: "embed principles of behavioural science into your business and then sit back and reap rich dividends."

**The Implication**
Audit your pricing page, your product launch sequence, and your return policy through a behavioural science lens — identify one decision point where the default is working against you and change the architecture, not the messaging.

### Principle 8

> The customer who does your work for you costs you nothing and delivers more than your marketing department.

**What This Means & Why**
When customers are made partial employees — through self-service, co-production, or user-generated content — two things happen simultaneously: costs fall and satisfaction rises. The counterintuitive mechanism is effort justification: when people invest effort in something, they value it more highly. Self-assembled furniture, self-check-in at airports, and self-customised food orders all generate higher satisfaction than the equivalent full-service experience, because the customer's own effort becomes part of the product.

**The India Reality**
India's e-commerce ecosystem has moved millions of consumers to full self-service purchase journeys — product discovery, comparison, payment, returns — that would have required a salesperson a decade ago. The consumers who adapted fastest were not necessarily the most digitally literate; they were the ones for whom the control and transparency of doing it themselves outweighed the comfort of human assistance.

**The Evidence**
In 'Improve customer satisfaction at minimal cost: Convert customers into employees' (2015), Founding Fuel cites McDonald's, airlines, and e-commerce platforms as having systematically outsourced work to customers — tray clearance, check-in, product curation — without compensation. The paradox: "complaining about bad service is tantamount to complaining against themselves." Customer satisfaction rises because the customer's own agency is now embedded in the outcome.

**The Implication**
Identify one step in your customer journey that currently requires staff time and redesign it as a customer-led action — but only after confirming that the customer gains a sense of control or customisation from doing it, not just a sense of abandonment.

### Principle 9

> Brand advocates are a different species from loyal customers — and most companies are farming the wrong one.

**What This Means & Why**
Loyalty is a retention metric. Advocacy is an acquisition engine. A loyal customer keeps buying; an advocate brings others. The distinction matters because the investments that generate loyalty (reward programmes, price consistency, service quality) are not the same as the investments that generate advocacy (identity resonance, community, story worth telling). Companies optimising for retention are not automatically building referral.

**The India Reality**
Nielsen data cited in FF's coverage establishes that 92% of consumers trust earned media — recommendations from people they know — over any form of advertising. In a market where family and community networks remain the primary trust architecture for most consumers, the multiplier effect of a single genuine advocate is larger in India than in more atomised consumer societies.

**The Evidence**
In 'Brand friend for life' (2015), Founding Fuel draws the hierarchy explicitly: satisfaction → loyalty → advocacy, and argues that most brand investment stops at loyalty. The piece cites Google's rejection of Sergio Zyman's marketing plan — "Focus on the user and all else will follow" — as the paradigmatic case of product-led advocacy: Google became the world's most valuable brand without traditional advertising because users became evangelists before the company spent a rupee on media.

**The Implication**
In your next NPS review, segment your promoters by referral behaviour, not just score — identify the subset who are actively bringing others and design a specific programme to amplify that cohort, separate from your general loyalty mechanics.

### Principle 10

> Experience is not a premium add-on — it is the only durable competitive moat for any product that can be copied.

**What This Means & Why**
Products can be reverse-engineered. Services can be replicated. Prices can be matched. The one thing that cannot be commoditised is a felt experience — because it is constructed in the moment, involves all five senses, and is inseparable from memory. Companies that sell a product are competing in a market; companies that sell an experience are creating a category.

**The India Reality**
Starbucks in India is not competing with Indian chai — it is selling a social experience and an identity moment that chai cannot replicate in that format. The conversation is not about coffee quality. This same logic applies to Zudio's store design, to MINISO's retail environment, and to why Paper Boat's packaging is as important as its flavour formulation. The experience layer is doing work that the product alone cannot.

**The Evidence**
In 'Why you should sell your brand as an experience' (2015), Founding Fuel uses Starbucks as the canonical case — "not by marketing coffee as a product... not by marketing coffee as a service... but by marketing it as an experience." The five-sense delivery mechanism is named explicitly: "When the five senses are engaged and assaulted in unison, they heighten any experience." The progression from product to service to experience is framed not as a luxury upgrade but as a survival strategy for any brand operating in a category with low switching costs.

**The Implication**
Map your customer's last interaction with your brand across all five senses and identify which ones you are currently ignoring — then design one deliberate sensory element into your next product, store, or service touchpoint.

### Principle 11

> The consumer your data describes and the consumer who exists in the market are often two different people.

**What This Means & Why**
Data aggregates behaviour across populations and flattens it into metrics. It cannot tell you why someone did what they did, what they were feeling when they did it, or what they would do next in a context your data has never seen. Ethnographic research — sitting with a consumer in their kitchen, watching them navigate a checkout, following them through a market — consistently surfaces behaviour that survey data and clickstream analysis miss entirely.

**The India Reality**
India's obsession with sample size in market research — treating quantitative scale as a substitute for contextual depth — is identified in FF's coverage as a specifically Indian trait that costs companies insight at exactly the moments they need it most. The future Group's "Future Ideas" cell, which built ethnographic observation into its retail strategy, is cited as an exceptional practice precisely because it was exceptional.

**The Evidence**
In 'Building consumer empathy' (2015), Founding Fuel defends ethnographic research against both the gut-instinct bias of creative teams and the data-fetishism of tech companies: "This obsession with sample size is a peculiar Indian trait." The article names banks, e-commerce companies, and telcos as the sectors most overconfident in their data — and most likely to be blindsided by consumer behaviour they never modelled. In 'Nuance is the new differentiator' (2025), Shrinath V deepens this: "Behaviour is flattened into metrics, and context becomes optional. But people don't live in clean funnels."

**The Implication**
Before your next product decision that rests on a survey or a dashboard, spend four hours in direct observation of three real customers using your product in their actual context — not a usability lab, their context.

### Principle 12

> TikTok was not a hinterland fad — it was the first proof that Bharat had built its own celebrity economy, and marketers missed it entirely.

**What This Means & Why**
The dismissal of TikTok by mainstream Indian brand managers was a class failure dressed up as a strategic judgment. "We believe that TikTok is for the Buntys and Bablis of Bharat" is a sentence that summarises how urban marketing departments maintained comfortable distance from 200 million of their own customers. What TikTok actually demonstrated was that the celebrity democratisation enabled by cheap data (Jio) had created a new layer of influence architecture that bypass every existing media channel.

**The India Reality**
Shivani Kapila from Surat — wears shorts, travels to London, makes videos on women's empowerment, supported by in-laws — is the archetype that FF surfaces as evidence of split modernity: surface adoption of western aesthetics combined with deeply held traditional values, expressed through a Chinese app on an Indian smartphone running on Indian data. This is not a contradiction. It is the actual consumer.

**The Evidence**
In 'Bharat is watching this. Is your brand watching too?' (2019), Piyul Mukherjee documents that FMCG majors, broadcasters, automobile companies, paint companies, and financial services firms were "blissfully insulated from an app that has stormed the eyeballs of at least 200 million of their own core customers." The article names this as brand manager blindness rooted in class: TikTok was Bharat, and Bharat was not in the room where brand decisions were made.

**The Implication**
Identify the one digital or social platform your marketing team does not use personally and spend one month consuming it as a non-marketer before deciding it is not relevant to your brand.

### Principle 13

> Modernity in non-metro India is not a rejection of tradition — it is a specific negotiation with it that outsiders cannot read correctly.

**What This Means & Why**
Western consumer frameworks — and most urban Indian brand frameworks — treat modernity and tradition as opposing ends of a spectrum. Consumers are expected to move along it directionally: as education and income rise, traditional values recede. This is empirically wrong in large parts of India. Consumers hold both simultaneously, without cognitive dissonance, because they are solving different problems with each.

**The India Reality**
Patanjali's success is the most commercially significant proof of this principle. A brand with dowdy packaging, no fancy advertising, and endorsement from a yoga teacher defeated multinational FMCG companies with decade-long distribution and billion-rupee media budgets — not because it was cheaper (though it was) but because it was a cultural signal. "In many cases, it has the ringing endorsement of the older generation in most Indian families — and that carries a lot more weight than the crores invested in advertising."

**The Evidence**
In 'The Patanjali phenomenon' (2016), Indrajit Gupta argues that the brand's disruption cannot be explained by price or herbal positioning alone — it is a trust shift driven by generational endorsement in joint-family structures. The article invokes Damodar Mall's hypothesis: Patanjali represents something deeper than a product preference, and the multinational FMCG managers who expected it to "peter out" were reading it through a framework that could not see what it actually was.

**The Implication**
In your next brand positioning exercise, audit whether your aspiration and tradition levers are being deployed as a spectrum or as simultaneous, co-existing signals — the latter is how most Indian consumers actually hold them.

### Principle 14

> The consumer technology is leaving behind is not a niche — it is a 340-million-person market hiding in plain sight.

**What This Means & Why**
Technology design defaults to the young, urban, visually able, fast-fingered user. Everyone else is an afterthought or a future version. India's ageing population — 340 million above 60 by 2050 — is approaching at a speed that product design timelines are not built for. The companies that have already started building for this consumer will not just have a moral advantage; they will have a commercial one.

**The India Reality**
ATM interfaces that time out before elderly users can complete a transaction are not a design oversight — they are a statement about whose experience matters. Joseph Coughlin's "expectations gap" — between what older consumers want and what products deliver — is wider in India because the category is almost entirely absent from organised retail and technology strategy. There is no Indian equivalent of the senior-focused product design industry that exists in Japan or Germany.

**The Evidence**
In 'The consumers technology is leaving behind' (2019), Founding Fuel documents the gap across multiple product categories — banking interfaces, health technology, digital communication — and invokes Coughlin's framing: "an expectations gap between what older consumers want from a product and what most of these products deliver." The SilverGenie case in 'Why Emotions Are First Principles in Product Strategy' (2025) by Shrinath V shows what happens when a company does read this consumer correctly: the adults buying for elderly parents responded to a model built around their guilt, not their parents' health — and the business worked.

**The Implication**
Add one person above 60 to your next user testing session and measure how many steps in your core user journey they cannot complete without assistance — that number is your addressable gap.

### Principle 15

> Speed is not a consumer virtue — it is a supplier assumption projected onto consumers who never asked for it.

**What This Means & Why**
The race to 10-minute delivery, same-day dispatch, and instant everything is driven by competitive dynamics between suppliers — not by a revealed consumer preference that waited patiently to be met. Most consumers, offered a genuine choice between speed and reliability, quality, or environmental cost, make a more nuanced trade-off than the industry assumes. The speed narrative serves investor pitches better than it serves consumer insight.

**The India Reality**
Zomato's 10-minute delivery was met with genuine public scepticism — "What kind of food can be delivered from the station to home in 10 minutes?" — and criticism of the risk it transferred to delivery partners. India's road infrastructure, traffic density, and the actual time cost of preparing food that arrives in good condition make the 10-minute promise as much a marketing claim as a logistics achievement.

**The Evidence**
In 'FF Insights #616: Slow food' (2022), Founding Fuel traces the Slow Food movement's argument — that hurry in food is a cultural pathology, not a preference — and applies it to the Indian quick commerce moment. The article cites Carlo Honore's 'In Praise of Slow' and the Slow Food manifesto: "Slow Food also preaches 'eco-gastronomy' — the notion that eating well can, and should, go hand in hand with protecting the environment." The piece positions Zomato's 10-minute delivery as the precise opposite of this insight — a supplier-driven race that externalises its costs onto delivery workers and food quality.

**The Implication**
Test your fastest delivery option against a slower, better-quality alternative in one market and measure not just conversion but repeat purchase rate and NPS — the consumer who values quality over speed is often more valuable per transaction and more loyal over time.

### Principle 16

> Green consumption is mostly status performance — and designing for the stated motive will get you the wrong product.

**What This Means & Why**
Consumers report pro-environmental motivations for green purchases. The research is consistent and overwhelming. It is also, in significant part, wrong — because the social signal of the purchase (conspicuous altruism, identity projection) is doing as much or more work than the environmental value. This does not make green consumption cynical; it makes it human. But it means products designed primarily for environmental performance will consistently underperform products designed for social legibility.

**The India Reality**
India's EV market is a live case study in this principle. Electric two-wheelers and three-wheelers have outsold electric cars by a large multiple — not because two-wheeler buyers are more environmentally motivated but because the use case (short urban commutes, no apartment charging politics, lower price point) makes the rational case more obviously. Electric cars, carrying a higher status signal, face the apartment society charging negotiation problem that has nothing to do with environmental conviction and everything to do with social friction.

**The Evidence**
In 'WFH Daily #205: Why do people go green?' (2020), Founding Fuel invokes Simler and Hanson's 'The Elephant in the Brain' to argue that Prius ownership is partly "an act of conspicuous altruism" — status performance dressed as environmental concern. "Conventional wisdom holds that consumers buy green goods in order to 'help the environment.' But we must think harder." In 'What they don't tell you about EVs in India' (2024), Pranav Srivilasan documents that "electric cars have lagged well behind scooters, accounting for only about one in fifty cars sold today" — a ratio that tracks social friction and use-case fit more precisely than environmental commitment.

**The Implication**
If your product has a genuine environmental benefit, lead your marketing with the social identity signal, not the environmental data — the consumer who buys for status will also deliver the environmental outcome, but the reverse is not reliably true.

### Principle 17

> Nostalgia is a product strategy, not a marketing tone — and Indian brands have barely started using it.

**What This Means & Why**
Nostalgia in branding is typically treated as a mood — a warm visual palette, a throwback logo, a retro jingle. This misses what nostalgia actually is as a consumer force: a psychological shortcut to trust, a felt connection to identity, and a reliable predictor of willingness to pay a premium. When a product can make you feel the way you felt at a specific moment in the past, it is not competing on the product dimension at all — it is competing on the memory dimension, where there is no price competition.

**The India Reality**
India's food memory is exceptionally potent — ghar ka khana carries the weight of unconditional care, seasonal mangoes carry childhood summers, millets carry a complete cultural reversal from poor people's grain to premium health food. Aavrani's success in the US with Indian-origin ingredients — packaging that evokes "the bright golden yellow of the mangoes her grandmother would cut for her" — demonstrates that this nostalgia is commercially portable across geographies.

**The Evidence**
In 'Paper Boat: Riding on a wave of nostalgia' (2016), Founding Fuel documents how Paper Boat built a brand not on beverage quality or distribution but on the specific memory attached to aam panna, jaljeera, and kokum — seasonal Indian drinks that their target consumer had last encountered at a grandmother's house. The brand's Zara-inspired seasonal strategy kept scarcity real, which kept nostalgia potent. In 'An Indian beauty upstart in the West' (2021), Aparajita Bhattacharyya shows the same architecture applied to skincare: Aavrani's founder Rooshy Roy built a luxury brand on adaptogens the West called exotic and she called home.

**The Implication**
Identify one ingredient, format, or ritual from your category's pre-industrial or pre-organised-retail history and test whether your core consumer has an emotional relationship with it — if they do, that is your brand story, not your R&D pipeline.

---

### Principle 18

> The attention economy is not competing with your competitors — it is competing with sleep, and you need to decide which side you are on.

**What This Means & Why**
Reed Hastings said it plainly: Netflix's biggest competitor is sleep. The platforms that have internalized this are not building products — they are building compulsion loops. The ethical question this raises has not been answered by any major platform, and in India, where 200 million first-generation smartphone users encountered these mechanisms with no prior media literacy framework, the consequences are still being counted.

**The India Reality**
India is the largest market for many of these platforms outside the United States. The breakup debate — alive in US regulatory and public discourse — had "not gained public attention yet" in India as of 2019, according to FF's coverage. The cheap data revolution (Jio) that democratised access to TikTok, YouTube, and social media for Bharat simultaneously exposed those users to engagement architecture designed for maximum attention extraction with no corresponding consumer protection infrastructure.

**The Evidence**
In 'A nudge against sleep and productivity' (2019), Charles Assisi uses the cocaine-dealer analogy without irony: "We aren't very different from cocaine peddlers. The first shot is always free." The article invokes both Gerd Gigerenzer on algorithmic decision-making and the broader nudge literature to argue that the harder users try to be productive, the easier it is for them to be nudged against their own interests. Assisi names India explicitly as the market where this matters most and where consumer protection is most absent.

**The Implication**
If you are building a consumer product with engagement metrics as a primary KPI, add one metric that measures whether users feel better or worse about their time spent after using it — and review both metrics together before your next product decision.

## FF's View

---

**The Core Conviction**

FF's single most important argument on consumer behaviour and retail is this: the consumer is not a rational agent making optimised choices, and any strategy built on the assumption that they are will eventually fail. This is not a controversial academic claim. It is a practical observation, drawn from watching how India's market actually moves — through the Patanjali disruption, through TikTok's conquest of small-town imagination, through the refusal of middle India to trust COD-free checkout flows, through elderly people unable to complete ATM transactions before the screen times out. The consumer is emotional first, contextual always, and rational only occasionally, and usually only after the purchase.

What makes this conviction distinctively FF's is not the claim itself — behavioural economics has been saying this since Kahneman — but where FF insists it leads. Most practitioners, when they accept this insight intellectually, immediately retreat to a safer version: "yes, emotions matter, so let's invest in brand." FF pushes harder. In 'Why Emotions Are First Principles in Product Strategy' (2025), Shrinath V argues that emotions are not a wrapper around the real product decision — they are the decision. The SilverGenie case makes this concrete: elder care technology failed not because the tech was wrong but because the product team was answering the wrong emotional question. The buyer wasn't an elderly person seeking independence. The buyer was an adult child managing guilt at a distance. These are entirely different products. Confuse them and you build something technically correct that no one buys.

This conviction runs through every period of FF's coverage, from the early brand-advocacy pieces in 2015 to the post-pandemic ethnographic work on Bharat. It challenges the dashboard model of consumer insight — where purchase funnels, conversion rates, and cohort retention charts substitute for understanding what people actually carry with them when they walk into a store or open an app. Data describes behaviour in aggregate; it does not explain the specific, contextual, emotional transaction happening at the individual level. FF has never been comfortable with the assumption that enough data closes that gap. In 'Nuance is the new differentiator' (2025), Shrinath V makes the case most sharply: "With AI tools now entering the research process, that gap may quietly widen." More data, processed faster, may produce a more confident picture that is further from truth.

The practitioner implication is concrete. If you lead a consumer business in India and your strategy relies primarily on what customers tell you they want — in surveys, in focus groups, in NPS scores — you are flying on instrumentation that was calibrated for a different aircraft. The map is clean. The territory is not.

**What India Means for This Theme**

India changes the rules of consumer strategy in ways that imported frameworks do not anticipate, and the first mistake is assuming that India is simply an emerging version of a mature market — that what worked in the US or China will work here once the income levels catch up. FF's reporting documents, repeatedly, that this is not a timing problem. It is a structural difference.

Start with the price-sensitivity architecture. In 'Why iPhone SE may lose out in India and why Amazon wants to open bookstores' (2016), Rajesh Srivastava identifies a precise distinction: "Indian consumers, unlike Chinese customers, are not looking for a smaller smartphone, but a cheaper one." Chinese consumers at equivalent income levels traded up for prestige. Indian consumers traded sideways for value. This is not irrational. It reflects a generations-deep household economy where money is counted carefully, where the 47% import duty structure creates real price pressure, and where the social comparison set is more local than aspirational. The calculator, as Srivastava writes, is fitted in the mind.

This manifests in retail in ways that confound standard category management. Zudio's success — documented in 'Noel Tata's brahmastra' (2023) by Indrajit Gupta — is built not on premiumisation but on the opposite: a "Turns Retailer" model (borrowing GP Cachon's Wharton typology), low markup, high inventory turns, a physical presence deliberately planted in Jharsuguda, Dimapur, Imphal, Pathankot, Gorakhpur. The insight is structural: GST rationalisation, better road infrastructure, and modern third-party warehousing have made the hinterland logistically viable in ways it was not a decade ago. Zudio went to where the aspiration was waiting, rather than waiting for the aspiration to come to the cities.

The trust architecture in India is also fundamentally different — not just quantitatively but qualitatively. In 'The Middle India consumer' (2022), FF documents how blue-collar workers in India had pension accruals they didn't know about, had no way to check their balance, and were sometimes defrauded by contractors withholding payouts. When Smartstaff introduced PF visibility as a feature, it became "a sticky feature from a trust perspective" not because workers suddenly valued retirement savings, but because transparency in a context of routine opacity is itself a product. Trust is not ambient in middle India. It must be constructed, earned, and maintained through specific operational choices.

The TikTok reporting from 2019 — Piyul Mukherjee's 'Bharat is watching this. Is your brand watching too?' — documents a different kind of structural difference: the democratisation of celebrity in small-town India. The enabling condition was not just the app. It was cheap data via Jio, first-generation smartphone ownership, and the specific social dynamics of markets where traditional gatekeepers of cultural production — Bollywood, advertising, English-language media — had never addressed the consumer at all. TikTok didn't just give small-town India entertainment. It gave them a microphone and a stage. FMCG majors, as Mukherjee notes, were "blissfully insulated from an app that had stormed the eyeballs of at least 200 million of their own core customers." That insularity was not an oversight. It was a class-based assumption that Bharat's media consumption was not worth monitoring.

The modernity paradox — documented through the figure of Shivani Kapila from Surat in 'Please look now: We are changing' (2019) by Mukherjee — is perhaps the most important structural insight in FF's India-specific reporting. Kapila wears shorts, travels to London, makes videos on women's empowerment, and is supported by her in-laws. Modernity is expressed at the surface. The value system beneath does not shift at the same pace. "Modernity is defined in the clothes, but the ethos does not reach deep value systems. And this is not seen as a contradiction." For consumer strategy, this means that the behavioural signal visible to the brand — the purchase, the engagement, the platform use — may not encode the value system you assume it encodes. Reading the signal without reading the context produces systematically wrong conclusions.

The Bharat dimension extends to design and technology. In 'From Bazaar to Breakthrough' (2025), Shrinath V documents a sugarcane juice vendor with a QR code, a tailoring shop taking orders via WhatsApp voice notes. These are not failures of tech adoption. They are local improvisations that work. The point is structural: in India, use cases are often not discovered by companies designing products. They are discovered by users who adapt what is available to fit their actual lives. The implication for consumer strategy is significant: products designed for a specific use case will be repurposed, sometimes profitably, sometimes not, often invisibly to the product team. Designing for adaptability is not optional. It is a description of how the market actually functions.

India's ageing population sits almost entirely outside this analysis in most corporate strategy rooms. In 'The consumers technology is leaving behind' (2019), FF documents that India will have 340 million people above 60 by 2050. ATM interfaces that time out before elderly users can respond are cited as a concrete failure — not just a usability oversight, but evidence of a systematic design assumption that the consumer is young, English-literate, fast, and digitally fluent. The market that is building itself on a demographic that will numerically dwarf the current target segment. That this is barely registering in consumer strategy circles is, in FF's framing, not a gap to be aware of. It is a specific competitive opportunity that is currently unoccupied.

**Where Global Best Practices Break Down**

The failure point of global best practices in India is almost always the same: they were designed for a consumer with high ambient trust, stable purchasing patterns, and the cognitive and financial bandwidth to absorb friction. India has all three of these in shorter supply, more unevenly distributed, and in configurations that global frameworks were not designed to handle.

The experience economy framework — product to service to experience, Pine & Gilmore's progression, Starbucks as the canonical case — is sound as far as it goes. But it was calibrated for consumers who have already had the product and the service. In India, large segments of the population are encountering formal retail, financial products, and digital platforms for the first time. The experience economy assumes a baseline. In middle India, building that baseline is itself the competitive challenge. FF's reporting on Razorpay's approach — documented in 'The Middle India consumer' (2022) — shows this clearly: Razorpay won trust from small businesses not by building a superior experience layer, but by becoming a useful content resource first. The sequence was inverted relative to the global model: trust, then usage, then product. The global best practice assumed product, then usage, then loyalty. In India, with first-time users of formal financial infrastructure, that sequence fails.

The omnichannel frameworks that dominated global retail thinking through the 2010s — the seamless customer journey, the integrated online-offline experience — imported a specific assumption: that consumers move fluidly between channels and expect consistency. In Indian traditional retail, the response to the channel-switching customer was not to build omnichannel capability. It was dismissal. The 'fit lifter' — the customer who tries on in-store and buys online — was named with contempt, not addressed with strategy, as documented in 'Wooing the multi-channel shopper' (2015). This is a cultural and organisational failure that global best practice could not prevent because global best practice assumed the organisation was already motivated to solve the problem. In India's traditional retail sector, the motivation was absent.

The CRM and loyalty programme orthodoxy — satisfaction leads to loyalty leads to advocacy — is challenged by the Patanjali case in ways that no Western brand management framework anticipated. Patanjali, as documented in 'The Patanjali phenomenon' (2016) by Indrajit Gupta, violated almost every rule of brand management: no premium packaging, no aspiration signalling, no trained sales force, no celebrity endorsement in the conventional sense, no high-margin product portfolio. It had dowdy packaging, which consumers read as authenticity. It had Baba Ramdev's endorsement delivered during yoga broadcasts, which carried multigenerational credibility in Indian households. It distributed through its own channels, bypassing distributor margins and passing the savings forward. The loyalty it generated was not built through a points system or a customer journey map. It was built through cultural resonance with a specific trust shift — a generational endorsement from the older members of Indian families, whose opinion on product choices carries structural weight that brand managers trained on aspirational marketing are not equipped to measure or respond to.

The speed and convenience orthodoxy — faster delivery, frictionless checkout, zero-click purchasing — encounters a specific Indian contradiction documented in 'Nuance is the new differentiator' (2025). Cash-on-delivery was not a workaround for consumers who lacked digital payment methods. It was, for many consumers in middle India, a rational response to a trust deficit. "COD restores a sense of control... Payment before delivery feels like a leap." The global best practice said: eliminate COD, it's operationally expensive, and push consumers toward prepay. The Indian consumer said: I'll move to prepay when I trust you, and I don't trust you yet. The brand that treated COD removal as an efficiency decision, rather than a trust decision, was answering a different question than the one the consumer was asking.

Green consumption is another area where global best practice breaks down on closer reading. In 'WFH Daily #205: Why do people go green?' (2020), FF applies the Simler and Hanson framework from The Elephant in the Brain: Prius owners are not primarily motivated by environmental concern. They are performing conspicuous altruism — signalling prosocial attitudes through a high-visibility status purchase. The implication for Indian green brands is significant: the environmental argument, however well-evidenced, is not the primary purchase driver. The status and identity signal is. Brands that lead with the environment and treat status as a secondary consideration may be answering in the wrong order.

Indian mainstream business media covers consumer behaviour primarily through three lenses: market size (the India opportunity narrative), sector disruption (e-commerce vs. traditional retail, OTT vs. theatrical), and brand campaigns (what worked, what won which award). FF's divergence from this is consistent and structural.

First, FF treats the consumer as a subject of serious analytical attention, not as the passive destination of business strategy. The articles on behavioural science — 'How Zara, Sensodyne and others use behavioural science to unlock value' (2015), 'What successful companies do to win customer loyalty' (2015) — approach consumer choice as something to be understood and taken seriously rather than as a response variable to be optimised. This is a different editorial posture. Mainstream business media tends to produce consumer coverage that is effectively produced for the brand, not the consumer.

Second, FF covered TikTok's Bharat penetration in 2019 — two full years before mainstream media acknowledged it as a strategic force — specifically because Piyul Mukherjee was doing ethnographic fieldwork in small-town India, not interviewing brand managers in Mumbai. The FMCG majors' insularity to TikTok, as documented in 'Bharat is watching this. Is your brand watching too?' (2019), was itself a story about media class bias. Mainstream media, with its advertiser-client base of metro-focused FMCG brands, had structural reasons not to cover what Bharat was actually watching.

Third, FF has consistently treated qualitative and ethnographic research as a legitimate and superior form of consumer intelligence in contexts where survey data and purchase metrics produce misleading signals. The 2015 piece 'Building consumer empathy' explicitly argues against "the peculiarly Indian obsession with sample size" in advertising research. Future Group's investment in ethnographic capability is presented as exceptional and admirable. Mainstream media coverage of research tends to default to Nielsen numbers, CMO-sourced insights, and the kind of claims that can be released in a press release. FF has remained interested in the methodological question: how do you actually know what a consumer wants, especially when they can't or won't tell you accurately?

Fourth, on the question of advertising's declining credibility, FF has been consistent from 2015 — citing Nielsen's 92% earned media trust figure against traditional advertising — while mainstream media, which depends on advertising revenue, has been structurally reluctant to publish the same argument. The 'Brand friend for life' (2015) framing — brand advocacy as the only sustainable consumer relationship — remains far more radical than mainstream media coverage of brand strategy, which tends to treat advertising effectiveness as a question of creative execution rather than structural credibility.

**Against Management Orthodoxy**

The management orthodoxy on consumer behaviour holds roughly this: understand your customer segment, build a value proposition that addresses their stated needs, communicate it through the right channels with the right creative, and measure the outcome through sales and satisfaction metrics. Iterate. Scale.

FF's evidence base contradicts this orthodoxy at every joint.

On customer needs: the orthodox position is that needs can be surfaced through research — surveys, focus groups, A/B testing — and that the job of the brand is to address what surfaces. The counter-evidence runs from SilverGenie (adult-child guilt, not elderly independence, was the purchase driver) through COD adoption (control need, not payment method preference) through Patanjali (cultural identity signal, not ingredient preference) through TikTok in Bharat (identity expression platform for first-generation smartphone users, not entertainment product). In every case, the stated need or the observable behaviour pointed in a different direction from the actual purchase driver. In 'Why Emotions Are First Principles in Product Strategy' (2025), Shrinath V articulates the principle directly: "We assume users are rational, needs are explicit, and decisions are logical. In reality, decisions are driven by fear, guilt, pride, and the need for control." The orthodox research process, designed to surface explicit needs, systematically misses this.

On value proposition communication: the orthodox position is that advertising — with sufficient investment, targeting, and creative quality — builds brand equity and drives purchase. FF's counter-position, consistent from the first batch of articles: "Advertising requires huge investments. But as these findings suggest, these investments are governed by the law of diminishing returns." The Ronaldo penalty kick scenario in 'Brand friend for life' (2015) — advertising that interrupts creates negative associations — is the reductio ad absurdum of the interruption model. The Google case is the positive counter-example: product excellence drives word-of-mouth, which builds brand without advertising spend. The management orthodoxy treats advertising budget as a primary lever. FF's evidence consistently shows it as a secondary one that works only when the primary levers — product quality, customer experience, advocacy — are already working.

On metrics: the orthodoxy says measure what matters — NPS, CSAT, retention, conversion. The counter-evidence is that the things that matter most are often the things the metrics do not capture. Patanjali's rise was invisible to conventional brand tracking until it was not. TikTok's Bharat penetration was invisible to FMCG brand dashboards. The COD preference in middle India looked like a payment method problem in the conversion funnel, not a trust problem requiring a different solution. As Shrinath V writes in 'Nuance is the new differentiator' (2025): "Behaviour is flattened into metrics, and context becomes optional. But people don't live in clean funnels."

On loyalty: the orthodoxy says build satisfaction, then loyalty, then advocacy. FF's hierarchy — documented in 'Brand friend for life' (2015) and the brand advocacy pieces — inverts the causal logic. Advocacy is not the outcome of a loyalty programme. It is the outcome of a product or experience so good, or a cultural signal so strong, that the consumer volunteers to carry the brand into their social network. Loyalty programmes, in this reading, are an expensive substitute for the thing that actually produces advocates: being genuinely excellent at something the consumer cares about.

On pricing: the Simon Sinek citation in 'FF Daily #463: The price game' (2021) — "selling based on price is like heroin" — is stronger than it first appears. In India's price-sensitive market, the reflex to compete on price is nearly universal and nearly universally corrosive. The Zudio model is instructive precisely because it manages to be genuinely low-price while avoiding the heroin trap: it does so through operational discipline (high inventory turns, own-label design, hinterland locations with lower real estate costs) rather than margin compression. The price is the result of operational choices, not a competitive strategy in itself.

**The Entrepreneurial Mindset Beyond Startups**

The entrepreneurial lens on consumer behaviour is not primarily about starting new ventures. It is about the willingness to question what you think you know about the consumer, to sit with uncomfortable evidence, and to act on observations that your existing organisation's incentive structure is designed to ignore.

Noel Tata's Zudio is the clearest example in FF's coverage of an entrepreneurial mindset operating within a large, established organisation. The decision to open in Jharsuguda, Dimapur, Imphal — documented in 'Noel Tata's brahmastra' (2023) — was not a data-driven optimisation of existing strategy. It was a hypothesis about where aspiration was waiting. The Trent organisation had to ignore the standard retail site-selection model (footfall density in existing trade zones) and act on a different model entirely (aspiration density in underserved geographies). That is an entrepreneurial act, independent of whether it involves a startup.

Razorpay's decision to build content for small businesses before building product adoption is another. The documented approach in 'The Middle India consumer' (2022) — becoming a trusted content resource as the path to product adoption — is a sequence that most large fintech organisations would not have chosen, because the content investment doesn't appear in the short-term conversion metrics. It required an entrepreneurial tolerance for an indirect path.

Meesho's model — using women entrepreneurs as trust intermediaries for introducing middle-India consumers to marketplace technology — documented in 'The Middle India consumer' (2022), is the structural solution to the trust problem that the conventional e-commerce model couldn't solve. Instead of trying to build platform trust directly, Meesho delegated trust to human networks that already existed. This is an entrepreneurial insight about the architecture of trust in markets where institutional trust is low.

The entrepreneurial mindset also shows up in the SilverGenie case — in the decision to answer the question "what does the adult child actually need to feel less guilty" rather than "what does an elderly person need to maintain independence." As documented in 'Why Emotions Are First Principles in Product Strategy' (2025), this reframing required ignoring the product category logic (elder care = elder-facing product) and following the emotional evidence instead. Investors pushed back — "where's the deep tech?" — because they were applying the standard venture lens to a non-standard insight. The entrepreneurial position was to hold the insight against that pressure.

For policymakers, the entrepreneurial lesson from FF's coverage is perhaps most visible in the India Stack documentation. The DigiLocker experience described in 'A digital immigrant's initiation into the digital-first world' (2021) — getting a digital driving licence "within seconds" — is the result of policy choices that treated user experience as a first-order design constraint, not an afterthought. That is entrepreneurial thinking in a government context: starting from the human experience and working backwards to the infrastructure, rather than starting from the infrastructure and hoping the experience works out.

**On Transformation: Process, Not Just Destination**

The transformation of Indian consumer behaviour and retail is not happening in straight lines, and FF's reporting is most valuable when it resists the temptation to narrate transformation as a before-and-after story.

The Patanjali disruption is the clearest case of transformation as a process that looked, from the outside, like it happened suddenly. Indrajit Gupta's 'The Patanjali phenomenon' (2016) makes the argument that the conditions were long in place: a generation of Indians for whom Baba Ramdev was a trusted daily presence through yoga broadcasts, an older generation in Indian households whose product endorsement carries structural weight, a distribution channel built over years through Patanjali's own yoga camps and retail network, and a 20–25% cost advantage that could be passed to consumers because the margin structure had been rebuilt from the ground up. None of this was visible in standard brand tracking until it was. The transformation looked sudden because the measurement was late, not because the change was sudden.

The post-pandemic reconfiguration of consumption patterns follows a similar logic in FF's coverage. 'On the menu: The changing attitude towards home cooking' (2020) documents a shift in the relationship with ghar ka khana that the pandemic made legible but didn't create. The emotional charge of home-cooked food — its association with unconditional care, with the "ghar ka khana you remember the most because it is made with emotions" — was always there. The pandemic created the conditions under which people could examine that relationship consciously, and, for many, choose differently. The transformation was not created by the crisis. It was surfaced by it.

The EV adoption trajectory in India, documented across 'How to choose the right electric scooter' (2021) and 'What they don't tell you about EVs in India' (2024), is a case study in transformation blocked at unexpected points. Two-wheelers and three-wheelers moved faster than four-wheelers. The apartment society charging politics — the individual trying to negotiate a charger installation on the fifth floor — is not a trivial barrier. It is a governance and social coordination problem that no amount of product improvement resolves. Transformation in this case is blocked not by the absence of good products, nor by consumer unwillingness, but by a specific institutional friction that requires a specific institutional solution.

The millet story in 'FF Life: The golden seed' (2023) is transformation-as-full-circle: a crop that was a poor person's staple, was abandoned for wheat and rice as those became status foods, and is now returning as a premium urban health product and climate-resilient agricultural choice simultaneously. The transformation is neither linear nor simple. It involves changing what a food means, who grows it, who eats it, at what price point, with what cultural connotation. Three separate transformations — individual health, agricultural economics, climate resilience — are converging on the same product. Managing a brand through that complexity requires a different model than the standard brand lifecycle story.

The Bollywood theatrical crisis — documented in 'Bollywood's wakeup call' (2022) by Indrajit Gupta — is a case where the industry was slow to recognise that the pandemic had accelerated a transformation that was already in progress. OTT platforms had been building audience for years. The lockdowns completed an experiment in OTT consumption that viewers, once they'd run it, were reluctant to reverse. The question Gupta raises — whether the change in user behaviour is "long drawn" — is still open. But the transformation mechanism is clear: the pandemic reduced the switching cost of moving from theatrical to OTT, and switching costs, once reduced, rarely fully recover.

**The Human and Cultural Dimension**

The single most consistent insight in FF's consumer behaviour coverage is that human and cultural factors are not a soft layer on top of the real, rational economic decision. They are the real decision. Everything else is rationalisation.

The mascara effect, documented in 'The mascara effect' (2020) by Charles Assisi, is extreme evidence for this. Leonard Lauder's observation that cosmetic sales rise in downturns is well-known. Charles Assisi extends it to the Yad Vashem documentation of beauty rituals in Holocaust concentration camps — women maintaining grooming routines in conditions of extremity. The implication is not that humans are irrational in a crisis. It is that identity maintenance, dignity assertion, and the preservation of normalcy are fundamental human drives that operate even when survival is the primary concern. For consumer strategy, this means that the emotional utility of a purchase — the maintenance of a sense of self — is often more durable than the functional utility. Comfort brands surge in recessions not because consumers have stopped calculating. They have calculated, and the emotional utility won.

The nostalgia economy in Indian food — documented in 'On the menu: The changing attitude towards home cooking' (2020) — operates through a specific mechanism: the value of ghar ka khana is latent until you are separated from it. "The nostalgic value only surfaces when you move away from home." This is a consumption behaviour insight with product strategy implications: the market for products that encode home, memory, and unconditional care grows as urbanisation and migration accelerate. Paper Boat's strategy, referenced in 'Paper Boat: Riding on a wave of nostalgia' (2016), is the commercial application of the same insight: seasonal products (aam panna, jamun kala khatta) that encode specific memory states are not competing on taste or price. They are competing on the ability to recall a specific feeling.

The cultural dimension of Patanjali trust — the older generation's endorsement carrying structural weight in Indian family purchasing decisions — is documented carefully in 'The Patanjali phenomenon' (2016). This is not sentimental. It is a description of how purchasing authority is distributed in Indian households, where the older generation's opinion on product quality, health claims, and brand integrity carries more weight than any advertising campaign targeted at the adult buyer. Brands that understand Indian household decision architecture will market differently from brands that assume individual purchase decisions.

The selfie economy transformation in Indian retail — documented in 'Death of the white shirt?' (2019) by Charles Assisi — is a cultural shift with direct retail consequence. Mumbai retailer data shows lower-body apparel sales falling and upper-body apparel rising. Young consumers financing Rs 1,000–1,500 purchases through Bajaj Finserv EMIs. The mechanism is cultural: the smartphone camera, selfie culture, and the association of visible self-presentation with the part of the body that appears in a selfie frame. A clothing buyer making a purchase decision partly on the basis of how they will appear in a specific digital format is not irrational. They are extremely rational about a cultural context that a retailer whose category structure was built in the 1990s may not have updated to understand.

The trust dimension in blue-collar India — documented in 'The Middle India consumer' (2022) — is cultural as much as institutional. The PF invisibility that Smartstaff solved was not just an administrative failure. It reflected a systemic pattern in which the formal economy had not bothered to make itself legible to blue-collar workers, and those workers had, rationally, extended low trust to institutions that had not earned the alternative. When Smartstaff made PF balances visible, it was not providing a feature. It was making a gesture of respect — treating the worker as someone whose financial life was worth making legible. That emotional transaction preceded the commercial one.

**The Systems View**

Consumer behaviour and retail in India cannot be understood outside of the infrastructure, policy, and ecosystem conditions that make specific behaviours possible or impossible. FF's reporting consistently embeds consumer behaviour in these broader systems.

The GST and logistics transformation documented in 'Noel Tata's brahmastra' (2023) is the clearest recent example. Before GST rationalisation and the build-out of third-party warehousing, the retail hinterland was logistically unviable for organised retail. The aspiration was there. The infrastructure was not. Zudio's expansion into Jharsuguda and Gorakhpur is not primarily a consumer insight story. It is a story about what becomes possible when the supply chain infrastructure catches up with the demographic map. The consumer behaviour was a latent variable waiting for a logistics solution.

The Jio infrastructure story — never named directly in FF's reporting but present in the background of every article about TikTok, about CRBTs, about the middle India digital consumer — is the same structure. CRBTs as early digital self-expression, documented in 'From Bazaar to Breakthrough' (2025), were a user-discovered use case enabled by a specific infrastructure moment: first-time mobile phone ownership, limited data, English-unfriendly interfaces. The QR code at the sugarcane juice vendor is enabled by cheap data and smartphone penetration. These are not consumer choice stories. They are infrastructure stories with consumer behaviour as the visible outcome.

The EV charging infrastructure gap — documented in 'What they don't tell you about EVs in India' (2024) — is a systems story about how a specific policy and infrastructure failure creates a specific consumer behaviour barrier. The apartment society governance problem (negotiating charger installation rights) is a second-order effect of a first-order infrastructure gap (inadequate public charging network). The consumer who would like to buy an EV is blocked not by preference but by a coordination problem that requires a policy solution, not a product solution. Understanding this is the difference between a consumer insight and a systems insight.

India's ageing population technology problem — documented in 'The consumers technology is leaving behind' (2019) — is a systems failure with multiple causes. The design assumption of youth and digital fluency is built into product development processes. The investment logic that rewards products targeting large, digitally active, English-literate segments reproduces the assumption at the funding level. The "expectations gap between what older consumers want from a product and what most of these products deliver" (citing Joseph Coughlin) is not an individual product design failure. It is a systems failure: the product development ecosystem, the investment ecosystem, and the design education ecosystem all point away from the 340-million-person market that will exist by 2050.

The food system transformation visible in the millets story — documented in 'FF Life: The golden seed' (2023) — is a second-order effects story. Individual health consciousness is the proximate driver of urban millet consumption. But the three factors FF identifies — production with fewer resources, resilience to climate change, wellness through food — converge on a single product in ways that create second-order agricultural and ecological effects. A consumer choosing millets for personal health is participating, whether they know it or not, in a transformation of the agricultural supply chain that has implications for farmer income, groundwater use, and climate resilience. Consumer behaviour at scale becomes agricultural policy. The systems view makes this legible; the individual consumer transaction does not.

The engagement economy — documented in 'A nudge against sleep and productivity' (2019) by Charles Assisi — is a systems critique. Reed Hastings's "we compete with sleep" is not a marketing boast. It is a statement about the architecture of attention capture at scale. The cocaine dealer analogy — "the first shot is always free" — points to a systems problem: platforms optimised for engagement maximisation are not optimising for user welfare. The nudge against productivity and sleep is not an edge case. It is the product's core mechanism. FF notes that the breakup debate was alive in the US but had not gained public attention in India, which was then (and remains) the largest market for these companies outside the US. The systems view here is that the absence of regulatory attention is not evidence of no problem. It is evidence of a specific regulatory failure.

**The Evolution of FF's Thinking**

FF's earliest coverage — the 2015 batch — is primarily prescriptive and framework-oriented. The articles on behavioural science, brand experience, customer loyalty, and prosumer dynamics are written as practical guides: here are the tools, here is how Amazon and Zara use them, here is what you should do. The India dimension is present but episodic. Patanjali gets serious analytical attention in 2016. The selfie economy and TikTok coverage in 2019 marks a shift toward ethnographic observation as primary method.

By 2020, the pandemic changes the texture of FF's consumer coverage significantly. The comfort brands analysis, the home cooking nostalgia piece, and the mascara effect article are all written with greater emotional depth than the 2015 pieces. The consumer is no longer primarily an analytical subject. The human stakes are visible. "What is painfully obvious to most of us now is that our lives have changed forever," as noted in 'FF Daily #213: FF Recommends | Personal mobility' (2020). The crisis created the conditions for more honest observation.

The 2022–2025 period shows FF's most sophisticated consumer thinking. The middle India consumer framework — documented in 'The Middle India consumer' (2022) — represents a structural attempt to name a demographic and behavioural category that mainstream analysis was ignoring. The nuance argument — 'Nuance is the new differentiator' (2025) — is FF at its most skeptical of its own earlier frameworks: the data-driven, funnel-optimised approach to consumer understanding is explicitly named as insufficient, and the alternative (contextual, emotional, behavioural observation) is defended as necessary precisely because AI and data tools are making the data-driven approach faster and more confident in ways that may widen the gap with reality.

The emotions-as-first-principles argument in 'Why Emotions Are First Principles in Product Strategy' (2025) by Shrinath V is the clearest statement of where FF's consumer thinking has arrived. It is further from the 2015 toolkit pieces than it might appear. The 2015 pieces treated behavioural science as a set of tools to deploy — loss aversion, scarcity effect, social proof. The 2025 piece treats emotional truth as the starting point of product strategy, not a feature of it. The evolution is from "use these levers to influence consumer behaviour" to "understand what the consumer is carrying emotionally before you design anything." These are different postures with different implications.

What FF no longer holds, relative to its 2015–2016 coverage: the confidence that the framework from elsewhere — experience economy, behavioural science toolkit, prosumer model — transfers directly to India with modest localisation. The later coverage is more willing to say that India requires a different starting point, not just a different application of the same starting point.

**Key Voices**

**Indrajit Gupta** is FF's most important voice on the structural dynamics of Indian retail and consumer markets. His 'The Patanjali phenomenon' (2016) remains the most analytically complete account of that disruption — it refuses both the dismissal ("it'll peter out") and the hagiography, and instead traces the structural conditions that made Patanjali possible and durable. His 'Noel Tata's brahmastra' (2023) on Zudio is equally rigorous: it uses the Wharton "Turns vs. Earns" retailer typology to explain what Zudio is doing operationally, connects it to macro-infrastructure changes (GST, logistics), and frames it as a strategic insight about where Indian aspiration actually lives. Gupta's distinctive angle is structural patience: he waits for evidence to accumulate before making the argument.

**Charles Assisi** brings a humanist sensibility to consumer coverage that distinguishes FF's approach from conventional business journalism. His 'The mascara effect' (2020) is genuinely unusual — it extends the lipstick effect through Yad Vashem documentation of beauty rituals in concentration camps to make an argument about the durability of identity maintenance as a consumption driver. His 'Death of the white shirt?' (2019) connects selfie culture to retail category data to EMI financing to the body-as-temple metaphor — a synthesis no single discipline would have produced. His 'A nudge against sleep and productivity' (2019) is FF's most pointed ethical critique of platform engagement design. Assisi's distinctive angle is the willingness to follow an insight from data to its human implications, without stopping at the commercially convenient point.

**Piyul Mukherjee** represents FF's most important ethnographic voice on Bharat. The TikTok pieces from 2019 — 'Bharat is watching this. Is your brand watching too?' and 'Please look now: We are changing' — are based on direct observation in small-town India, not on platform analytics or media-buyer data. The Shivani Kapila archetype — the split-modernity figure whose surface behaviour and deep value system move at different speeds — is Mukherjee's most enduring analytical contribution. Her work makes the case, by example, for the ethnographic method in contexts where quantitative data systematically misses what matters.

**Shrinath V** represents FF's most recent and most sophisticated analytical voice on consumer and product strategy. His three major pieces from 2025 — 'From Bazaar to Breakthrough', 'Nuance is the new differentiator', and 'Why Emotions Are First Principles in Product Strategy' — form a coherent argument: India's consumer behaviour requires contextual, emotional, and adaptive understanding; standard analytics and AI tools are making this worse, not better; emotions are not soft factors but the primary architecture of purchase decisions. His distinctive angle is the integration of cognitive science, behavioural observation, and Indian market specificity in a way that produces actionable frameworks rather than just analytical observations.

**Rajesh Srivastava** provided FF's earliest and most direct engagement with India-specific consumer economics. His 2016 piece on iPhone SE and Amazon bookstores — 'Why iPhone SE may lose out in India and why Amazon wants to open bookstores' — established the calculator-fitted-in-the-mind framing of Indian price sensitivity that remains one of the most precise descriptions of how Indian consumers relate to pricing decisions. His work was more prescriptive than later contributors, but it established the India-specificity of consumer behaviour as a standing concern, not an occasional observation.

**Harsh Mall** contributed the comfort brands and mascara effect framing through 'I want my MTV' (2020), connecting pandemic consumer behaviour to the long history of comfort brand surges in downturns. His work on crisis consumption establishes that consumer behaviour in extremity is not aberrant — it is a window into what consumption is for when stripped of aspiration and convenience.

**What FF Is Skeptical Of**

FF is skeptical of advertising's declining ROI, documented consistently from 2015 through the brand advocacy pieces to the buzz-economy arguments. The specific claim — "advertising investment is governed by the law of diminishing returns" — is made in 'Brand friend for life' (2015) and is supported by Nielsen's 92% earned media trust figure. FF does not argue that advertising is useless. It argues that advertising at scale, with high spend and interruptive placement, produces negative associations at the margin while delivering less value than earned advocacy. This is not a popular position for a media company to hold, which makes FF's consistency on it more notable.

FF is skeptical of the "e-commerce kills physical retail" narrative — documented through MINISO coverage and through the Zudio physical expansion story. The argument is not nostalgic. It is analytical: physical retail that solves a different problem than e-commerce — experience, immediate gratification, trust for first-time buyers, social shopping contexts — can and does survive. Physical retail that simply sells the same product at a higher price cannot.

FF is skeptical of "data as the solution to consumer insight," and this skepticism has intensified in the 2025 coverage. 'Nuance is the new differentiator' (2025) makes the sharpest version of this argument: AI tools entering the research process may widen the gap between data confidence and human truth. The 2015 piece 'Building consumer empathy' made a similar argument about sample-size obsession in Indian advertising research. The skepticism is not anti-data. It is anti-data-as-substitute-for-human-context.

FF is skeptical of speed as an inherent virtue in consumer services — most clearly in the Zomato 10-minute delivery critique in 'FF Insights #616: Slow food' (2022). The critique is not primarily that 10-minute delivery is operationally difficult. It is that the demand for 10-minute delivery reflects a cultural pathology — a destruction of the social and sensory dimension of eating — that the industry is incentivised to enable rather than question.

FF is skeptical of tech-first solutions to fundamentally human problems — documented through SilverGenie's experience in 'Why Emotions Are First Principles in Product Strategy' (2025). The seniors didn't want apps, smartwatches, or sensors. "Wearing a smartwatch felt like being monitored. It wasn't restoring anything." The tech-first frame, in this case, was producing products that solved the wrong problem. FF's position is that the emotional understanding must precede the technical solution, not follow from it.

FF is skeptical of Ola Electric's quality culture — quoting industry expert Hormazd Sorabjee directly in 'AMA on EVs' (2024): "Ola, frankly—unfortunately, the company doesn't have a quality culture. It's all about getting products out fast." This is a specifically uncomfortable form of skepticism, because Ola Electric represents a narrative that the Indian technology industry has invested heavily in — the bold, fast-moving domestic EV champion. FF's willingness to name the quality failure directly, attributed to a credible source, is characteristic of its approach.

---

**What FF Celebrates**

FF celebrates operational intelligence in service of genuine consumer value — not clever marketing that masks a weak product, but companies that build their advantage into the structure of what they do.

Zudio earns extended celebration in 'Noel Tata's brahmastra' (2023) for exactly this: its low prices are not a promotional device. They are the result of a specific operational model — high turns, own-label design, hinterland locations — that creates a structural cost advantage passed to consumers. The aspiration alignment is genuine: Zudio is in the towns where the aspiring young Indian is, not waiting for that consumer to come to the mall. The celebration is of operational integrity matched to market insight.

Amazon's anticipatory strategy — documented across the 2015 coverage — is celebrated not for its scale but for its posture. "Admired enterprises do not wait for trouble to raise their ugly heads; they pick up issues at a nascent stage." The Pay to Quit policy, anticipatory shipping, and personalisation algorithms are celebrated as examples of systematic consumer understanding embedded into operations, not just expressed in advertising.

Razorpay's trust-building through content — documented in 'The Middle India consumer' (2022) — is celebrated for its sequencing: trust before product adoption, utility before commercial relationship. This is an approach that most large organisations' KPI structures would not allow, which makes it more admirable when it happens.

Meesho's trust architecture — using women entrepreneurs as intermediaries — is celebrated for the structural insight: in markets where institutional trust is low, the solution is not to build institutional trust directly but to route through existing human trust networks. The model's elegance is that it serves the business objective (market penetration) and the social objective (women's entrepreneurship) simultaneously.

SilverGenie and Poulomi Bhattacharya are celebrated in 'Why Emotions Are First Principles in Product Strategy' (2025) for the methodological courage of following the emotional evidence to a counterintuitive product insight — that the buyer was the adult child, not the elderly person — against investor pressure to frame the product as "deep tech."

Paper Boat is celebrated, implicitly through the nostalgia framework coverage in 'Paper Boat: Riding on a wave of nostalgia' (2016), for commercialising emotional memory at a product level. The seasonal specificity — aam panna, jamun kala khatta — creates authentic scarcity and authentic nostalgia simultaneously, rather than engineering either one.

**The Historical Lens**

FF uses history to establish that what looks like new consumer behaviour is often old human behaviour in a new technical context — and that the newness is usually less important than the underlying continuity.

The mascara effect's historical reach — extended in 'The mascara effect' (2020) to concentration camp beauty rituals at Yad Vashem — establishes that identity maintenance through personal grooming is not a modern consumption phenomenon. It is a fundamental human response to stress that has been observed in extremity. The historical extension makes the contemporary observation more robust: this is not a quirk of affluent Western consumer behaviour. It is a cross-cultural human constant.

The slow food movement's historical context — traced in 'FF Insights #616: Slow food' (2022) through Carl Honore's 'In Praise of Slow' — connects Zomato's 10-minute delivery debate to a much longer story about what industrialisation did to the relationship between humans and food. "Hurry took its place at the dinner table during the Industrial Revolution." The current debate about food delivery speed is not a new argument. It is the same argument that has been running since the invention of fast food, now compressed to 10 minutes.

The millet history in 'FF Life: The golden seed' (2023) is the full-circle argument: a crop that fed India's rural poor for centuries, was stigmatised as poor people's food when the Green Revolution elevated wheat and rice as aspirational staples, and is now returning as a premium urban product precisely because of the nutritional and ecological properties that were always there. The historical lens prevents the narrative of "rediscovery" — as if millets were new — and instead reveals the ideological work that went into making them seem inferior in the first place.

The CRBT story in 'From Bazaar to Breakthrough' (2025) uses technological history to make an argument about Indian digital adoption. First-generation mobile users' use of caller ring-back tones as digital self-expression predates smartphones by years. The improvisation of digital identity in the absence of apps designed for it is not a new Indian behaviour. It is a continuation of bazaar-logic adaptation — using what is available to meet needs the designer didn't anticipate.

The Patanjali disruption, traced carefully in 'The Patanjali phenomenon' (2016), has historical antecedents in the Swadeshi movement — the association of Indian-made goods with cultural identity and national pride. Baba Ramdev's yoga platform provides the distribution mechanism for a trust signal that has always carried weight in certain Indian households. The historical lens makes Patanjali legible as a cultural phenomenon, not just a brand strategy — which is precisely why FMCG managers trained on Western brand frameworks found it hard to respond to.

**Unresolved Tensions**

The most important unresolved tension in FF's consumer coverage is between the case for deep qualitative consumer understanding and the practical reality that most organisations — and most markets — operate on scale and speed. The ethnographic method, the emotional insight-first approach, the nuance argument: these are compelling. They are also slow, expensive, and difficult to operationalise at the scale at which Indian consumer businesses need to move. The principles articulated by Shrinath V in 2025 are not easily translated into a brief for a 200-person analytics team. FF has not resolved this tension. It is unlikely to be resolved, because it is structural.

The second unresolved tension is between the celebration of low-price, high-access models (Zudio, Meesho) and the concern about unsustainability in fast fashion and supply chain ethics. Zudio is celebrated for operational excellence and hinterland aspiration alignment. Shein is critiqued for environmental damage, poor fabric quality, and labour practices. Both are low-price, high-volume models. The line between the two is not always as clear as the coverage implies. FF has not fully worked out what distinguishes a celebrated low-price model from a criticised one, beyond the specific quality of the product and the sourcing practices. The tension between access and sustainability in Indian mass-market retail is real and not resolved.

The third unresolved tension is between the engagement economy critique — platform design as addictive by architecture — and the celebration of platforms like TikTok that demonstrated those same dynamics in their Bharat penetration. The 2019 TikTok coverage celebrates the platform's democratisation of self-expression. The 2019 engagement economy piece ('A nudge against sleep and productivity') critiques the ethics of the same design logic. Both are true. FF has not resolved how to think about platforms that are simultaneously empowering and extractive, or how the power equation changes in markets where the extractive architecture is applied to users with less institutional protection and fewer regulatory alternatives.

The fourth tension is between the comfort-brands-in-crisis observation — that consumers return to known, trusted, large brands in downturns — and the broader argument that challenger brands with cultural resonance (Patanjali, Paper Boat) are taking share from exactly those large brands. Both are documented in FF's coverage. The crisis playbook says comfort brands win. The structural disruption playbook says cultural challengers win. Which one is right depends on whether you are looking at the short-term (crisis) or the long-term (structural shift). But in practice, most consumer businesses are managing both simultaneously, and FF has not provided a framework for navigating the tension.

---

**The Missing Voices**

The most significant gap in FF's consumer coverage is the consumer's own voice at scale across linguistic and cultural contexts beyond Hindi-English-metropolitan observation. The TikTok and middle India pieces are notable precisely because they go beyond the metropolitan frame. But the vast majority of FF's consumer coverage is urban, English-speaking, and centred on the purchase behaviour of consumers who are already integrated into the formal market economy. The rural consumer — not the aspirational semi-urban consumer, but the genuinely rural consumer whose consumption patterns, trust architecture, and product relationships operate in entirely different parameters — is largely absent.

The female consumer in non-metro India is under-documented. Meesho's women entrepreneurs appear in the coverage, but as distribution intermediaries, not as primary consumers whose behaviour, preferences, and constraints deserve analytical attention in their own right. The selfie economy piece tracks retail category shifts in Mumbai. The modernity paradox piece features Shivani Kapila from Surat. These are valuable observations. They do not constitute a systematic account of how women in non-metro India make consumption decisions, what emotional and cultural constraints they operate within, and how those constraints are changing.

The elderly consumer — mentioned in 'The consumers technology is leaving behind' (2019) — appears as an identified gap but not as a voice. The analysis is correct: design is failing elderly users, there is a 340-million-person market by 2050, the expectations gap is real. But the coverage is done looking at elderly users, not with them. Their own articulation of what they want, what frustrates them, and how they navigate a world designed for younger bodies and minds is absent.

The sustainability-conscious consumer — a growing segment across income levels in urban India — is present in the green consumption piece and the millet story but not examined as a distinct behavioural and attitudinal type in the Indian context. What does environmental concern look like in a market where many consumers are only recently transitioning to formal, organised retail? Is the green premium viable at Zudio price points? These questions are unasked.

Voices from retail workers — the frontline people who actually implement the strategies that FF covers — are entirely absent. The experience of the Meesho entrepreneur, the Zudio store associate in Gorakhpur, the delivery partner for a 10-minute food delivery are structurally important to understanding how consumer strategies actually work at the point of execution. FF's coverage is overwhelmingly of the people who design strategies, not the people who carry them out.

---

**What This Means For You**

**The belief to update:** You probably believe that if you have enough data — enough purchase history, enough session data, enough cohort analysis — you know your consumer. The evidence in FF's coverage, from the COD trust problem to the Patanjali disruption to the SilverGenie elder care mis-read to TikTok's invisible Bharat penetration, says that data describes what people did, not why they did it, and "why" is where the competitive insight lives. Your conversion funnel is not a consumer understanding. It is a record of what happened the last time. The consumer's emotional context — what they are carrying, what they fear, what they are trying to prove to themselves and others — is not in the funnel. You need a method for getting at it that is not the dashboard.

**The thing to stop:** Stop competing on price as your primary strategic response to competitive pressure. 'FF Daily #463: The price game' (2021) makes this argument directly through Simon Sinek: "Selling based on price is like heroin. The short-term gain is fantastic, but the more you do it, the harder it becomes to kick the habit." This applies to Indian consumer businesses at every scale. The reflex to drop price when a competitor enters, to run discounts to hit quarterly numbers, to fund growth through margin compression — each of these choices makes the next choice harder. Zudio is low-priced through operational discipline, not through margin sacrifice. That is the distinction. If your low price is not the outcome of structural advantage, it is a loan you will eventually have to repay with market position.

**The thing to start:** Identify one consumer segment that your organisation currently serves primarily through data and metrics, and arrange one day per quarter of direct, unstructured, in-context observation — not a focus group, not a survey, not a usability test. Go to where the consumer actually is and watch what they do. This is what Future Group's 'Future Ideas' cell did, as noted in 'Building consumer empathy' (2015). It is what Piyul Mukherjee did in small-town India that produced the TikTok insight two years before the market caught up. It is what revealed the COD trust architecture in middle India that pure funnel data was misreading. One day of direct observation, done with genuine curiosity and without a predetermined hypothesis, will surface something that your dashboard has been missing. The discipline is not the observation itself — the discipline is going without already knowing what you're looking for.

The conviction to carry out of this page: the consumer is always several steps ahead of your model of them, and in India, that gap is wider, changes faster, and operates at more registers simultaneously than any dashboard you will ever build can fully capture — which means the practitioner who maintains a direct, humble, curious relationship with the human behind the transaction will always have an edge over the one who trusts the data to do the understanding for them.
