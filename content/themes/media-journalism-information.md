# Media, Journalism & Information

---

## PART 1 — THE OPENING

The problem isn't that Indians are misinformed. The problem is that the machinery producing misinformation has become indistinguishable from the machinery producing journalism. Walk into any newsroom — print, digital, broadcast — and you'll find the same economic logic at work: advertisers first, access next, truth somewhere further down the list. The business model is not a support structure for journalism. It has become journalism's replacement.

This matters because the failure is invisible from the outside. Headlines still run. Anchors still shout. Bylines still appear. The form of journalism persists while the function — holding power accountable, informing citizens, naming what is actually true — has been quietly traded away. Access journalism means journalists who need tomorrow's quote can't file today's story. WhatsApp forwards reach a hundred million people who believe group membership confers insider knowledge. Subscription models, once held up as the clean alternative to ad-dependence, turn out to create their own pathology: people pay to have their existing beliefs confirmed. Every business model has its own way of corrupting the product.

Meanwhile, the information environment compounds the crisis. Social media doesn't create filter bubbles through clickbait — it creates them through lies and hatred, and these travel faster than corrections. India's scale makes this uniquely dangerous: the country that produces the most WhatsApp forwards is also the country where a rural voter's entire political information diet may arrive through a WhatsApp group administered by a party worker.

This page will not tell you that journalism is important and social media is bad. You already know that. What it will give you is a precise account of how the ecosystem actually works, which corruptions are structural rather than ethical, what India's specific vulnerabilities and advantages are, and what the evidence shows about what — if anything — can be done.

## PART 2 — PRINCIPLES

---

### Principle 1

> Bullshit is more dangerous than lying, because liars still respect truth enough to hide from it.

**What This Means & Why**
A liar knows what is true and works against it — which means truth remains the reference point. A bullshitter has abandoned that reference point entirely. They are not saying false things; they are saying things with no relationship to whether they are true or false. This distinction matters enormously for how you detect and counter information pollution. When you try to fact-check a lie, you can win. When you try to fact-check bullshit, you've already lost the frame, because the bullshitter was never playing a truth game.

**The India Reality**
WhatsApp forwards, political speeches, and large portions of social media discourse in India operate almost entirely in bullshit mode — not lies constructed to contradict a known truth, but assertions made with complete indifference to evidential grounding. The volume and velocity of the medium ensures that most recipients never pause to ask whether the claim has a truth-value at all.

**The Evidence**
In 'FF Daily #370: Truth, lies, and bullshit' (2021), Founding Fuel applies Harry Frankfurt's philosophical distinction between lying and bullshitting to the Twitter and WhatsApp ecosystem. Frankfurt's argument — "Bullshit is unavoidable whenever circumstances require someone to talk without knowing what he is talking about" — is presented as the precise diagnosis of social media discourse, where speaking constantly is the norm and knowing what you're talking about is optional.

**The Implication**
When you encounter a claim on WhatsApp or Twitter, don't ask "is this true or false?" — ask first whether the person making it cares about the distinction, and respond accordingly.

### Principle 2

> Access journalism doesn't corrupt media occasionally — it is the operating system of most Indian business journalism.

**What This Means & Why**
Access journalism is not a lapse in professional ethics; it is a structural outcome of how beat reporting works when sources control information flow. The journalist who files a critical story loses tomorrow's access. The journalist who softens today's story gets tomorrow's exclusive. Over time, this selection pressure eliminates critical reporters from beats and leaves only those who have made peace with the exchange. The reader never sees the bargain — only its results.

**The India Reality**
Indian business journalism operates with particularly high access concentration: a small number of powerful conglomerates, promoter families, and political-business nexuses control most of the material that makes a business story. When a tycoon can demand cover placement before agreeing to speak to a "prominent business magazine" — and get it — the editorial function has already been surrendered.

**The Evidence**
In 'Confessions of a practising journalist' (2015), Charles Assisi names the mechanics explicitly: journalists who challenge powerful sources find access denied; junket culture trades favorable coverage for invitations; digital outlets solicit payment for positive features. Assisi describes named archetypes — infrastructure magnates who control document pipelines, promoters who demand editorial quid pro quo — without the euphemism that usually accompanies such accounts. The piece remains one of the most direct accounts of access journalism's operating logic written from inside an Indian newsroom.

**The Implication**
When reading Indian business coverage of a major conglomerate, ask not only what the story says but what story would have required losing access to write — and whether that story has been written anywhere.

### Principle 3

> Slower information is more valuable than faster information — and this is not a preference, it is an epistemological fact.

**What This Means & Why**
The news cycle creates the illusion that speed equals information. In practice, early reports are most often wrong, context-free, or actively misleading. The event that seems significant at 9am is frequently revised, superseded, or reframed by 6pm. A weekly summary is more accurate than seven daily updates precisely because it has been through one round of correction. A book is more accurate than the weekly summary for the same reason. Speed is a manufacturing choice that trades accuracy for immediacy — and the trade is rarely disclosed.

**The India Reality**
India's rolling news culture — driven by 24-hour news channels competing for first-byte advantage — has produced a journalism where being wrong first is considered less damaging than being right second. The cost of this to Indian public understanding of complex events — demonetisation, the Covid surge, electoral vote counts — has been substantial and largely unmeasured.

**The Evidence**
In 'FF Daily #392: News vs. meaningful information' (2021), Founding Fuel presents Tim Harford's argument that "daily news always seems more informative than rolling news; weekly news is typically more informative than daily news. A book is often better still." Nassim Taleb's prescription is also cited: "To be completely cured of newspapers, spend a year reading the previous week's newspapers." FF explicitly positions itself as the slow-information alternative: "It's a view we hope you get from following Founding Fuel."

**The Implication**
Replace one daily news habit — a WhatsApp group, a news app's breaking news alerts, a TV morning show — with one weekly long-form source and measure the difference in your actual understanding of events after one month.

### Principle 4

> Transparency is not integrity — and mistaking one for the other makes the corruption worse.

**What This Means & Why**
Transparency creates the appearance of accountability without requiring the substance. An organisation can disclose everything and still behave badly — in fact, disclosure can become a tool of legitimacy laundering, signaling honesty while the disclosed facts are arranged to obscure rather than reveal. Worse, the promise of transparency shifts the burden to the reader to find wrongdoing in a haystack of disclosed information, rather than requiring the institution to be honest in the first place.

**The India Reality**
India's regulatory environment produces enormous volumes of mandated disclosure — SEBI filings, RBI circulars, Ministry press releases — while permitting opacity about the facts that would actually change understanding. The result is a culture where compliance with disclosure requirements is treated as equivalent to transparency, which is treated as equivalent to trustworthiness. None of those equivalences holds.

**The Evidence**
In 'FF Daily #255: The dark side of transparency' (2020), Founding Fuel quotes Clay Johnson: "Clinging to transparency as a replacement for integrity is a bad idea... if you turn the lights on in a roach-infested apartment, it doesn't kill the roaches, it just makes them organize in the shadows." The point is extended: "You can simply claim to be transparent, and create a halo of honesty about you, without actually being honest."

**The Implication**
When evaluating a company's or institution's communications, stop asking "have they disclosed this?" and start asking "what would they need to be true to explain what they haven't disclosed?"

### Principle 5

> Social media's danger is not clickbait — it is lies and hatred, which are algorithmically rewarded by the same platforms that claim to fight them.

**What This Means & Why**
Clickbait is an attention problem — it promises more than it delivers. The clickbait critic treats the problem as one of misleading packaging. But the actual crisis is content that is not merely sensational but structurally designed to produce outrage, which produces engagement, which produces revenue. Lies and narratives of hatred outperform accurate content on every engagement metric, which means every ad-funded platform has a financial incentive to distribute them. This is not a moderation failure — it is a business model feature.

**The India Reality**
The Yogi Adityanath "abba jaan" episode is instructive: a narrative of victimhood and communal othering, deployed on social media with no mainstream media challenge, reaching millions before any correction circulated. The political use of WhatsApp in Indian elections — where rural voters treated group membership as insider access — shows the mechanism at mass scale, in a country where the platforms have weaker content moderation infrastructure than their Western counterparts.

**The Evidence**
In 'Politics, the business of news, and the Radia tapes' (2021), Founding Fuel quotes Vir Sanghvi directly: "The danger of social media doesn't come from clickbait, but from lies, hatred and bogus victims" and "On social media, a narrative of victimhood and hatred does well." The same observation about the "abba jaan" episode is cited as a case where mainstream media's failure to call out political disinformation left the social media narrative unchallenged.

**The Implication**
When a platform claims to be fighting misinformation through fact-checking labels or third-party review, examine whether its recommendation algorithm rewards or penalises content that generates outrage — because the moderation layer is cosmetic if the business model layer is unchanged.

### Principle 6

> Both ad-driven and subscription-driven media models are structurally compromised — each in a different and less visible way.

**What This Means & Why**
The media industry's response to the collapse of advertising has been to promote subscriptions as the ethical alternative. Subscriptions, the argument goes, align incentives with readers rather than advertisers. What this argument misses is that subscription models create their own corruption: people pay to have their existing beliefs confirmed, which means subscription-funded media is financially rewarded for telling its audience what it already thinks. The reader gets validation; the publisher gets revenue; the truth gets edited to fit.

**The India Reality**
Only two or three Indian digital publishers crossed Rs 100 crore in annual revenues in the early 2020s — the subscription model is still nascent here, which means India is making the structural choices now that will determine which pathology it builds in. The pattern of ad-model corruption (the Times of India's advertiser-first editorial logic) is well-documented; the subscription-model corruption is arriving with the next wave.

**The Evidence**
In 'How tech firms are eating media' (2021), NS Ramnath presents Andrey Mir's argument from *Postjournalism*: "subscription models have led to polarisation because people essentially pay for media platforms to validate, confirm their own beliefs." The article pairs this with an observation about Indian media's FDI restrictions, which constrain media startups while allowing tech platforms to operate freely — creating a structurally unequal competitive environment.

**The Implication**
If you are funding a media organisation — through subscription, investment, or advertising — ask explicitly which corruption its business model is most susceptible to, and what editorial safeguards exist against that specific failure mode.

### Principle 7

> The curator's role is not aggregation — it is creation, filtration, and leadership away from noise.

**What This Means & Why**
In a world flooded with content, curation is treated as a lesser function — as if the curator merely finds what already exists, while the creator makes something from nothing. This misunderstands what curation actually does. A skilled curator creates meaning by selecting against the default; they filter by applying judgment that most readers cannot exercise at volume; they lead by pulling attention toward what matters and away from what merely circulates. These are active intellectual contributions, not passive administrative ones.

**The India Reality**
India's information environment is among the highest-volume in the world — the country that produces the most WhatsApp messages, the most YouTube content, the most vernacular digital publications. In this environment, the scarcity is not content but reliable curation. The business models for curation remain underdeveloped; the cultural status of curators remains lower than creators.

**The Evidence**
In 'The necessity of curators' (2019), Charles Assisi argues directly: "In a noisy world where everyone imagines themselves as content producers, algorithms included, curators play some crucial roles... They create... They filter... They lead." The piece makes the case that curation is not downstream of content creation but parallel to it — and that in high-noise environments, the curator's function is more valuable, not less.

**The Implication**
Identify one person or publication whose judgment you trust as a filter, invest in following them more deeply, and deliberately reduce the number of raw sources you consume directly.

### Principle 8

> The media's failure is not ethical but structural — individual journalists rarely choose corruption; the system selects for it.

**What This Means & Why**
The temptation is to frame media failure as a story of individuals making bad choices: the journalist who took the junket, the editor who softened the story, the anchor who performed outrage for ratings. These people exist. But the structural account is more useful and more honest: these outcomes are what you get when you build a journalism business on advertising dependence, access preservation, and competition for eyeballs. The individuals are doing what the system rewards. Reform that targets individuals while leaving the system intact changes nothing.

**The India Reality**
The Times of India's advertiser-first model is the clearest Indian example: Samir Jain's editorial logic was not a secret or an aberration — it was a strategy, openly discussed, that redefined what Indian newspaper editors were for. The strategy worked commercially. What it did to Indian journalism's accountability function is a separate and largely unasked question.

**The Evidence**
In 'Politics, the business of news, and the Radia tapes' (2021), Founding Fuel presents Vir Sanghvi's candid account of the Times of India model: "a Times of India creation" that was explicitly advertiser-first. Sanghvi describes how competitive pressure — Outlook's declining circulation — drove editorial decisions including publishing the Radia tapes. The decision that looked like accountability journalism was also a competitive business move. Both things are true, and the conflation of them is the structural point.

**The Implication**
When you encounter journalism that serves power, before attributing it to individual cowardice, map the economic relationship between the publication and the subject — because the story usually begins there.

### Principle 9

> In India, WhatsApp is not a messaging platform — it is the primary political information architecture for rural voters, and it runs on manufactured credibility.

**What This Means & Why**
Western frameworks for thinking about social media misinformation focus on Facebook newsfeeds, Twitter virality, YouTube recommendations — open, visible, algorithmically driven. WhatsApp is different: it is closed, personal, trusted. A message arrives not from a stranger but from a group you were added to — which signals that someone who knows you considered this worth sharing. This social endorsement is the credibility mechanism, and it is almost impossible to dislodge with a fact-check because the trust is relational, not evidential.

**The India Reality**
With over 200 million users at its peak, India is WhatsApp's largest market. The political parties who understood this earliest built WhatsApp group networks that reached rural voters with messaging explicitly calibrated to feel like insider information. The voter who received a BJP or Congress WhatsApp forward in 2019 was more likely to believe it than the same voter receiving the same claim via television, because the channel itself implied personal endorsement.

**The Evidence**
In 'WFH Daily #178: WhatsApp as a political platform' (2020), Founding Fuel quotes Shivam Shankar Singh directly: "Most voters believed that being added to a political WhatsApp group gave them access to some kind of insider information. Instead of being irritated by the messages, they read them with gusto, in the belief that they were receiving information from a credible source." Singh describes knowing "early on that WhatsApp would be an effective political platform because it allows for targeted delivery of information."

**The Implication**
If you are in communications, public affairs, or policy and you are not actively mapping what is circulating about your issue on WhatsApp — not Twitter, not mainstream media, WhatsApp — you do not know what the actual information environment looks like.

### Principle 10

> The craft of interviewing is a discipline, and silence is its most powerful tool — a fact that most practitioners never learn.

**What This Means & Why**
Most people who conduct interviews — journalists, analysts, researchers, executives — fill silence instinctively, because silence feels like failure. The result is that the interviewer talks, the subject relaxes, and the information that would have emerged under pressure of silence is never extracted. The most disciplined interviewers do the opposite: they ask the question and stop. The subject's need to fill the silence does the work. This is not a personality trait — it is a learnable, practicable technique.

**The India Reality**
Indian business media tends toward the reverential profile — the interview that confirms what the subject wants confirmed, conducted in the manner the subject prefers. The adversarial, silence-weaponising interview is the exception rather than the norm, partly because of access pressures and partly because the craft of interviewing is not formally developed in most Indian journalism training.

**The Evidence**
In 'WFH Daily #153: FF Recommends | 30 people to follow on Twitter' (2020) and 'FF Daily #431' (2021), Founding Fuel cites Robert Caro's method: "Interviews: silence is the weapon, silence and people's need to fill it—as long as the person isn't you, the interviewer." Caro's notation in his own notebooks — "SU" (Shut Up) — is held up as the discipline that produced the most revealing presidential biographies in American letters.

**The Implication**
In your next significant interview or high-stakes conversation, ask the most important question and then stop talking — count to ten internally if you must, but do not rescue the silence.

### Principle 11

> India's print market has a structural resilience that its digital competitors consistently underestimate — and a structural vulnerability they consistently ignore.

**What This Means & Why**
Digital-first observers have been declaring Indian print dead for fifteen years. They are wrong about the timing but right about the direction. The error is assuming that print's resilience reflects permanence rather than structure: vernacular newspapers serve readers without reliable internet, in languages where digital alternatives are thin, in communities where the physical newspaper has social functions beyond information delivery. The vulnerability they ignore is advertising: print's resilience is audience-side; its crisis is revenue-side.

**The India Reality**
Vernacular newspaper subscriptions bounced back to 88–90 percent of pre-Covid levels within a year of the pandemic — a recovery that surprised almost everyone. Yet the advertising model that funds those newspapers was collapsing simultaneously: print advertising fell 41 percent during Covid, and digital ad revenues overtook print for the first time. The audience is there; the money is leaving. That combination is uniquely dangerous.

**The Evidence**
In 'What the media won't tell you about the media' (2021), Founding Fuel presents the counterintuitive argument that print's advertising strength comes partly from premium audiences who have migrated to SVOD streaming and are no longer reachable via television — making print the vehicle of last resort for reaching them. At the same time, only two or three Indian digital publishers were crossing Rs 100 crore annually, showing that digital has not yet built the revenue infrastructure to replace what print is losing.

**The Implication**
If you are allocating marketing spend and have written off print as a legacy channel, segment your target audience by media consumption pattern before deciding — the premium audience you want may have exited digital news and returned to print.

### Principle 12

> Media's democratic function and its commercial function are in genuine conflict — and pretending otherwise is how institutions justify neglecting the former.

**What This Means & Why**
The media industry has spent decades resolving this conflict by redefining journalism as a product and readers as customers. Once that substitution is made, the "give customers what they want" logic becomes unchallengeable: it sounds like service, it sounds like responsiveness, it sounds like respect for the audience. What it actually does is relieve the institution of any obligation to produce journalism that readers might not want but that a functioning democracy requires — investigative pieces, accountability reporting, uncomfortable truths.

**The India Reality**
The Indian media environment has produced extreme versions of both failures: the entertainment-first model (conflict, celebrity, cricket as proxy war) and the access-capture model (positive coverage in exchange for proximity to power). The accountability journalism that does exist — the Indian Express's investigation of Chanda Kochhar, the independent YouTubers who covered the 2024 election from rural polling booths — is almost always structurally separate from the dominant commercial media.

**The Evidence**
In 'How the media has let down democracy' (2017), Arun Maira frames the conflict philosophically, citing a Michael Sandel seminar at Aspen in 2002 where Jerry Levin's "give customers what they want" defense was directly challenged: Sandel's drug dealer analogy — many people want hard drugs; does that justify supplying them? — is used to demolish the customer-preference justification for editorial choices. Maira asks: "How would citizens be engaged with the deeper issues of their societies if all they saw and read in the media was designed only to entertain them?"

**The Implication**
When evaluating a media organisation's editorial choices, apply the Sandel test: is this what readers want, or is this what citizens need — and when those differ, which does the organisation choose?

### Principle 13

> The information diet metaphor is not a metaphor — junk information produces the same cognitive outcomes as junk food produces physical ones.

**What This Means & Why**
The food analogy for information consumption is usually deployed loosely, as a general warning against too much low-quality content. The more precise version is more useful: junk food is specifically engineered to override satiety signals, to produce consumption beyond need, to be more satisfying in the moment than nutrition would be. Junk information works identically — engineered to produce engagement beyond the point of comprehension, satisfying the appetite for novelty and outrage while delivering no nutritional informational value. Quantity is the enemy of quality in both cases.

**The India Reality**
India produces and consumes information at a scale that makes the junk-versus-nutrition distinction particularly critical. The 24-hour news cycle, the WhatsApp forward ecosystem, the algorithm-optimised social feed — each of these is an engineered junk-information delivery system. The consumers who notice they feel anxious, stupid, or enraged after an hour of news consumption are accurately reading a signal: they have eaten badly.

**The Evidence**
In 'The ultimate guide to good journalism' (2020), Charles Assisi writes: "More is not good. Instead, it makes our minds stupid and depletes the soul — much like junk food makes our bodies obese." He extends this to challenge the conventional wisdom that more content consumption equals more intelligence: "Convention has it the more content we consume, the smarter we get. But if the food analogy be extrapolated here, how absurd this assertion is makes itself obvious." Clay Johnson's *The Information Diet* provides the theoretical framework cited in 'FF Daily #255: The dark side of transparency' (2020).

**The Implication**
Audit your information consumption this week the way you would audit your food intake — track source quality, not just volume, and eliminate the equivalent of processed snacks before adding more nutrition.

### Principle 14

> The Forbes contributor model revealed what happens when a brand-name platform decouples quality from publishing — and every platform faces the same temptation.

**What This Means & Why**
Forbes's decision to open its platform to unedited contributor content was framed as democratisation. What it actually produced was a platform for marketing dressed as journalism, scams dressed as business coverage, and incentive-misaligned contributors generating "low-grade clickbait, hot takes, and deceptive headlines" under a trusted masthead. The brand equity built over decades was used to launder content that the brand's original editorial standards would have rejected. This is not an accident — it is the predictable outcome of separating platform trust from content quality.

**The India Reality**
Indian digital media has seen variants of the same pattern: news sites that accept sponsored content without disclosure, platforms where PR firms pay for placement that appears as editorial, aggregators that republish press releases as journalism. The Forbes case is useful precisely because it shows the dynamic at full clarity — the brand did not fall; it was quietly emptied out.

**The Evidence**
In 'FF Insights #588' (2022), Founding Fuel presents a detailed critique of Forbes.com's contributor model: "Forbes suddenly became the easiest way for a marketer to get their message onto a brand-name site." The piece approvingly cites the characterisation of "Forbes.com as a platform for scams, grift and bad journalism," and notes that the economic incentives of the contributor model made quality deterioration structural rather than accidental.

**The Implication**
When you read a piece on a major platform that feels like marketing, check the author's contributor status, their other recent publications, and whether they have any economic relationship with the subject — because the platform's brand no longer guarantees that the editorial function was exercised.

### Principle 15

> Post-truth is not a political condition — it is a design outcome of systems built to maximise engagement over accuracy.

**What This Means & Why**
The "post-truth" framing suggests a cultural or moral shift — that something changed in people's relationship to facts. The more precise and more useful account is that the systems people use to receive information were redesigned, over two decades, to optimise for engagement rather than accuracy. Engagement correlates with outrage, novelty, and confirmation of existing beliefs. Accuracy correlates with none of these. The systems work exactly as designed; the "post-truth" discourse blames the users for what the designers built.

**The India Reality**
India's entry into mass digital information consumption coincided almost exactly with the period in which these systems were fully optimised for engagement. There was no pre-digital period of high-quality digital journalism to which people might return. India arrived into the information ecosystem at its worst-designed moment, at scale, with fewer institutional checks than older media democracies had built.

**The Evidence**
In 'Conversations in a post-truth world' (2019), Founding Fuel documents the civilisational weight of the problem: "With the power of amplification that rests with social media, alternative truths jostle with truth and often overshadow truth." NS Ramnath's tweet is quoted: "I have no idea how we got here, but we seem to have gotten to a place where one man's obvious fake news is another man's incontrovertible fact. It's one thing to disagree on opinions but quite another to disagree on facts."

**The Implication**
When you find yourself in a disagreement about facts — not opinions, facts — stop trying to correct the factual claim and start mapping the information source that produced the belief, because that is where the actual intervention needs to happen.

### Principle 16

> Anonymity in media is not primarily a problem to be regulated — it is a protection that serves both the person and the argument.

**What This Means & Why**
The campaign against anonymous accounts on social media is built on a legitimate concern — harassment, coordinated inauthenticity, state-sponsored influence operations. But the proposed cure (verified identity requirements) destroys the protection that anonymity provides: whistleblowers, dissidents, survivors of violence, and anyone whose truth-telling would cost them their job, safety, or freedom all depend on the ability to speak without attaching their name. The cure is worse than the disease when the disease is not anonymous speech but anonymous harassment, which requires a different remedy.

**The India Reality**
India's information environment includes both the abuses of anonymous accounts (coordinated trolling, communal incitement) and the genuine necessity of anonymous speech (journalists protecting sources, whistleblowers in corporate and political environments, survivors of crimes speaking about powerful perpetrators). A blanket identity requirement would silence the latter while barely inconveniencing the former, who can simply create new verified accounts under aliases.

**The Evidence**
In 'The importance of being anonymous' (2017), NS Ramnath invokes the Thirukkural — "intelligence is in seeing the truth irrespective of who says it" — to argue that anonymity protects "both the person and the message." Ramnath explicitly resists the SNP politician Alyn Smith's call for a ban on anonymous social media accounts as too blunt an instrument, while acknowledging the genuine misuse that drives the demand for such bans.

**The Implication**
When evaluating anonymous speech — a tip, a social media account, a leaked document — assess the content's internal coherence and verifiability rather than defaulting to dismissal because the source is unidentified.

### Principle 17

> India's 2024 election marked a structural shift in journalism — rural vox pop YouTubers replaced urban studio experts as the reliable signal, and mainstream media missed it entirely.

**What This Means & Why**
The 2024 Lok Sabha election produced results that almost every mainstream media prediction got wrong. The explanation is not primarily about polling methodology — it is about whose voices were being listened to. The studio expert in a Noida AC studio is structurally disconnected from the voter in a rural polling booth. The independent journalist with a phone camera and a YouTube channel, sitting at a tea stall in Uttar Pradesh, was in a different information environment altogether — and that environment turned out to be more accurate.

**The India Reality**
The mainstream TV channel consolidation around a single political narrative was so complete by 2024 that the alternative media ecosystem — independent YouTubers, regional social media journalists, independent newsletters — became not just a supplement to mainstream media but its functional replacement for anyone seeking an accurate picture of electoral ground reality.

**The Evidence**
In '13 Most Memorable Moments: Lok Sabha Elections 2024' (2024), Founding Fuel documents: "Where mainstream TV channels sang just one tune, YouTubers, social media and independent journalists brought us an alternative voice." The same article describes the election as marking "the mainstreaming of vox pop journalism" — rural voices at tea shops replacing "experts seated inside AC studios in Noida" — and expresses the hope that "media remembers its first and only job: to hold those in power accountable."

**The Implication**
If your understanding of any major Indian political or social event comes primarily from mainstream television, seek out at least two independent sources from the region where the event is happening before forming a judgment about what is actually occurring on the ground.

### Principle 18

> The craft of journalism is disappearing not because of the internet but because the institutions that sustained it — subeditors, rewriters, editors who held pieces for months — have been dismantled as inefficiencies.

**What This Means & Why**
The common explanation for journalism's quality decline is digital disruption: speed requirements, shrinking ad revenues, competition from social media. These are real pressures. But they operate through a specific mechanism that is rarely named: the elimination of the editorial infrastructure that enforced quality. Subeditors who caught errors, rewriters who improved prose, editors who held a story until it was right — these were the quality control layer of journalism. They were also expensive. They were cut first, because their value was invisible until they were gone.

**The India Reality**
The generation of Indian journalists trained in the 1970s and 1980s experienced a journalism culture where rewriting was the norm, where "shoe-leather reporting" was the expected method, where an article held for months before publication was a sign of editorial seriousness rather than dysfunction. That culture has been almost entirely replaced by one where speed is the primary editorial value and the subeditor is a luxury no publication can afford.

**The Evidence**
In 'Remembering P.G. Mathai' (2024), DN Mukerjea writes an extended tribute to Mathai as an exemplar of a vanishing editorial culture: the rewriter, the subeditor, the "shoe-leather reporting" ethic, articles held for months before publication. The piece is explicitly a meditation on what has been lost — not just a person but an entire set of craft practices and institutional standards that no longer have a home in contemporary Indian journalism.

**The Implication**
If you run a publication, newsletter, or any editorial operation — however small — restore at least one editorial layer between first draft and publication, because the value of that layer is proportional to the damage its absence causes over time.

## FF's View

**The Core Conviction**

FF's single most important argument on media, journalism, and information is this: the crisis of journalism in India is not a technology problem, not a platform problem, and not even a misinformation problem. It is a values problem that has been reclassified as a business problem, and the reclassification is itself the disease.

The argument runs through every article FF has published on this theme, from Charles Assisi's 2015 confessional anatomy of access journalism to the 2026 platform redesign essay. At its centre is a deceptively simple claim: when media organisations stop asking "what do citizens need?" and start asking "what do customers want?", they do not merely lower quality. They defect from democracy. They become something else — entertainment businesses, influence machines, validation engines — while retaining the institutional authority of journalism. That gap between the function they claim and the function they perform is where the damage lives.

In "How the media has let down democracy" (2017), Arun Maira recounts a seminar where Jerry Levin of Time Warner defended his company by arguing that media must give customers what they want. Michael Sandel pushed back with the drug dealer's question: if customers want hard drugs, does that justify supplying them? The analogy is not elegant, but it is precise. Media, like drugs, operates on the nervous system of a democracy. What it feeds determines what the body politic can think, feel, and decide. And when media organisations optimise for engagement, for access, for advertiser relationships, or for subscription renewal, they are optimising for something other than truth — and calling it journalism.

FF's position is that this cannot be solved structurally without first being named as a values failure. No business model reform, no platform pivot, no regulation will fix it if the industry does not first accept that the "customer" framing is wrong in kind, not just in degree. The person reading a newspaper is not buying a product. They are a citizen whose capacity for self-governance depends on the quality of the information they receive. That distinction is not romantic — it is constitutional. And abandoning it has consequences that show up not in circulation numbers but in election results, in public health crises, and in the normalisation of authoritarian politics.

This is an uncomfortable position because it demands that media organisations accept constraints that no other business accepts. A restaurant can serve what diners want. A media outlet cannot — not entirely — because the reader's informed consent to their information diet depends on the quality of journalism that precedes it. You cannot meaningfully consent to misinformation. The market cannot self-correct here because the product being degraded is the very instrument of correction.

FF holds this position not as nostalgia for a golden age that never existed, but as a structural argument about what democracy requires. It is a position most practitioners resist, because it implies obligations that cut against revenue. But the evidence from India — WhatsApp forwards deciding elections, print advertising collapsing, TV channels reduced to single narratives in 2024 — suggests the cost of ignoring it is not theoretical.

**What India Means for This Theme**

India does not merely add local colour to the global media crisis. India changes the coordinates of the problem so fundamentally that most frameworks imported from Western contexts fail on arrival.

Start with scale. India has 200 million-plus WhatsApp users. It is the third-largest podcast consumer globally, behind only the United States and China, per Hindustan Times data cited in "FF Life: Six podcasts to decode a shifting world" (2022). It has the largest vernacular print newspaper market outside China, with circulation bouncing back to 88-90% of pre-Covid levels by late 2021, as reported in "What the media won't tell you about the media" (2021). These are not the dynamics of a media market in transition. They are the dynamics of a media market that is simultaneously at an early stage and a late stage — early in digital monetisation, late in the collapse of analogue certainties.

But the deeper India-specific reality is epistemic, not structural. In "WFH Daily #178: WhatsApp as a political platform" (2020), Founding Fuel quotes political consultant Shivam Shankar Singh on a phenomenon with no real Western parallel: rural Indian voters, added to political WhatsApp groups, believed they were receiving insider information. They read the forwards with what Singh calls "gusto." The group membership itself — the fact of being included — conferred credibility on the content. This is not gullibility. It is the logical consequence of information scarcity meeting social trust. When mainstream media is either inaccessible (language, cost, literacy) or discredited (captured, sensationalist, urban-centric), and when a WhatsApp message arrives through a social relationship, the social relationship transfers its credibility to the content. The message is trusted because the person is trusted. Misinformation travels on the rails of human connection.

This makes India's information problem structurally different from, say, the American one. In the United States, the crisis is primarily one of choice — people choosing partisan media that confirms their views. In India, for hundreds of millions of citizens, choice is not the operative variable. Access is. Trust architecture is. The social structure through which information flows is. A correction to American media dysfunction might involve better algorithmic design, stronger platform regulation, or media literacy campaigns. A correction to India's requires something more fundamental: building information sources that are trusted at the community level, in vernacular languages, through relational rather than institutional authority.

The business model consequences are equally distinct. In "Politics, the business of news, and the Radia tapes" (2021), Vir Sanghvi describes the Times of India's advertiser-first model as "a Times of India creation" — not a global template but an indigenous Indian innovation. The model worked precisely because the Indian market lacked the subscription culture that sustained Western quality outlets. India Today's cover clickbait was not a digital-era disease; it was built into the magazine's economics from the beginning, because newsstand sales (not subscriptions) drove circulation, and covers had to sell copies. As Sanghvi notes in that same conversation, "We think that clickbait is something the millennials invented with the internet. The cover of a magazine is not just a representation of the contents of the magazine, it is also a marketing tool." Indian media's engagement-over-truth logic predates the internet. The internet merely industrialised it.

The FDI restriction layer adds another dimension entirely. In "How tech firms are eating media" (2021), NS Ramnath notes that while businesses can deploy global capital to build media platforms in India, traditional media companies are bound by FDI restrictions. This creates a structural asymmetry: technology companies with effectively unlimited capital face no restrictions on operating what are functionally media businesses, while journalism organisations face caps that constrain their ability to scale. The result is a regulatory environment that unintentionally advantages the entities least accountable to democratic norms.

The Bharat dimension — beyond urban, English-speaking India — shifts the analysis further. The 2024 Lok Sabha elections were a turning point, documented in "13 Most Memorable Moments: Lok Sabha Elections 2024" (2024). While mainstream TV channels "sang just one tune," YouTubers, social media accounts, and independent journalists created an alternative information ecosystem that was simultaneously more representative and less accountable. Rural voices at tea shops were captured on video and circulated — "vox pop journalism" mainstreamed in a way that no editor, no newsroom, no fact-checker controlled. This is democracy's information ecosystem working, and failing, simultaneously. The voices were real. The absence of verification infrastructure was also real.

All India Radio — Akashvani — deserves a mention that mainstream media analysis consistently omits. As "FF Life: 21 Places that define India@75" (2022) notes, it is the largest radio network in the world, holding archives that include rare recordings of Tagore and Bose. It reaches audiences that no digital platform reaches, in languages that no venture-funded outlet serves. Its institutional decay — from genuinely public to bureaucratically captured — is a story about what happens when a state loses faith in the democratic function of information. It is also a reminder that India's media infrastructure includes layers that urban analysts never see.

**Where Global Best Practices Break Down**

The dominant global playbook for media in the digital era runs roughly as follows: transition from print advertising to digital advertising; when digital advertising fails to sustain quality journalism, transition to subscriptions; build audience loyalty through distinctive coverage and community; invest in data and personalisation to reduce churn. The Financial Times is the canonical success story. The New York Times is the scale success story. Both are held up as models.

The playbook is not wrong in its own context. But it breaks down in India along several axes, and breaking down in India matters because India is not a small market with local quirks — it is a billion-person democracy whose information ecosystem shapes global outcomes.

The first failure is the subscription assumption. In "What the media won't tell you about the media" (2021), the observation is stark: only two or three Indian digital publishers were crossing Rs 100 crore a year. The FT had one million digital subscribers. The gap is not a lag — it is not that India will catch up in five years. It reflects structural differences in income distribution, language fragmentation, and the historical pricing of information. Journalism in India has largely been free, or close to free, for generations. The newspaper that landed at the door cost less than a cup of tea. Building a subscription culture in that context requires not just better product but a fundamental renegotiation of the perceived value of information — a project that takes decades, not quarters.

The second failure is the assumption that platform-agnostic journalism succeeds by quality alone. In India, the platform through which information arrives shapes whether it is trusted. A WhatsApp forward from a trusted contact beats a well-sourced article from an unknown website. A YouTube video in Bhojpuri from a local journalist beats a beautifully produced English explainer. The distribution architecture is not neutral. It encodes trust. Global best practice says: build your audience on your own platform, drive them there from social. Indian reality says: your audience lives in ecosystems you don't control, and they evaluate you through relational rather than institutional criteria.

The third failure is the assumption that fact-checking scales. The global response to misinformation has been institutionalised fact-checking — dedicated organisations, verification desks, third-party labelling on platforms. This works, modestly, in contexts where the information consumer is already somewhat sceptical, where platforms carry institutional authority, and where the social cost of sharing debunked content is non-trivial. In the Indian WhatsApp context, as documented repeatedly in FF's coverage, none of these conditions hold. The forward comes from a trusted contact. The debunking, if it exists, comes from an institution the receiver has no relationship with. Trust defeats truth-claim in this architecture every time.

The fourth failure is the advertiser-quality-content alignment assumption. The global model says: quality journalism attracts quality audiences, which attracts premium advertisers. In "How tech firms are eating media" (2021), NS Ramnath notes the deeper structural shift: as premium audiences migrate to SVOD platforms, print becomes the only vehicle for reaching them — a counterintuitive resilience. But this resilience is temporary and market-specific. The broader failure is that programmatic advertising — the global industry's dominant model — rewards volume and engagement, not quality. An article that generates outrage and sharing earns more than an article that informs and satisfies. The incentive structure runs in the wrong direction, and no best-practice recommendation changes that without platform-level intervention.

Where the skeptics of the dominant narrative are right: the global media reform conversation is too focused on newsroom solutions to a problem that is, at its root, a platform problem, and ultimately a political economy problem. Making better journalism will not save journalism if the distribution infrastructure penalises depth, if the regulatory environment advantages tech over media, and if the political economy rewards polarisation over accuracy.

**How FF Diverges from Mainstream Media**

Mainstream Indian business media covers media as an industry. FF covers it as a democratic institution. The difference in framing produces systematically different stories.

Mainstream coverage of Indian media focuses on market shares, revenue figures, ownership changes, and the strategic moves of large players. It treats the Times of India group, Network18, Bennett Coleman, and Zee Entertainment as businesses to be analysed like any other. It covers the digital transition as a market opportunity or a disruption challenge. It is not wrong, exactly. But it is incomplete in a way that matters.

FF's divergence begins with the question it refuses to drop: what is media actually for? This is not a rhetorical question in FF's coverage — it produces different reporting. The access journalism anatomy in "Confessions of a practising journalist" (2015) is not a story mainstream business media would write, because mainstream business media is embedded in exactly the ecosystem it would have to critique. PR-dependent journalists parrotting corporate lines, junket culture, payment for positive features — these are not secrets. They are known, widely. But they are not named, loudly, by outlets that participate in the system.

FF named them. Charles Assisi, writing in 2015, described the mechanics with enough specificity that the piece reads as an industry document: the tycoon of impeccable pedigree who demanded cover placement before agreeing to speak; the infrastructure magnates who controlled document pipelines; the editors who dangled junket invitations as incentives for star performers. No mainstream business outlet would run this, and not primarily because of legal risk. It is because the audience for mainstream business media includes exactly the people being described.

On the 2024 election, FF's framing diverged sharply from both the triumphalist coverage of TV channels aligned with the ruling dispensation and the hand-wringing of English-language liberal media. "13 Most Memorable Moments: Lok Sabha Elections 2024" (2024) noted, without celebration or alarm, that independent journalism had found its moment — YouTubers, social media journalists, vox pop — while simultaneously noting that mainstream TV "sang just one tune." This is not advocacy journalism. It is observation journalism that refuses to pick a preferred narrative.

On the Aadhaar debate, NS Ramnath's "Rhetoric versus meaning" (2017) explicitly resisted the polarised frame that dominated mainstream coverage. The online battle, he argued, was a "fairly small set of people" — most Indians were neither flag-wavers nor fearful, but cautiously exploratory. This was a heterodox position at a time when both sides of the Aadhaar debate were claiming to speak for all Indians. FF refused both.

Where has consensus caught up? On access journalism's structural corruption — the conversation has slowly entered the mainstream, catalysed by the Adani-Hindenburg episode in 2023, when FF explicitly noted the failure of accountability journalism in its curated list "FF Life: 8 Stories of Financial Fraud & Hubris" (2023). On WhatsApp's role in Indian politics — by 2019, this was widely acknowledged; FF was tracking it from 2017. On the limits of subscription models as the clean alternative to advertising — this remains a minority view in mainstream media commentary, but Andrey Mir's *Postjournalism* argument, surfaced by FF in "How tech firms are eating media" (2021), is beginning to find broader citation.

**Against Management Orthodoxy**

Management orthodoxy on media, such as it is, tends to arrive in one of two forms: the transformation playbook (pivot to digital, build direct subscription, create audience communities) or the platform-thinking playbook (unbundle your content, distribute everywhere, find your monetisable niches). Both have their uses. Both fail to address the central problem that FF's evidence base consistently surfaces.

The first orthodoxy: give audiences what they want. This is not merely a media orthodoxy — it is the ur-orthodoxy of market-based thinking. Customer centricity, user research, product-market fit. In media, this logic produces the Jerry Levin position: our job is to serve customer preferences, not to impose editorial judgments. The evidence says this is wrong, not merely inadequate. In "How the media has let down democracy" (2017), Arun Maira invokes Michael Sandel's counter: the drug dealer also gives customers what they want. The analogy is uncomfortable because it implies that some customer preferences should not be served — that editorial judgment is not paternalistic but constitutive. The evidence from India's WhatsApp ecosystem, from TV channels optimised for engagement during elections, from digital platforms that reward outrage, consistently shows that serving audience preferences at scale does not produce an informed citizenry. It produces a captured one.

The second orthodoxy: transparency is the solution to trust deficits. This is the reformist consensus — if journalists show their work, reveal their sources, explain their methods, audiences will trust them more. FF's evidence base demolishes this with a sentence from Clay Johnson's *The Information Diet*, cited in "FF daily #255: The dark side of transparency" (2020): "If you turn the lights on in a roach-infested apartment, it doesn't kill the roaches, it just makes them organize in the shadows." Transparency without integrity is performance. It produces the halo of honesty without its substance. The piece notes: "You can simply claim to be transparent, and create a halo of honesty about you, without actually being honest." The management orthodoxy that transparency rebuilds trust conflates disclosure with trustworthiness — a category error with serious consequences for media reform efforts.

The third orthodoxy: more content, more distribution, more data. The assumption that volume and reach are the primary variables of media success runs through nearly every media strategy deck. FF's counter, developed most fully in "The ultimate guide to good journalism" (2020), is the food analogy extended: "Convention has it the more content we consume, the smarter we get. But if the food analogy be extrapolated here, how absurd this assertion is makes itself obvious." The evidence on information overload — cognitive depletion, filter bubble formation, the vulnerability of overwhelmed attention to misinformation — suggests that volume is not neutral. It is actively harmful when the content lacks nutritional value. The media organisation that publishes thirty stories a day may be doing more damage to its readers' epistemic health than the one that publishes three.

The fourth orthodoxy: the subscription model is the ethical alternative to advertising. This is the reformist consensus of the past decade. FF's engagement with Andrey Mir's *Postjournalism*, cited in "How tech firms are eating media" (2021), complicates it seriously: subscription models lead to polarisation because people essentially pay for validation and confirmation of existing beliefs. The financial logic of subscription — reduce churn, deepen loyalty, personalise experience — runs directly against the democratic logic of journalism, which is to challenge, inform, and sometimes unsettle. The subscriber who is surprised, contradicted, or disturbed is more likely to churn. The subscriber whose worldview is confirmed is more likely to renew. Subscription models, at scale, may be producing ideological capture through an entirely different mechanism than advertising — not selling audiences to advertisers, but selling audiences to themselves.

**The Entrepreneurial Mindset Beyond Startups**

The entrepreneurial lens on media, journalism, and information is not primarily about founding a media startup. It is about the posture of independent judgment in a field saturated with institutional capture.

The most important entrepreneurial insight in FF's evidence base is the curation argument developed in "The necessity of curators" (2019) by Charles Assisi. Curation is not aggregation. It is not algorithm. It is editorial judgment exercised actively — creating, filtering, leading. "In a noisy world where everyone imagines themselves as content producers, algorithms included, curators play some crucial roles... They create... They filter... They lead." This is an entrepreneurial function available to anyone in any organisation who pays attention to information quality. The journalist, the business leader, the policymaker, the analyst — each is responsible for the information environment they create for the people who depend on them. When a business leader shares a forwarded WhatsApp story in a company group, they are curating. When a policymaker briefs a cabinet on data selectively, they are curating. The question is whether they are doing it consciously, with judgment, or passively, with noise.

The *Spotlight* framework, developed in Charles Assisi's "The Spotlight on Incest" (2016), is directly applicable to non-media organisations. Assisi reads the film not primarily as a journalism story but as a leadership story. Marty Baron arrives as an outsider at the Boston Globe and immediately demonstrates the entrepreneurial move: he asks why something that everyone knows has not been reported. The answer — institutional relationships, access dependencies, social costs — is identical to the answer you get when you ask why any large organisation fails to surface a known problem. The investigative journalism model is a model for institutional honesty. Patient, outsider-led, structurally independent of the relationships that create the problem.

Applied to large organisations: the information pathology that corrupts journalism — access dependency, confirmation bias, the social cost of uncomfortable truths — operates identically inside corporations. The strategy team that tells the CEO what he wants to hear is practicing access journalism. The board that receives filtered reports is operating in a filter bubble. The management that punishes messengers is doing to its internal information environment what captured TV channels do to the public information environment. The entrepreneurial leader's job is to design against this — to create information flows that reward accuracy over palatability, that protect the bearer of bad news, that build in the structural independence that good journalism requires.

For policymakers, the systems implication is stark: if the information environment is a public good — which FF consistently treats it as — then it requires active stewardship, not just passive regulation. The FDI restriction that caps media companies while leaving technology companies unconstrained, documented in "How tech firms are eating media" (2021), is not a neutral regulatory position. It is a choice about whose information infrastructure gets protected and whose gets constrained. The entrepreneurial policymaker asks: what information environment do we actually want, and what does building it require?

**On Transformation: Process, Not Just Destination**

The standard media transformation narrative is destination-focused: legacy to digital, print to online, advertising to subscription. FF's reporting suggests this framing misses the actual mechanism of change, which is slower, messier, and more culturally embedded than any business model transition.

The most important evidence on the mechanism of transformation is the 2026 platform rebuild documented in "Designing for Signal" by Charles Assisi and Shrinath V. The piece describes a deliberate design decision to build against the dominant pattern: "A lot of media platforms optimise for continuous consumption — more surfaces, more prompts, more movement. That works if volume is the goal. Here, the priority is depth." This is not a technology decision. It is a values decision that then drives technology design. The transformation FF is enacting is not from one platform to another, but from one theory of what media is for to a different one — and that theory revision has to precede the platform revision, not follow it.

The Vir Sanghvi conversation about the Radia Tapes, documented in "Politics, the business of news, and the Radia tapes" (2021), reveals something important about how media transformation actually happens in practice. The decision to publish the Radia Tapes — the most significant accountability journalism moment in Indian media in recent memory — was driven partly by competitive pressure. Outlook's declining circulation created the conditions in which taking the risk was more rational than not taking it. Transformation in media, as in other industries, often happens under duress, not from virtue. The organisational courage that looks like a values shift from the outside is frequently a survival calculus from the inside.

The PG Mathai tribute in "Remembering P.G. Mathai" (2024) by DN Mukerjea is a meditation on how craft knowledge transfers — or fails to. Mathai's legacy was not a byline or a scoop. It was the culture of rewriting, of holding stories, of subordinating the individual voice to the quality of the work. That culture does not transfer through policy or training. It transfers through apprenticeship, through the daily practice of being corrected by someone who knows more than you, through the patient accumulation of what Robert Caro, cited in "FF Daily #431" (2021), calls the "SU" discipline — Shut Up, and wait. When that chain of transmission breaks — and it has broken, in Indian journalism, as the generation that learned from people like Mathai retires — the institutional knowledge disappears. It cannot be rebuilt quickly. Transformation here is not a pivot. It is a generational project.

The slow-information argument is simultaneously a business model argument and a transformation argument. Tim Harford's principle — daily news is more informative than rolling news, weekly is more informative than daily, a book is often better still — cited in "FF Daily #392: News vs. meaningful information" (2021), describes the direction of transformation FF is navigating toward. Not against speed on principle, but for depth because depth is what the moment requires. The transformation of media from speed-optimised to depth-optimised is not primarily a technology problem. It is a reader relationship problem. Can you build an audience that values slowness? FF's existence as a subscriber-supported, depth-oriented outlet is the test of that proposition.

**The Human and Cultural Dimension**

The human and cultural factors in India's information crisis are not soft factors. They are the primary factors, and strategy that ignores them fails not at the edges but at the centre.

The most important human factor is the relationship between social trust and information credibility. This is not unique to India, but it is uniquely intense here. When Shivam Shankar Singh, quoted in "WFH Daily #178: WhatsApp as a political platform" (2020), describes rural voters reading political forwards "with gusto" because they believed group membership conferred insider access, he is describing the operation of social trust in an information-scarce environment. The human desire to be included — to be one of the people who knows — is a fundamental motive that bad actors in any information ecosystem exploit. In India, where formal information institutions (media, government, academia) have limited credibility with large populations, and where information has historically arrived through social networks rather than institutional channels, this vulnerability is structural.

The listening dimension, developed by Arun Maira and cited across multiple FF articles, is underappreciated as a cultural practice. Maira's framework in "How the media has let down democracy" (2017) is not about media as an institution but about the human capacity to receive information from people who are different from you. The Buddhist three wisdom tools — listening, contemplating, meditating — are invoked not as spiritual prescription but as a description of what good information processing requires. In a culture that prizes quick response, visible reaction, and the performance of having opinions, the capacity for deep listening is both rare and undervalued. Media that is designed for reaction — the outrage click, the like, the share — actively degrades this capacity. Media that creates space for it is doing cultural work that no algorithm can replicate.

The Gauri Lankesh murder, cited in "The battle for our data" (2017), is the limiting case of what happens when the cultural dimension of journalism is ignored in favour of the political or commercial one. "She was killed for her views. That's the most cruel way to say, 'I don't want to listen to you.'" This is not a policy analysis. It is a statement about what violence against journalists actually means — the elimination of a voice, the assertion that certain perspectives do not have the right to exist in public discourse. The cultural attack on journalism in India is not only legal or economic. It is also physical, directed at specific people with specific views, and it functions as an intimidation of all journalists who might hold similar views or cover similar subjects.

The human dimension of the craft loss is equally important. The PG Mathai tribute is a eulogy not just for a person but for a practice — the culture of patient rewriting, of editors who held stories rather than publishing them, of the subordination of individual ego to institutional standards. This is a cultural formation, not a technical one. It was built through relationships, through the specific human experience of being mentored and then mentoring in turn. Its loss cannot be addressed by technology or by training programmes. It requires the rebuilding of the human chain of transmission through which craft knowledge passes.

The India-Pakistan cricket coverage, analysed in "India versus Pakistan is not cricket, but war" (2017), reveals the human and cultural cost of media's manufacturing of emotion. Charles Assisi asks: "When did violence permeate into something as benign as a game of cricket?" The answer is: when media found that gladiatorial framing drove ratings, and when that framing met a cultural context in which the partition wound was still live. The media did not create the historical animus, but it amplified it into something qualitatively different — an emotion that substituted for geopolitical complexity, that gave audiences the catharsis of a proxy war without requiring them to think about the actual consequences of real conflict. This is the human cost of giving audiences what they want.

**The Systems View**

The information ecosystem is not a sector. It is an infrastructure — as fundamental to the functioning of a democracy as roads, electricity, or water — and it has the same kinds of interdependencies, second-order effects, and systemic vulnerabilities that physical infrastructure has.

The clearest systemic framing in FF's evidence base comes from NS Ramnath's "How tech firms are eating media" (2021). The argument is structural: technology companies with global capital are building what are functionally media businesses — distribution platforms, content surfaces, audience aggregators — while operating under none of the regulatory constraints, civic obligations, or accountability norms that media organisations face. The result is a regulatory arbitrage that hollows out journalism while filling the resulting space with content that is optimised for engagement rather than accuracy. The FDI restrictions on media companies are not the cause of this problem, but they are a symptom of a regulatory framework that has not caught up with how the information system actually works.

The second-order effects of this structural asymmetry are visible in the 2024 election evidence. When mainstream TV channels "sing one tune," as documented in "13 Most Memorable Moments: Lok Sabha Elections 2024" (2024), the information void is not left empty — it is filled by YouTube channels, WhatsApp groups, and social media accounts that operate with no verification infrastructure, no accountability mechanisms, and no editorial standards. Some of this alternative journalism is genuinely valuable — the vox pop journalism that captured rural voices was more representative than anything on TV. But the same infrastructure that carries authentic voices also carries coordinated misinformation. The absence of institutional journalism creates a space that is filled by whatever serves the interests of whoever has the most money and the least scruples.

The third-order effect is epistemological. When the information environment is sufficiently degraded — when enough people receive enough false or distorted information through enough trusted channels — the capacity for collective deliberation breaks down. Not because people are stupid, but because deliberation requires some minimal shared factual ground. The post-truth crisis documented in "Conversations in a post-truth world" (2019) is not merely a political problem. It is a systems failure: the infrastructure of shared reality has been compromised at multiple levels simultaneously, and the repair requires intervention at multiple levels simultaneously.

The government data opacity issue, documented in the context of Covid in "Tim Harford on sensemaking and storytelling" (2021), is a systems problem of a different kind. When Gautam Menon at Ashoka University describes Covid data as "completely unavailable to anyone outside the government system," the failure is not just journalistic — it is a failure of the data infrastructure that journalism depends on. You cannot hold power accountable without data. You cannot make sense of a pandemic without data. The information ecosystem's quality depends upstream on whether the data the ecosystem reports on is accessible. Government data opacity is not a journalism problem — it is a democratic infrastructure problem that manifests first as a journalism problem.

The newsprint price crisis — from $450 to $950 per tonne between 2019 and 2022, cited in "FF Insights #613" (2022) — is a supply chain systems issue with direct democratic consequences. When the cost of physical distribution of news doubles in three years, the publications that survive are not necessarily the most accurate or the most important. They are the ones with the deepest capital reserves, the most powerful owners, or the most attractive advertising inventory. The systemic effect is concentration and capture — both of which are enemies of the independent journalism that democracy requires.

The advertising ecosystem's role in the information system is perhaps the most underappreciated systemic variable. Programmatic advertising — the dominant model — does not buy audiences for quality journalism. It buys eyeballs for the cheapest possible cost, which means it systematically rewards high-volume, low-cost content over high-quality, expensive content. The aggregate effect, operating across thousands of publications simultaneously, is a race to the bottom in quality that no individual publication can escape through virtue. The system itself is anti-journalism, and reforming individual outlets without reforming the system produces the journalistic equivalent of cleaning up pollution downstream while ignoring the factory upstream.

**The Evolution of FF's Thinking**

The earliest FF articles on media and journalism — Charles Assisi's 2015 confessionals — are largely diagnostic. They name the disease: access journalism, paid content, junket culture, the social media vanity economy of purchased followers. The implicit frame is that these are corruptions of a system that could function better if practitioners made different choices. The problem is primarily one of professional ethics.

By 2017, the frame has shifted. Arun Maira's "How the media has let down democracy" is not a piece about individual practitioners making bad choices. It is a structural argument: the business model has superseded the civic mission, and no amount of individual ethical behaviour can fix that without changing the model. The *Spotlight* analysis, Assisi's India-Pakistan cricket piece, the Gauri Lankesh reference — all of these treat media dysfunction not as a professional failure but as a democratic emergency. The stakes have been raised.

Between 2017 and 2019, the WhatsApp and social media threads develop. The concern shifts from media organisations making bad choices to information architecture making good choices impossible. The filter bubble argument, the post-truth documentation, the political weaponisation of WhatsApp — these point to a problem that is not primarily a journalism problem at all. Journalism could be perfect, and the information ecosystem could still be catastrophically broken, because the distribution infrastructure rewards exactly what good journalism resists. This is a significant shift: from media-as-problem to media-as-symptom.

By 2020 and 2021, FF's own editorial practice becomes part of the argument. The information diet framing — content as food, more as fattening, depth as nourishment — is fully developed. The platform articles begin. The argument that slow information is more valuable than fast information is made explicitly and defended with evidence. FF is no longer only diagnosing; it is modelling what it believes the alternative looks like. The "Creating space for reflection" frame (2017) has by now produced an editorial practice built around it.

The 2021 conversation with Vir Sanghvi is notable for what it introduces: self-awareness about media's own complicity, spoken by a prominent practitioner rather than an outside critic. The Radia Tapes conversation is not an outside critique of Indian journalism. It is journalism examining itself — the competitive dynamics, the access dependencies, the clickbait logic — with unusual candour. FF's willingness to run this as a "masterclass on journalism," as it describes it in "FF Life: The Best Stories of 2021" (2022), reflects an evolved conviction that the most important journalism on media is the kind that implicates the journalists themselves.

By 2024, the election coverage analysis suggests a new development: the emergence of a decentralised, platform-agnostic, vernacular journalism that is simultaneously hopeful and concerning. FF's framing in "13 Most Memorable Moments: Lok Sabha Elections 2024" (2024) does not celebrate or condemn this development. It holds the ambiguity — independent journalism found its voice in 2024, and that was important; it also operated without the infrastructure of accountability that makes journalism trustworthy, and that was dangerous. This is a more mature position than the 2015 framing, which located the problem primarily in professional ethics, or the 2017 framing, which located it primarily in business models. By 2024, the problem is simultaneously ethical, structural, infrastructural, and systemic.

The 2026 platform redesign articles — "Editor's Note | Sharpening the Signal" and "Designing for Signal" — represent the fullest statement of FF's evolved position. The argument is now operational: not just what journalism should be, but how to build the infrastructure that enacts it. The design choice to resist "speed-driven patterns" and "continuous consumption" is the 2015 professional ethics argument converted into product architecture. The conviction has not changed. The understanding of what it requires to enact the conviction has deepened significantly.

**Key Voices**

**Arun Maira** is FF's most important intellectual anchor on the democratic function of media. His distinctive contribution is the frame of media as civic institution — not a business, not a profession, but a pillar of democracy with specific obligations that cannot be discharged by market logic alone. His listening framework — developed in the context of *Listening for Well-Being* and applied in multiple FF pieces — offers the most sophisticated counter to polarisation in FF's evidence base. He is not a media insider; his perspective comes from public life, governance, and management, which gives him a vantage point that full-time journalists often lack. He is at his sharpest in "How the media has let down democracy" (2017), where he brings the Michael Sandel encounter to bear on the business-vs-democracy tension, and in "The battle for our data" (2017), where he extends the listening framework to the social media context.

**Charles Assisi** is FF's primary voice on the practice and pathology of journalism. His distinctive contribution is the willingness to implicate himself — to write confessionally about access journalism, to buy Twitter followers in order to expose the ecosystem, to analyse his own craft with the same rigour he applies to others'. He brings the insider's knowledge of how the industry actually works — the mechanics of access dependency, the junket economy, the PR-journalism interface — combined with an outsider's willingness to name it. He is at his sharpest in "Confessions of a practising journalist" (2015) and "The ultimate guide to good journalism" (2020), and his analysis of the Spotlight film as a leadership text rather than a journalism text reveals the breadth of his frame.

**NS Ramnath** is FF's most analytically rigorous voice on the structural and systemic dimensions of media. His contribution is the macro view: how business models shape information quality, how technology companies are reordering the media ecosystem, how regulatory frameworks create the conditions for media dysfunction. He is not a polemicist — his analyses are careful, qualified, and precise in a way that makes them more durable than polemic. He is at his sharpest in "How tech firms are eating media" (2021), where he synthesises Andrey Mir's *Postjournalism* argument with Indian structural realities, and in his Tim Harford interview, "Tim Harford on sensemaking and storytelling" (2021), which brings the slow-information argument into direct contact with India's data opacity problem.

**Vir Sanghvi**, though not a regular FF contributor, is the most important external voice in FF's evidence base on the historical and political economy of Indian media. His candour about the Times of India model, the India Today cover logic, and his own role in the Radia Tapes episode provides a practitioner's self-examination that is unusually honest. His distinctiveness is his willingness to historicise Indian media's dysfunction — to locate the clickbait logic not in the internet era but in the newsstand economics of the 1980s. He is at his sharpest in "Politics, the business of news, and the Radia tapes" (2021).

**DN Mukerjea**, the author of the PG Mathai tribute, represents the craft memory dimension that no other voice in FF's evidence base covers. His distinctive contribution is the documentation of what is lost when a generation of journalists who learned from people like Mathai retires — not the big structural arguments, but the daily practices of rewriting, holding stories, subordinating ego to quality. This is the granular level of journalism's cultural formation, and it is largely invisible in both journalism studies and media business analysis.

**What FF Is Skeptical Of**

FF is skeptical of the claim that digital transformation is primarily a technology challenge. The technology has changed. The human and civic requirements of good journalism have not. Treating the media crisis as a technology transition problem — solve the platform, solve the business model, solve the distribution — consistently misses the fact that the underlying values failure predates digital. Indian media's access-journalism corruption, its advertiser-first logic, its clickbait cover culture — all of these were present before the internet. Digital accelerated and amplified them. It did not create them.

FF is skeptical of social media influencers as a meaningful replacement for institutional journalism. Vir Sanghvi's question — "When you see these headlines that seem like clickbait and see the story is nonsense, are you going to be influenced by them?" — from "Politics, the business of news, and the Radia tapes" (2021), points to the gap between attention and influence. High-follower accounts command attention. Whether they command genuine influence on opinion formation, particularly among audiences who retain any critical capacity, is much less clear. The conflation of reach with authority is an error that media analytics perpetuates because reach is measurable and authority is not.

FF is skeptical of fact-checking as a scalable solution to misinformation. The WhatsApp evidence — forwards trusted because of their social context rather than their epistemic quality — suggests that information credibility is not primarily a fact problem. It is a trust architecture problem. Fact-checking an article that arrived through a trusted social network does not displace the social trust. It adds an institutional counter-claim that the receiver has no reason to privilege over the relational trust they have already placed. Fact-checking matters at the margins. It does not address the structural problem.

FF is skeptical of the Forbes.com contributor model as journalism, documented in detail in "FF Insights #588" (2022). The model that turns brand-name outlets into open platforms — allowing anyone to publish under a prestigious masthead for attention, money, or marketing purposes — is not an innovation in journalism. It is the destruction of the editorial function while retaining its symbolic authority. The result is "scams, grift and bad journalism" under a trusted brand name, which is worse than scams and bad journalism under an unknown brand name because the trusted name provides misleading signals of quality.

FF is skeptical of rolling news as information. Tim Harford's hierarchy — book better than weekly, weekly better than daily, daily better than rolling — cited in "FF Daily #392" (2021), is not a preference but a diagnostic. Rolling news is not primarily information production. It is attention capture. The claim that watching cable news for three hours makes you better informed than reading a well-reported 3,000-word article for thirty minutes is not supported by evidence. FF's own editorial practice is built on this skepticism.

FF is skeptical of the "more voices, more democracy" claim that underpins platform companies' defense of their own ecosystem effects. More voices does not produce more informed discourse if the infrastructure through which those voices travel rewards sensationalism, punishes nuance, and is indifferent to accuracy. The 2024 election's decentralised media moment was real and valuable. But valuing it does not require pretending that an unmediated, unverified, algorithmically amplified information environment is democratic simply because it is plural.

**What FF Celebrates**

FF celebrates journalism that functions as an accountability institution. The Indian Express's investigation of Chanda Kochhar, praised in "The Fourth Estate versus Chanda Kochhar" (2019), is the template: meticulous, patient, document-driven, willing to pursue a story against institutional resistance. The Boston Globe's Spotlight team, analysed in "The Spotlight on Incest" (2016), is the international template: outsider leadership, structural independence, willingness to confront institutions the newsroom has historically been close to. These are not romantically different from ordinary journalism — they are ordinary journalism performed at its full potential. What FF celebrates is not the exception but the standard being met.

FF celebrates editorial courage that costs something. Vir Sanghvi's Outlook decision to publish the Radia Tapes, made under competitive pressure and at significant risk, is praised not despite its mixed motivations but because of the honesty with which those motivations are disclosed. The Financial Times's Gillian Tett, praised in "FF Daily #392" (2021) for tracking derivatives markets before 2008, is celebrated because she was paying attention to something almost no one else was — not because she had a scoop, but because she had done the sustained analytical work that made the scoop possible when the crisis came.

FF celebrates RK Laxman as a model of the independent conscience in Indian public life — a cartoonist as truth-teller, celebrated in "FF Daily #351: The politician and the cartoonist" (2021), who maintained his independence across decades in which every other institution was, at various points, captured. The cartoonist's distinctiveness is structural: the cartoon speaks obliquely, cannot be fully captured by official interpretation, and reaches audiences that long-form journalism never does. Laxman understood that the form was itself a mode of freedom.

FF celebrates slow information and the organisations committed to it. The Wall Street Journal's "breadth and depth," praised in "The ultimate guide to good journalism" (2020), The Economist's willingness to "upset people on both the Right and Left wing with its strong opinions," the Financial Times's 1 million digital subscribers built through patience rather than pivot — these are celebrated because they represent proof that depth and independence are commercially viable, given sufficient conviction. They are not models that transfer directly to India's structural context, but they are existence proofs that the conviction is not merely idealistic.

FF celebrates Maria Ressa for beginning the work on Facebook's democratic damage before it was fashionable, cited in "FF Daily #497" (2021). The Nobel Prize is noted not as an institutional endorsement but as a belated recognition of work done at personal cost before consensus arrived. This is FF's consistent model of what it celebrates: not the award, but the sustained attention that made the award eventually legible.

FF celebrates Mastodon, cautiously, in "FF Life: Mastodon" (2022), as an alternative to engagement-optimised social media. "No trolls or abusive memes" — not celebrated as a technology triumph but as evidence that design choices about what to optimise for produce different human environments. The implicit claim is that the toxic social media environment is not inevitable. It is the product of specific design decisions made by specific people for specific financial reasons. It can be designed differently.

**The Historical Lens**

FF uses history not as context-setting but as a diagnostic tool — to understand what is actually structurally novel about the present media crisis and what is a recurrence of older patterns in new forms.

The most important historical argument in FF's evidence base is Vir Sanghvi's historicisation of Indian media clickbait, from "Politics, the business of news, and the Radia tapes" (2021). The conventional wisdom is that clickbait is a digital-era phenomenon, produced by algorithmic incentive and internet attention economics. Sanghvi's historical account says this is wrong: Indian magazine covers were always clickbait, because newsstand sales required covers that sold copies, not covers that represented content. India Today under Aroon Purie built one of India's most important media institutions on this logic. The clickbait charge against digital media is not wrong, but it mistakes the vehicle for the driver. The driver is the attention economy; the vehicle changes with each technological era.

The access journalism problem, anatomised by Assisi in 2015, has a history that also predates digital. The structural relationship between business elites and English-language media in India was forged in the post-liberalisation era, when the expansion of corporate India and the expansion of business media happened simultaneously and interpenetrably. The infrastructure magnates who controlled document pipelines, the tycoons who demanded cover placement — these relationships were built in the 1990s and 2000s, in print newsrooms, before any of the digital disruptions that are now blamed for journalism's problems. The access journalism problem is not a new disease. It is an old one that the new technology has made more visible and more consequential.

The RK Laxman tribute, in "FF Daily #351: The politician and the cartoonist" (2021), invokes a longer history of the independent conscience in Indian public life. The cartoon tradition in Indian journalism predates independence — the political cartoon was one of the few forms that colonial censors found difficult to suppress because its meaning is always deniable. Laxman's decades-long practice of the Common Man as witness to Indian political life is a historical argument about what independence in journalism requires: not just institutional independence, but a form that structurally resists capture. The cartoon cannot be captured because it speaks indirectly. The lesson for contemporary journalism is not to draw cartoons, but to think about what forms of expression carry their independence in their structure rather than depending on organisational virtue to maintain it.

The Akashvani archive, mentioned in "FF Life: 21 Places that define India@75" (2022), is a historical record of what Indian public information infrastructure was intended to be — genuinely public, reaching audiences that no commercial media served, in languages that no market-driven outlet found worth serving. Its degradation from public broadcaster to state instrument is a historical case study in how public information institutions fail: not through dramatic capture but through the slow substitution of institutional service for political service, one appointment at a time.

**Unresolved Tensions**

FF holds, without resolving, several genuine contradictions that run through the entire evidence base.

The first: slow information is more valuable, but fast information is what democracy sometimes requires. Tim Harford's hierarchy — book better than weekly, weekly better than daily — is intellectually sound. But a democracy in which a journalist takes six weeks to write the definitive account of a crisis that required a response in six days has not served democracy well. The Radia Tapes were published under competitive pressure, quickly, and that mattered. The investigative journalism that broke the Kochhar story was patient, but it was not infinitely patient — there was a moment at which the story had to be told. FF's deep commitment to depth and slowness sits in genuine tension with the democratic requirement for timely accountability. The tension is not resolved; it is managed, differently in each case.

The second: curation is essential, but all curation is editorial control. FF argues forcefully for the value of curation over algorithmic feeds, for the necessity of the curator's role in filtering noise. But curation is a form of power. The curator decides what you see. In a media landscape already dominated by a few large players making curatorial decisions for hundreds of millions of people, adding a new layer of editorial curation may reduce noise while concentrating power. The WhatsApp model — distributed, peer-to-peer, no central curation — is epistemically dangerous. The centralised editorial model — trusted editors, depth, quality — carries its own capture risk. FF does not resolve this tension, because it cannot be resolved at the level of media design. It requires institutional accountability mechanisms that go beyond any individual outlet's editorial practice.

The third: the subscription model is corrupted by confirmation bias, but it is the only viable funding model for independent journalism at this moment. FF is reader-supported. It believes in the independence this creates. It also cites Andrey Mir's argument that subscription models cause polarisation through validation-seeking. These positions are in tension. If subscription audiences pay to have their existing beliefs confirmed, then FF's subscription model — dependent on retaining subscribers — is subject to the same structural pull toward affirmation and away from challenge. FF's editorial practice resists this pull. Whether resistance is sustainable at scale, or whether the financial logic of subscription will eventually reshape editorial choices, is genuinely open.

The fourth: independent journalism is gaining ground in India (the 2024 YouTube/social media evidence), but it operates without accountability infrastructure. FF celebrates the emergence of independent voices and vox pop journalism. It also insists on verification, craft, and accountability as non-negotiable. These two commitments pull in opposite directions when the independent journalism that is actually emerging is unverified, craft-free, and accountable to no one. There is no resolution here — only the uncomfortable acknowledgment that the democratisation of voice has not been accompanied by the democratisation of trustworthiness.

---

**The Missing Voices**

The most significant absence in FF's coverage is vernacular journalism — not as a subject of analysis, but as a practitioner voice. The reporting on India's media ecosystem is conducted primarily in English, by English-language practitioners, about English-language institutions, with vernacular media appearing only as structural data (circulation numbers, WhatsApp dynamics, podcast consumption statistics). The journalist writing in Marathi, the editor of a Tamil weekly, the correspondent covering Jharkhand's forest rights issues in Hindi — these voices do not appear in FF's evidence base. This is not a minor gap. Vernacular journalism is where most Indians' information environment is actually formed. The principles that FF develops from its English-language evidence base may not transfer to contexts in which the structural pressures, the relationship between journalists and communities, and the economics of production are completely different.

The second absence is the reader — or rather, the information consumer who is not also a practitioner or intellectual. FF's coverage of media is addressed to business leaders, policymakers, and thoughtful practitioners. The ordinary citizen as information consumer — the person making sense of a WhatsApp forward, the rural voter deciding what to believe about a politician, the urban worker navigating information overload — appears in FF's coverage primarily as a data point (polling numbers, consumption statistics) rather than as a subject with their own strategies for managing information. This absence makes FF's prescriptions for media reform systematically more useful to producers of information than to consumers of it.

The third absence is economic journalism about media — not the business model analysis that FF does well, but the labour economics of journalism. The reporter's salary, the working conditions of a correspondent in a conflict zone, the contractualisation of editorial work, the collapse of the sub-editing function that P.G. Mathai embodied — these are absent from the evidence base. The craft argument is made eloquently through the Mathai tribute. The structural argument about why craft has collapsed — the economic conditions that make Mathai-type journalism unsustainable — is not made.

The fourth absence is academic research. FF's evidence base draws extensively on practitioners, thinkers, and essayists. The media studies literature — which has a great deal to say about concentration, capture, the political economy of journalism, and the effects of different media environments on democratic outcomes — is largely absent. This is partly a function of FF's practitioner orientation, but it means that some of the strongest evidence for FF's core arguments (that media's democratic function is being undermined, that business models shape editorial output, that social media damages epistemic health) is not cited where it would be most persuasive.

---

**What This Means For You**

The first belief to update: you probably believe that your information problem is a volume problem — too much noise, too many sources, not enough time. The evidence says this is wrong in kind, not just degree. Volume is the symptom. The disease is the absence of a conscious information diet. In "The ultimate guide to good journalism" (2020), Charles Assisi makes the food analogy precisely: more content does not make you smarter any more than more food makes you healthier. The question is not how to consume less of everything but what you are actually choosing to consume and why. If your daily information intake consists primarily of WhatsApp forwards, Twitter notifications, rolling news, and content algorithms have decided you want — you are eating junk, regardless of how much time you spend consuming it. Update the belief from "I need to manage my time" to "I need to manage my diet."

The one thing to stop: stop treating access to powerful people as a measure of your information quality. The access journalism trap is not unique to professional journalists. Every business leader, policymaker, and strategist who values a conversation because of who it was with — rather than what it revealed — is practicing access journalism on themselves. When the CEO of a major company tells you something, the instinct is to treat it as signal because of the source's authority. But Charles Assisi's anatomy in "Confessions of a practising journalist" (2015) shows exactly how this logic is exploited: the powerful person controls access, and therefore controls the information you receive, by making continued access conditional on receiving the information they want you to have. Stop weighting information by the prestige of its source. Start weighting it by the quality of its reasoning and the specificity of its evidence.

The one thing to start: build your own editorial process for the information you circulate. Every time you share a link in a professional group, forward a document to a colleague, reference a piece in a meeting, or cite a statistic in a presentation, you are curating. You are making an editorial decision about what is worth circulating in your world. Start treating that as a craft. Before you share, ask what you are asserting by sharing it: Is the source accountable? Is the claim verified? Is the framing honest? This is not perfectionism — it is the minimum standard that the evidence base suggests makes a difference. The WhatsApp misinformation ecosystem runs on forwarded content that no one in the chain ever interrogated. You are in that chain. Your standards for what enters it are not your private concern. They are your contribution to the information environment your colleagues, your organisation, and your community inhabit.

Here is the conviction to carry out: the information environment is not something that happens to you — it is something you continuously make, through every source you trust, every claim you repeat, and every voice you amplify; and the quality of every decision you make, collective and individual, depends on whether you take that responsibility seriously.
