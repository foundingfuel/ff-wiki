# Innovation & R&D

---

## PART 1 — OPENING

Here is the uncomfortable truth about Indian innovation: the country that gave the world zero, chess, and the decimal system now imports the tools it uses to count its own growth. India's R&D spending has never crossed 1% of GDP. No Indian company appears in Interbrand's global top 100. The unicorns celebrated in the business press are, as Amit Ranjan noted, largely gatekeepers to a large market — not creators of path-breaking products from first principles.

This is not a failure of talent. The engineers who built Silicon Valley, the researchers behind AlphaFold, the executives running Google — many of them are Indian. The failure is systemic: of institutions, incentives, and imagination. India has world-class talent and a third-world innovation infrastructure. That gap is the central tension this page addresses.

The conventional understanding of this problem makes it worse. Practitioners reach for the wrong solutions — R&D tax breaks that reward the wrong behaviour, Make in India policies that mistake assembly for invention, startup ecosystems that celebrate fundraising as a proxy for building. They import Silicon Valley frameworks without the Valley's conditions. They confuse jugaad with frugal innovation, app-layer copying with product creation, and GCC headcount with indigenous R&D capacity.

What this page offers is different. It is built on a decade of Founding Fuel's reporting — from Rishikesha Krishnan's systemic analysis of why India has not produced a household-name invention, to Haresh Chawla's forensic dissection of how mega-funding kills innovation, to Sridhar Vembu's first-principles challenge to the entire services-only model. It will not tell you to "think differently." It will tell you what the evidence actually shows — about what works, what doesn't, and what India's practitioners must stop pretending.

## PART 2 — PRINCIPLES

---

### Principle 1

> The real innovation deficit in India is not talent or ideas — it is the courage to build products from first principles rather than copy foreign models with local tweaks.

**What This Means & Why**
Indian startups have built extraordinary distribution, acquired millions of users, and attracted billions in capital — and almost none of it constitutes genuine product innovation. The dominant strategy has been to identify a proven Western business model, adapt it to Indian conditions, and race to capture the market before rivals do. This produces scale, sometimes, but never category creation. The confusion between market capture and product innovation is not semantic — it shapes what gets funded, what gets built, and what India's economy looks like in twenty years.

**The India Reality**
India's tech unicorns are valued as market gatekeepers, not as technology creators. The government's own HR department reportedly did not know what a "product manager" was — an institutional symptom of a services-oriented economy that has never quite believed it can make things the world wants.

**The Evidence**
In 'Paving the road for world-beating software products from India' (2019), Amit Ranjan argues that "most of the new age tech unicorns in India are based on ideas and business models that are copied from foreign innovators (with some local tweaks). Their outsized valuations are a result of them being the gatekeepers to the large Indian market, rather than from having created path-breaking products from first principles."

**The Implication**
Before greenlighting your next product initiative, ask explicitly: are we adapting a proven model to Indian conditions, or are we solving a problem no one outside India has solved — and if the former, decide deliberately, not by default.

---

### Principle 2

> Mega-funding kills the innovation it claims to accelerate.

**What This Means & Why**
The dominant story of Indian tech in the 2010s was that capital scarcity was the constraint — that if Indian startups had more money, they would build more boldly. The evidence runs the other direction. Large funding rounds created sprawling teams without focus, replaced competitive urgency with comfort, and turned fundraising itself into the product. The hunger that produces genuine innovation is incompatible with the security that large rounds provide. Investors who understood this were rare; the herd was not.

**The India Reality**
India's startup ecosystem spent most of the 2010s celebrating the funding announcement rather than the product milestone. The metrics reported were GMV, app downloads, and valuation — almost never unit economics, product differentiation, or defensible technology.

**The Evidence**
In 'How India's digital economy can rediscover its mojo' (2016), Haresh Chawla writes: "Mega-funding killed the innovation and hunger, the leanness and the sharpness. It created sprawling teams without focus." He frames this not as an accident but as a structural consequence of capital abundance decoupling innovation effort from survival pressure.

**The Implication**
Set a specific constraint — a budget ceiling, a team size cap, a deadline — before the next innovation initiative begins, and treat the constraint as a design specification, not an obstacle.

---

### Principle 3

> Technologies do not disrupt — business models do. Most companies are trying to innovate in the wrong place.

**What This Means & Why**
The dominant framework in corporate innovation is technology-led: find the new technology, build around it, wait for disruption. But the historical record shows that the decisive innovations — low-cost airlines, Netflix's streaming pivot, Haier's micro-enterprise model — are not technology breakthroughs. They are reconfigurations of who pays, what is bundled, how value is delivered. The technology is often pre-existing. The business model is the innovation.

**The India Reality**
85% of CEOs say innovation is at the top of their agenda; 80% say their business models are at risk; only 6% believe they have the capability to respond. These numbers, cited from McKinsey research, describe a systemic capability gap — organisations know the threat but are building the wrong competence in response.

**The Evidence**
In 'Technologies do not disrupt, business models disrupt' (2015), Indrajit Gupta reports Erik Roth's argument from McKinsey: "Technologies do not disrupt, business models disrupt." Roth cites research showing the most successful innovations employ six or more kinds of innovation simultaneously, with business model and customer experience shifts at the core.

**The Implication**
Audit your current innovation pipeline: for each initiative, identify whether the innovation is in the technology layer, the business model layer, or both — and redirect resources accordingly.

---

### Principle 4

> Constraints accelerate innovation; abundance sedates it.

**What This Means & Why**
The intuitive case for removing constraints — give innovators more resources, more time, more freedom — is almost always wrong. The evidence across industries, geographies, and contexts consistently shows that constraints force the cognitive reframing that produces genuine novelty. Carlos Ghosn's frugal engineering at Renault-Nissan, the development of jugaad solutions in rural India, MINISO's brick-and-mortar model built around e-commerce's limitations — all are products of constraint, not freedom.

**The India Reality**
India has embedded frugal innovation in its economic DNA by necessity. The problem is not a shortage of constrained environments — it is that Indian organisations have learned to celebrate the constraint as a temporary problem to be solved with capital, rather than as a permanent design parameter to be leveraged.

**The Evidence**
In 'Moderate constraints actually boost creativity. Here's why' (2017), Charles Assisi argues that frugal engineering and disruptive innovation are "embedded in our psyche. We have always called it jugaad... by our very nature, we are built to make do with less." He links this to the Theory of Constraints and to experimental research on creativity under scarcity conditions.

**The Implication**
When scoping the next product or process innovation, explicitly specify what you will not spend, what you will not build, and what you will not assume — and treat those limits as the brief, not the problem.

---

### Principle 5

> Framing the right question is harder than finding the answer — and most organisations spend all their time on the latter.

**What This Means & Why**
The standard innovation process begins with ideation: generate ideas, filter, prototype. It skips the harder step that precedes it — establishing whether the question being answered is the right one. Dell's business model emerged from a question about why computers cost five times the sum of their parts. The moon mission succeeded because Kennedy specified the destination, the deadline, and the constraint in a single sentence. The question is the design specification. Most organisations treat it as a given.

**The India Reality**
India's startup ecosystem has been particularly prone to answering questions that were already answered abroad. The copy-to-India model is structurally a question-avoidance strategy — it borrows the question along with the answer, leaving no space for the kind of original problem-framing that produces genuinely new markets.

**The Evidence**
In 'FF Insights #645: Elon Musk and the art of asking the right questions' (2022), Founding Fuel cites Musk's insight that "a lot of times, the question is harder than the answer, and if you can properly phrase the question, then the answer is the easy part." The companion piece, 'FF Insights #650: Creativity is a dialogue' (2022), adds: "Without a problem, without a failure, without a flaw, without a frustration, innovation has nothing to latch on to."

**The Implication**
Before the next innovation sprint or offsite, spend the first session exclusively on problem-framing — write the question you are trying to answer and test it: is it genuinely novel, or is it a known question with a known answer borrowed from elsewhere?

---

### Principle 6

> Innovation at scale requires institutional scaffolding — not one-off programs, not hackathons, not isolated genius.

**What This Means & Why**
The dominant corporate innovation model is episodic: a hackathon here, an innovation lab there, an "intrapreneurship" initiative that lasts two years before being absorbed or killed. The evidence from organisations that have genuinely built innovation cultures shows something different — sustained, multi-year, structurally embedded mechanisms that make innovation a repeatable organisational capability rather than a periodic event.

**The India Reality**
Tata Innovista ran across 2,700 teams, 65 companies, 18 countries, and 30,000 participants over a decade. That scale of institutional commitment is the exception in Indian corporate life, not the rule. Most organisations launch innovation programs; few build innovation infrastructure.

**The Evidence**
In 'Lessons from the House of Tata on Technology and Innovation' (2015), Rishikesha T Krishnan uses the Tata Innovista example to argue that sustained institutional scaffolding — not one-off programs — is the mechanism by which innovation culture is created. The program's scale and decade-long duration are treated as causally significant, not incidental.

**The Implication**
If your organisation's innovation initiative has a sunset date or is owned by a single champion, treat it as a program, not a capability — and decide whether you are serious about building the latter.

---

### Principle 7

> India's real innovation opportunity is not to copy the West cheaper — it is to invent the model that the other 6 billion people will adopt.

**What This Means & Why**
Reverse innovation is not a consolation prize for countries that cannot afford frontier R&D. It is a structurally different design mandate — one that requires starting from deprivation, not from abundance, and produces solutions that are not merely cheaper but categorically different. GE's MAC 400 ECG machine, designed in India for Indian conditions, was not a cheaper version of the American machine. It was a different machine, built around different assumptions, that then found a global market. That is the template, and India has barely begun to exploit it.

**The India Reality**
R&D in developed countries is designed for 1 billion people. India's mandate, if it accepts it, is to design for the remaining 6 billion. India has 12 million of the world's 45 million blind — 80% preventably so. It is the diabetes capital of the world. It has the largest cohort of underserved healthcare patients on the planet. These are not problems to be managed; they are the brief for the next generation of global medical innovation.

**The Evidence**
In 'Innovating for a 6 billion-strong market' (2017), N Dayasindhu documents GE's MAC 400 ECG machine — designed in India at one-hundredth the cost of existing machines to handle dust, humidity, and fluctuating power — and argues: "R&D in developed countries will focus on 1 billion of the world's population while R&D in countries like India should focus on the rest of the 6 billion."

**The Implication**
Map the constraints of your most under-served Indian customer — power instability, price sensitivity, language barriers, infrastructure gaps — and treat those constraints as the design specification for a product that can eventually be exported globally.

### Principle 8

> The scientific method — not gut instinct, not experience, not pattern-matching — is the highest-performing decision tool available to innovating entrepreneurs.

**What This Means & Why**
Entrepreneurial intuition is celebrated in startup culture as a competitive advantage. The evidence is skeptical. A randomised trial of 116 Italian startups showed that founders who followed the scientific method — framing a hypothesis, designing a test, measuring outcomes, updating beliefs — got more customers, generated higher revenues, and were more willing to pivot or shut down when the evidence demanded it. The scientific method is not bureaucratic caution. It is the fastest known path from idea to validated insight.

**The India Reality**
Indian startup culture, like startup culture globally, runs on conviction narratives. The pitch is a story about certainty. The investor meeting is a performance of confidence. The incentive structure rewards those who sound certain, not those who know what they do not know. This is not a cultural quirk — it is a structural bias against the epistemic humility that makes the scientific method work.

**The Evidence**
In 'WFH Daily #94: Are you measuring what matters?' (2020), Founding Fuel cites the Italian startup study: entrepreneurs who followed the scientific method — framing, hypothesis, testing, metrics — got more customers, higher revenues, and were more willing to pivot or shut down. The contrast with gut-instinct-led founders is explicit and statistically robust.

**The Implication**
For every major product or strategy decision, write down the hypothesis being tested, specify in advance what evidence would change your mind, and commit to reviewing that evidence before the next decision point.

### Principle 9

> System redesign beats technology retrofit — every time.

**What This Means & Why**
The default corporate approach to innovation is additive: take what exists, add new technology, improve performance. The evidence from genuine transformations shows that the most important innovations required scrapping the existing system entirely and redesigning from the base assumptions up. This is harder, slower, more expensive in the short term, and almost always the only path to the outcome that matters.

**The India Reality**
India's infrastructure constraints — power instability, poor roads, thin last-mile connectivity — mean that technology retrofitting almost never works at scale. The solutions that have worked, from Aadhaar-enabled service delivery to Aravind Eye Care's assembly-line surgery model, were ground-up redesigns, not improvements on inherited systems.

**The Evidence**
In 'Sandith Thandasherry and what it takes to run a boat on solar power' (2020), N S Ramnath documents that Thandasherry did not add solar panels to a wooden ferry. He redesigned from scratch using fibre-reinforced plastic and aluminium, cutting the vessel's weight from 35 to 17 tonnes, creating a system in which solar power became viable. The principle the article makes explicit: "for new technology to work, often, you will have to redesign an entire system."

**The Implication**
When a new technology is not performing as expected in your organisation, ask first whether you are retrofitting it onto a system designed for different assumptions — and budget for the system redesign, not just the technology implementation.

### Principle 10

> Frictions kill more innovations than lack of ideas — and innovators almost never study the friction.

**What This Means & Why**
The standard innovation model focuses almost entirely on making solutions more attractive — better features, lower prices, stronger marketing. It systematically ignores the other side of the equation: the habits, anxieties, inertia, and switching costs that work against adoption. A product can be demonstrably superior and still fail because the friction of switching exceeds the value of the improvement. This is not a marketing problem. It is a design problem. And it requires a different kind of customer research than most organisations conduct.

**The India Reality**
India's navigation technology story captures this precisely. The product worked; the users would not switch from human guides to digital ones. The habit was not the absence of a better alternative — it was the presence of a trusted existing practice. "The fiercest competition for new technology is rarely another product. It's the habit people already trust."

**The Evidence**
In 'FF Daily #382: The battle between fuel and friction' (2021), Founding Fuel endorses Nordgren and Schonthal's argument that "by focusing on Fuel to enhance attraction, innovators neglect the other half of the equation — the Friction that works against the change we seek to create." In 'Maps, History, and Finding the Throughline' (2025), Shrinath V documents how India's navigation adoption required three different pitches for Delhi, Mumbai, and Bangalore — not because the product was wrong, but because the friction was different in each market.

**The Implication**
Add a friction audit to every product launch process: explicitly map the habits, anxieties, and switching costs that will work against adoption, and design mitigation strategies for each before launch.

### Principle 11

> India has built a world-class R&D infrastructure for multinational corporations — and almost none of it translates into indigenous innovation capability.

**What This Means & Why**
The 1,250-plus Global Capability Centres in India, employing over a million people and generating nearly $16 billion in R&D revenue, are routinely cited as evidence that India is an R&D powerhouse. The claim is half-true. India is an excellent location for high-quality, cost-effective R&D execution on behalf of foreign companies. What it has not built is the institutional capacity to own, commercialise, or compound that R&D into Indian products. The talent flows in; the intellectual property flows out.

**The India Reality**
Texas Instruments set up its Bangalore centre in 1985. Four decades later, GCCs have displaced CSIR and ISRO as the largest hubs of R&D activity in India. But the innovation output of those centres belongs to American, European, and Japanese companies. India has been an R&D destination without becoming an R&D origin.

**The Evidence**
In 'India is an R&D hub for MNCs. Will global protectionism play spoilsport?' (2019), Rishikesha T Krishnan documents that GCCs "have displaced government-owned and operated research laboratory networks such as CSIR and ISRO as the largest hubs of R&D in India" — and that while GCCs served as learning grounds for startup founders, the structural question of whether this creates indigenous R&D capability remains unanswered.

**The Implication**
If your organisation uses GCC talent, create an explicit mechanism — a ring-fenced team, a separate IP ownership structure, a product initiative — to convert that R&D execution capability into a product your organisation owns.

### Principle 12

> Outsiders solve problems that insiders cannot — and the reason is the expert's curse, not the novice's luck.

**What This Means & Why**
The standard assumption in innovation is that deep domain expertise is the prerequisite for solving hard problems. The evidence from open innovation, prize competitions, and cross-disciplinary breakthroughs consistently shows the opposite: insiders are trapped by the accumulated knowledge of their field, which narrows the solution space they can see. Outsiders, lacking that knowledge, approach the problem without the constraint of knowing what is supposed to be impossible.

**The India Reality**
Team Indus — celebrated by FF as one of India's most important innovation stories — had no aerospace background. They were entering a Google Lunar XPRIZE competition against established space agencies. The absence of orthodox aerospace training was not a handicap; it was a design advantage.

**The Evidence**
In 'FF Daily #446: Why outsiders may be better at problem-solving' (2021), Founding Fuel cites the InnoCentive case: John Davis, a chemist with no oil industry experience, solved Exxon Valdez's oil-separation problem by applying a concrete vibration analogy. Epstein's explanation: "We tend to view things with all the information we've gathered in our industry, and sometimes that puts us down a path that goes into a wall."

**The Implication**
When a problem has resisted internal solution for more than six months, bring in an outsider from a completely unrelated field and specifically ask them to ignore what cannot be done.

### Principle 13

> Innovation metrics as currently designed systematically measure the wrong things — and in doing so, destroy the conditions they claim to create.

**What This Means & Why**
The attempt to make innovation legible — to track it, report it, optimise it — introduces a systematic bias toward what is measurable, which is almost always incremental rather than transformational. The calculative mode of thinking is the enemy of the imaginative mode. Roger Martin's argument is precise: customer behaviour will never be transformed by a product whose design is based on an analysis of past behaviour. The data that would predict a breakthrough does not exist until after the breakthrough.

**The India Reality**
India's Bloomberg Innovation Index ranking is systematically biased against India's actual innovation competencies. The index rewards R&D-intensive, high-tech manufacturing — a profile India does not match. India's real strengths — affordability innovation, accessibility innovation, social innovation at scale — are unmeasured. The metrics do not just fail to capture Indian innovation; they actively discourage it by defining innovation in someone else's image.

**The Evidence**
In 'Tracking the progress of innovation in India' (2016), Rishikesha T Krishnan argues that "the driving force for innovation in India is improving accessibility and affordability. But, both the WIPO-Insead Global Innovation Index and Bloomberg don't capture these dimensions well." In 'FF Insights #684: The enemy of creativity' (2022), Founding Fuel endorses Roger Martin's argument that metrics "inhibit risk-taking, an inevitable concomitant of exploration and creativity."

**The Implication**
Design your organisation's innovation scorecard to include at least one metric that cannot be derived from historical data — a measure of directional bet-taking, not just output delivered.

### Principle 14

> Purpose, not quarterly results, is the only time horizon from which transformational innovation is possible.

**What This Means & Why**
Long-cycle R&D — the kind that produces genuinely new categories — requires investment that cannot be justified on a quarterly basis. The obsession with short-term returns does not merely slow innovation; it makes certain kinds of innovation structurally impossible. This is not an argument against accountability. It is an argument that the accountability structure must match the time horizon of the problem being solved.

**The India Reality**
India's corporate R&D investment, already anaemic at below 1% of GDP, is further constrained by the quarterly reporting culture imported from public markets. The companies that have made genuine innovation contributions — Aravind Eye Care, Narayana Health, Isro — have all operated outside the quarterly accountability regime, either as not-for-profits, government entities, or unusually patient private enterprises.

**The Evidence**
In 'How to innovate for India' (2017), Indrajit Gupta writes: "The obsession with quarterly results has bred a culture of short-termism. Such long-term innovative solutions often get sacrificed at the altar of expediency." In 'Building Back Better: Lessons from Zoho's founder' (2022), Founding Fuel documents Sridhar Vembu's explicit critique of Jack Welch-era financialisation: "India is tempted to follow — not invest in R&D, manufacturing prowess, and basic capabilities, and instead go for cheap finance. India should be careful not to copy this approach."

**The Implication**
Identify the one innovation initiative in your organisation that requires a five-year runway and explicitly remove it from the quarterly review cycle — protect it from the accountability structure designed for the existing business.

### Principle 15

> The innovation potential of India's public digital infrastructure is still vastly underestimated — by the private sector, by policymakers, and by the press.

**What This Means & Why**
Aadhaar, UPI, and India's broader Digital Public Infrastructure stack are not welfare programs with a technology interface. They are innovation platforms — substrates on which entirely new categories of business can be built, at a cost structure that no private player could have assembled independently. The degree to which India's private sector has failed to think architecturally about this infrastructure — treating UPI as a payment feature rather than a financial OS — represents one of the largest missed opportunities in Indian business history.

**The India Reality**
Morocco is building its identity system on India's open-source platform. Google urged the US Federal Reserve to implement a UPI-like system. The Bank for International Settlements called India's approach "relevant and applicable for all economies." India built something the world wants — and India's own private sector largely treats it as a commodity.

**The Evidence**
In 'Lessons from India's tortuous path to make tech work for the people' (2020), NS Ramnath documents that UPI crossed 1.3 billion transactions in December 2019, Aadhaar was issued to 1.25 billion people, and Morocco was building its identity system on India's open-source platform. In 'The Billion-User Bet' (2025), NS Ramnath traces Nilekani's "billion-user bet" from 2015 prediction to vindication, arguing the virtuous cycle of adoption driven by identity, network effects, and falling smartphone costs is still in early innings.

**The Implication**
Map your organisation's product roadmap against India's DPI stack and identify one service that could be rebuilt — not integrated, but rebuilt — on top of Aadhaar, UPI, or Account Aggregator infrastructure at a fraction of the current cost.

### Principle 16

> R&D tax breaks do not drive R&D decisions — competitive intensity does.

**What This Means & Why**
The standard policy prescription for increasing R&D investment is fiscal incentives: tax breaks, credits, deductions. The evidence is that these instruments reward behaviour that was already happening, at the margin that is easiest to game, without changing the fundamental calculus. Companies do not invest in R&D because the tax treatment is favourable. They invest because the competitive environment demands it — because not innovating will kill them faster than the cost of innovating.

**The India Reality**
India's R&D tax policy has been in place for decades. R&D spending has not crossed 1% of GDP. The correlation between the policy and the outcome is negative. The causal mechanism — competitive intensity — has been suppressed by licensing regimes, import protection, and the absence of the kind of market pressure that makes R&D existential.

**The Evidence**
In 'If not tax breaks to seed innovation, then what?' (2015), Sachin Joneja argues that R&D tax breaks reward wrong behaviour at the wrong time and that "the only thing that can work is to increase the competitive intensity of the marketplace." He describes Xerox PARC as the world's "most famous vanity project" — proof that even well-resourced, incentivised R&D produces nothing if the competitive pressure to commercialise is absent.

**The Implication**
Advocate internally for exposing your business unit to external competitive pressure — a specific competitor benchmark, a customer defection metric, a market share floor — as the condition that makes R&D investment real rather than performative.

### Principle 17

> India is a bazaar, not a cathedral — and this is a competitive advantage that Indian innovators keep trying to overcome rather than exploit.

**What This Means & Why**
The cathedral model of innovation is top-down, deliberate, and orderly. The bazaar model is open, improvisational, and emergent. India's technology adoption has consistently followed the bazaar pattern — users discovering use cases the designers never anticipated, reshaping tools for contexts they were never designed for, unlocking business models rooted in local knowledge. This is not a consolation prize for the absence of a cathedral. It is a structurally different capability, and one that the West is beginning to recognise as the model for the next generation of platform innovation.

**The India Reality**
A Tamil caller tune that builds patient trust. Voice AI for mandi rates in Marathi. The missed call as a communication protocol that half of Indian phone users had independently invented before any company formalised it. These are not edge cases — they are the pattern. Indian users do not wait for products to be designed for them; they redesign products for themselves.

**The Evidence**
In 'From Bazaar to Breakthrough' (2025), Shrinath V writes: "India's strength lies in stretching technologies to local needs, reshaping them, and unlocking business models rooted in context." He cites the mobile telephony arc — CRBTs as digital self-expression for first-time mobile users — and argues: "Use cases are not always discovered by companies designing products or services. They're discovered by users."

**The Implication**
Build a formal mechanism to surface unsanctioned user adaptations of your product — the workarounds, the unexpected use cases, the features being ignored — and treat the most common adaptations as your next product specification.

### Principle 18

> AI's pull toward the statistical average is the single largest structural threat to breakthrough innovation in the next decade.

**What This Means & Why**
Generative AI learns from historical data and optimises around statistical averages. This makes it extraordinarily useful for executing known solutions at scale, and extraordinarily poor at discovering unknown ones. The danger is not that AI is wrong — it is that AI is consistently, systematically, persuasively average. In domains where the breakthrough is a deviation from the mean, AI does not merely fail to help; it actively points away from the answer.

**The India Reality**
India is in the early stages of integrating AI into R&D, product development, and decision-making. The organisations deploying AI most aggressively are deploying it on the problems where it performs best — pattern recognition in known solution spaces. The problems where India most needs innovation — healthcare access, agricultural resilience, financial inclusion for the unbanked — are precisely the problems where historical data is thinnest and deviation from the average is most valuable.

**The Evidence**
In 'When AI Raises the Average, Who Protects the Breakthrough?' (2026), Arjo Basu and Debleena Majumdar argue that "generative AI learns from historical data and optimises around statistical averages... it tends to pull decisions toward the middle rather than away from it. It smooths variation, elevates what is most common, and treats unexplained deviation as noise." They connect this to the US military cockpit finding from 'FF Insights #630: Design for the extremes' (2022): "Metrics, and especially averages, encourage you to focus on the middle of a market, but innovation happens at the extremes."

**The Implication**
For every R&D or product decision where AI is generating options, explicitly review the options it did not generate — the outliers it smoothed away — and assign a human to evaluate whether any of the excluded options are the most important ones.

### Principle 19

> All technologies are first opposed — and the opposition is manufactured, not organic, by those invested in what the technology replaces.

**What This Means & Why**
The standard framing of technology resistance is sociological: people fear change, incumbents lobby against disruption. Calestous Juma's research adds precision: the opposition is not random or culturally determined. It is structurally manufactured by the specific actors who have the most to lose from the transition. Understanding this means that the question for any technology innovator is not "will there be opposition?" — there will — but "who will manufacture it, through what channels, and with what arguments?"

**The India Reality**
Aadhaar's trajectory is the defining Indian case. A technology that has now issued identity to 1.25 billion people and underpins the world's fastest-growing payment system was subjected to sustained, well-organised opposition at every stage of its development. Understanding that opposition as manufactured — by specific actors with specific interests — rather than as organic public concern changes how innovators should respond to it.

**The Evidence**
In 'Aadhaar and tech entrepreneurs' (2018), Charles Assisi invokes Calestous Juma's 'Innovation and its Enemies' to argue: "All technologies are first opposed" and "Opposition to any new technology will be manufactured by those invested in current technologies that work in the existing ecosystem." The article is explicit that this is not a claim about the merits of opposition but about its structural origin.

**The Implication**
When your innovation encounters organised opposition, map who has a financial or institutional interest in the status quo before deciding how to respond — and distinguish that manufactured opposition from legitimate user concerns.

### Principle 20

> The smartest people do not work for you — and the companies that act as if they do will consistently be out-innovated by those that build open pipelines to the outside.

**What This Means & Why**
The closed innovation model — build your lab, hire the best people, protect your IP — was a competitive advantage when talent was scarce and information moved slowly. Neither condition holds. The organisations doing the most consequential innovation today are those with the best pipelines to external knowledge, not the largest internal R&D departments. This is not a collaboration platitude. It is a structural claim about where the knowledge that matters most is located.

**The India Reality**
India's industry-university collaboration share of research papers declined at -6.1% CAGR between 2009 and 2013. China grew at +1.3% CAGR in the same period. India has the talent in its universities and the problems in its industries — but the institutional infrastructure connecting the two is among the weakest in any major economy.

**The Evidence**
In 'Smart people don't work for you' (2018), Founding Fuel cites Prof. Mohanbir Sawhney of Kellogg: "The smartest people don't work for you" and "The thinking needs to change from 'the lab is my world' to 'the world is my lab.'" In 'For ease of doing science, India must foster ease of collaborations' (2021), N Dayasindhu documents India's deteriorating industry-university collaboration metrics against China's improving ones.

**The Implication**
Identify one university department or research institution working on a problem adjacent to your core business, assign a senior technical person to build a genuine relationship with them over twelve months, and fund one joint project — not a CSR initiative, a real R&D partnership.

---

### Principle 21

> Innovation happens at the extremes of the design space — designing for the average user guarantees mediocrity.

**What This Means & Why**
The US Air Force discovered in the 1950s that designing cockpits for the "average pilot" was literally killing people — because not a single one of 4,000 measured pilots was within the average range on all ten bodily dimensions. The average is a statistical artefact. No one lives there. Products designed for the average serve no one well, and in a market as heterogeneous as India — in language, income, infrastructure, literacy, and aspiration — the average is a particularly dangerous fiction.

**The India Reality**
India is not one market. It is several hundred overlapping markets — by language, by state, by urban-rural divide, by income cohort, by infrastructure availability. The navigation product that worked in Mumbai did not work in Delhi. The rural innovation that succeeded in Tamil Nadu did not transfer to Bihar. Designing for an imagined average Indian user is not a simplification — it is a design error.

**The Evidence**
In 'FF Insights #630: Design for the extremes' (2022), Founding Fuel cites Rory Sutherland: "Metrics, and especially averages, encourage you to focus on the middle of a market, but innovation happens at the extremes." The cockpit finding — "Not a single pilot of the 4,000 measured was within the average range on all ten bodily measures" — is treated as a universal design principle.

**The Implication**
Identify the most extreme user in your target market — the one with the least resources, the worst connectivity, the most atypical use case — and design your next product to work for them first, then test whether it works for everyone else.

---

### Principle 22

> The core competence doctrine has kept India's most capable companies from building the capabilities they actually need.

**What This Means & Why**
The Prahalad-Hamel argument — focus on what you do best, outsource everything else — has been the dominant strategic doctrine in Indian corporate life for three decades. The evidence from India's most pressing innovation gaps suggests this doctrine is now the primary structural barrier to the next level. A services industry that refuses to invest in semiconductors or cloud infrastructure because it is "not our core" is an industry that will remain dependent on the infrastructure its customers control.

**The India Reality**
India's semiconductor dependence is total. Its cloud infrastructure is largely foreign-owned. Its AI models are being built on compute and data owned by American companies. The core competence doctrine — applied sensibly for twenty years — has produced an economy that is excellent at executing on the infrastructure others build and owns none of the infrastructure that will matter most in the next twenty years.

**The Evidence**
In 'Building Back Better: Lessons from Zoho's founder' (2022), Founding Fuel documents Sridhar Vembu's critique: "The idea of core competence should be challenged. It has kept, for example, the software industry in India from investing in semiconductors or cloud infrastructure." Vembu is explicit that this is not a general attack on specialisation but a specific critique of a doctrine that has been applied beyond its useful range.

**The Implication**
Identify one capability adjacent to your core that, if owned rather than outsourced, would give you structural independence in your most critical value chain — and begin building it at a scale that is strategically meaningful, not merely exploratory.

## FF's View

**The Core Conviction**

FF's central argument on innovation and R&D is this: India has a talent surplus and an innovation deficit, and the gap between them is not explained by ability. It is explained by system design. The country produces engineers in numbers that stagger the world, exports leaders who run Google and Microsoft and Adobe, and has demonstrated repeatedly — in Aravind Eye Care, in UPI, in Isro, in the MAC 400 ECG machine — that it can produce genuinely original solutions to genuinely hard problems. And yet, as Rishikesha T Krishnan wrote in 'Tech-savvy entrepreneurs, can you put India on the global innovation map?' (2015), Indian companies have stayed "quite some distance away from radical technological innovation." NR Narayana Murthy's question — "Why has there been no earth-shaking invention or technology from India in the last 60 years?" — is one FF takes seriously rather than deflects.

The explanation FF returns to, across a decade of reporting, is systemic. India has consistently mistaken the activity for the capability, the input for the outcome, the announcement for the institution. It has celebrated Sundar Pichai's promotion while ignoring InMobi's Miip launch. It has funded apps rather than platforms. It has measured R&D by spend rather than by the problems solved. It has imported Silicon Valley frameworks and applied them to contexts they were not designed for. It has substituted jugaad — the brilliant hack — for the sustained, patient, institutionally supported work that produces durable innovation.

What FF believes that most practitioners resist is precisely this: innovation is not a talent problem. It is a system design problem. And until India redesigns the systems — the metrics, the incentives, the institutions, the risk capital, the culture of questioning — it will continue producing the raw material for other people's breakthroughs.

That is the conviction this essay defends.

---

**What India Means for This Theme**

India changes the rules of innovation in ways that imported frameworks consistently miss. The changes are structural, cultural, and institutional — and they run in multiple directions simultaneously, some as disadvantages, some as advantages that are not yet legible to the frameworks doing the measuring.

Start with what is genuinely distinctive about the mandate. In 'Tracking the progress of innovation in India' (2016), Krishnan poses the foundational question: "In a country with the largest number of poor and under-nourished people on the planet, what kind of innovation matters, and how should we measure it?" The Bloomberg Innovation Index, the WIPO-Insead Global Innovation Index — both measure what matters in R&D-intensive, high-tech manufacturing economies. India's core innovation competencies — affordability, accessibility, inclusivity — are systematically unmeasured by both indices. India appears to underperform not necessarily because it innovates less but because it innovates differently, along dimensions the dominant indices cannot see.

This is not a defensive argument. It is a structural observation with practical implications. When GE Healthcare India designed the MAC 400 ECG machine — built ground-up for Indian conditions, powered by battery because rural infrastructure demands it, sold at one-hundredth the cost of existing machines — it was not doing a lesser version of what GE does in Cincinnati. It was doing something categorically different: designing for constraints that the American market does not impose. As N Dayasindhu argued in 'Innovating for a 6 billion-strong market' (2017), "R&D in developed countries will focus on 1 billion of the world's population while R&D in countries like India should focus on the rest of the 6 billion." This is not a consolation prize. It is India's actual innovation frontier.

The Bharat dimension changes the analysis further. The problems are different in scale and kind. Twelve million of the world's 45 million blind are in India, and 80 percent of Indian blindness is preventable — as Soum Paul reported in 'Two questions that led to an innovative device to prevent blindness' (2017). Fifty million Indians have Type II diabetes. India is the diabetes capital of the world, as Indrajit Gupta noted in 'How to innovate for India' (2017). The infant mortality numbers are worse than Bangladesh and Nepal at comparable spending levels. These are not development statistics — they are innovation briefs. They describe problems at a scale that has no parallel in any high-income economy, and they define a design space that no Western R&D lab is optimizing for.

The infrastructure gap is equally defining. In 'With patient capital to fix basic problems, India too can think big' (2016), Tom Hyland made a point that most imported innovation frameworks take as settled: "In India you can't take these pieces for granted, and in order to ensure a seamless customer experience — whether it's healthcare, small business loans, or delivering vegetables — you largely have to build the stack and its component parts yourself." The assumption baked into Lean Startup, into Design Thinking, into most American innovation pedagogy, is that infrastructure exists. In India, the innovator often has to build the infrastructure before they can build the product. That changes timelines, capital requirements, team structures, and the definition of what counts as a breakthrough.

The language barrier is real and almost never discussed in elite innovation circles. India has 800 languages. The entire backbone of the internet — and of most innovation toolkits — was built in English. As Hyland noted in the same article, this is a specific structural barrier to digital inclusion that Western tech discourse ignores entirely.

But India also has structural advantages that are underappreciated. The country has, as Indrajit Gupta wrote in 'How to innovate for India' (2017), produced in Aravind Eye Care, Narayana Health, and the Jaipur Foot a set of models that are not just cost-reduced versions of Western healthcare — they are architecturally different solutions that the West is now studying. India's DPI stack — Aadhaar, UPI, the broader IndiaStack architecture — has become, as N S Ramnath documented in 'Lessons from India's tortuous path to make tech work for the people' (2020), a global template. Morocco built its identity system on open-source platforms from India. Google urged the US Federal Reserve to implement a UPI-like system. The Bank for International Settlements called India's approach "relevant and applicable for all economies." India's constraints — the need to serve a billion people at near-zero marginal cost — produced a payment infrastructure more sophisticated than what the United States has built.

The jugaad heritage is real and double-edged. Charles Assisi, in 'Moderate constraints actually boost creativity. Here's why' (2017), argued that "frugal engineering and disruptive innovation are embedded in our psyche. We have always called it jugaad... by our very nature, we are built to make do with less." This is a genuine competitive advantage. But as the observations from Batch 3 note, the book "Saying No To Jugaad" — celebrated in FF's coverage — points to its limit: jugaad is a workaround, not a system. India's innovation challenge is to build the institutional scaffolding that converts the jugaad impulse into sustained, reproducible, globally competitive innovation.

The GCC story adds a dimension that is easy to miss. Texas Instruments set up its Bangalore centre in 1985. By 2019, as Krishnan reported in 'India is an R&D hub for MNCs. Will global protectionism play spoilsport?' (2019), 1,250 global capability centres employed over a million people and generated $15.7 billion in engineering and R&D revenue. These GCCs have, as Krishnan noted, "displaced government-owned and operated research laboratory networks such as CSIR and ISRO as the largest hubs of R&D in India." They have also been the training ground for entrepreneurs behind India's leading tech startups. India is already, in this sense, a global R&D hub — just not for Indian companies. The question is whether the capability accumulated in GCCs can be channelled toward building Indian products and platforms.

The Isro story is emblematic of something deeper. In 'The culture that led to Isro's world class capabilities' (2017), N Dayasindhu made a point that should be better known: Isro's earth observation data costs one-sixth of comparable international data. It is "probably one of its best kept secrets." Isro mastered the practices that management consultants now recommend — breaking silos, meritocratic culture, technical decision-making pushed to subject matter experts — "way back in the 1970s." India has proven models of excellence innovation. The problem is not that they don't exist. The problem is that they don't scale, and that India's broader innovation ecosystem has not studied them seriously enough.

**Where Global Best Practices Break Down**

The central failure of imported innovation thinking in India is the assumption that the bottleneck is knowledge. Give people design thinking training, lean startup methodology, a business model canvas — and innovation will follow. This assumption is wrong in the Indian context in three specific ways.

First, it assumes infrastructure that doesn't exist. Design thinking's five-stage process (empathize, define, ideate, prototype, test) was developed in contexts where you can order components on Amazon, find co-working spaces in any city, and test digital prototypes on an audience with smartphones and reliable data. In rural India, ethnography itself is a logistical challenge, prototyping requires building supply chains, and testing requires educating users before you can measure their responses. The stack has to be built before the methodology can be applied. As Hyland wrote in 'With patient capital to fix basic problems, India too can think big' (2016), the innovator in India must often build the infrastructure they're innovating on. This is not a minor qualification — it changes what counts as a minimum viable product and what counts as a viable team.

Second, imported innovation frameworks consistently misread India's market structure. The dominant HBR/McKinsey orthodoxy on innovation assumes a market that is either mass affluent or clearly segmented. India's market is both simultaneously, with a complexity of purchasing power distribution, cultural variation, and institutional context that no single framework captures. The GE MAC 400 story is instructive: GE's global innovation process would never have produced it, because GE's global innovation process optimized for GE's global customer base. The MAC 400 required a separate team, a separate brief, and a willingness to accept radically different cost and functionality constraints. What management orthodoxy calls "frugal innovation" or "reverse innovation" is, in India's context, simply correct innovation for the actual user.

Third, the R&D tax break story illustrates the gap between policy orthodoxy and Indian reality. Sachin Joneja, in 'Is the R&D tax break really an incentive to innovate?' (2015), argued that R&D tax breaks reward the wrong behavior at the wrong time, don't actually drive R&D location decisions, and that the real driver of R&D is competitive intensity in the marketplace. "The only thing that can work is to increase the competitive intensity of the marketplace," Joneja wrote in the companion piece 'If not tax breaks to seed innovation, then what?' (2015). This directly contradicts the standard policy toolkit — and the evidence supports it. Regulatory-driven innovation, as in BS-VI norms, is real but insufficient. Tax incentives produce R&D theater rather than R&D substance.

The Silicon Valley model breaks down at the VC level too. As Krishnan noted in 'Getting The Funding Piece Right' (2015), the Indian VC community "uses a cookie cutter approach with very limited imagination. They would rather blow their cash on an app (without proof of concept) than on a good service model that has growth potential." The best practice from Sand Hill Road — identify a large addressable market, back a strong team, move fast — produces in the Indian context a herd of food delivery apps and ride-sharing clones, not the deep-tech or product innovation that India's structural challenges actually require. Israel's Yozma model — fund of funds, tech investors with operating experience — is held up in 'Getting The Funding Piece Right' (2015) as the model India should have followed.

The pricing orthodoxy is a further case in point. Georg Tacke, in 'Innovation the smart way, an interview with Georg Tacke' (2017), demonstrated that 72-76 percent of new products fail because companies "build it and try to sell it" rather than "design the product around the price." The Amazon Fire Phone — built without any price discipline — is the canonical failure. In India, this problem is more acute: the price point is not a downstream consideration but an upstream constraint that defines the entire design space. Building for India's mass market and then figuring out pricing is backwards. The Mac 400 wasn't priced after it was designed — the price was the design brief.

The big data orthodoxy is particularly misleading for Indian organizations. In the 'Creating value with Little Data' series (2016), Nitin Srivastava made a case that still deserves wider hearing: for resource-constrained firms — which describes most Indian companies — incremental, manually managed data insights outperform expensive analytics infrastructure. "Big Data, with its mighty investments and equally spectacular returns" is the wrong frame for most of India. "Little Data has a very small effort and capability threshold to cross." The assumption that data science requires data infrastructure is wrong for the majority of Indian enterprises.

Where the skeptics of imported innovation orthodoxy are right: they are right that India's problems require India-specific methodology. They are right that the measurement frameworks used to assess India's innovation performance are structurally biased against what India actually does well. They are right that competitive intensity, not tax incentives, drives R&D. They are right that the jugaad impulse is a real starting point for innovation, even if it is an insufficient endpoint.

**How FF Diverges from Mainstream Media**

Mainstream Indian business media covers innovation the way it covers most things: through the lens of announcements, valuations, and personalities. A startup raises a hundred-million-dollar round — that is innovation. A CEO is profiled speaking about disruption — that is thought leadership. A government launches a scheme — that is policy. The deeper questions — Is this building genuine technical capability? Is this creating a product from first principles or replicating a foreign model? Does this address a structural Indian problem? — are rarely asked.

FF's divergence from this pattern is consistent and has been present from the earliest coverage. The Sundar Pichai example is emblematic. In 'InMobi's Miip May Be More Important to India than Pichai' (2015), Krishnan made a pointed argument: the hoopla in the English-language press about Pichai's appointment as Google CEO was "surely misplaced." What Pichai represented was Indians as "conformist managers who refrain from rocking the boat" — excellent execution in a culturally diverse company. InMobi's Miip launch, a genuinely original product innovation from an Indian company, "got lost in the noise." Mainstream media celebrated the manager; FF asked why India wasn't producing the founder.

The Valley Kool-Aid critique — Baba Prasad's formulation in 'Indian Innovation: Too much Valley Kool-Aid without the Valley' (2015) — is another divergence. FF named explicitly what mainstream media treated as aspiration: "the flow of ideas is one-directional. Ideas from the West are absorbed and spouted in India as if they were divine truth, but there is absolutely no return flow." This is not a nationalist argument. It is a diagnostic one. If India's innovation ecosystem imports frameworks without interrogating their provenance or applicability, it will keep producing solutions optimized for contexts that are not India.

FF was also early to articulate the difference between funding and innovation — a distinction that mainstream media collapsed entirely during the Flipkart-Snapdeal-Ola boom years. Haresh Chawla's 'How India's digital economy can rediscover its mojo' (2016) named it directly: "Mega-funding killed the innovation and hunger, the leanness and the sharpness. It created sprawling teams without focus. Funding became the distraction." While mainstream media was treating fundraising rounds as innovation milestones, FF was already writing its post-mortems. The consensus eventually caught up — the startup correction of 2022-23 validated much of what Chawla wrote in 2016.

FF has been consistently skeptical of Make in India as an innovation policy, again before this became a mainstream critique. Kiran Karnik's argument — that Make in India is "a dated idea" and that the focus should be "Think in India" — appeared in 'The growth mindset' (2017) when the program was still being celebrated as transformational. The distinction matters: manufacturing assembly generates employment but does not build the capability infrastructure for innovation. India needs, as Raghuram Rajan and Rohit Lamba argued in the 2023 book noted by D Shivakumar in 'Eight best business books of 2023' (2023), to focus on services and tech-led industries rather than assuming that low-cost assembly will lead to higher value-added manufacturing.

The product software story is another area where FF was ahead. Amit Ranjan's 'Paving the road for world-beating software products from India' (2019) articulated a thesis that has become more mainstream since — that India's most celebrated tech unicorns are "based on ideas and business models that are copied from foreign innovators (with some local tweaks). Their outsized valuations are a result of them being the gatekeepers to the large Indian market, rather than from having created path-breaking products from first principles." The National Policy on Software Products, which Ranjan called a "watershed moment" — the first official recognition of software products as distinct from services — should have been front-page news. It was not.

FF has also been more rigorous than mainstream media in holding two truths simultaneously: that India's DPI stack is a genuine global contribution, and that it has traveled a "tortuous path," in Ramnath's phrase, precisely because the political and institutional conditions for such infrastructure are fragile and contested. The Aadhaar story in mainstream media is either celebratory or conspiratorial. FF's treatment — documenting the innovation while tracking the opposition, the implementation failures, the privacy tensions — is more honest and more useful.

**Against Management Orthodoxy**

The received wisdom of business schools and consulting firms on innovation can be stated with fair precision: invest in R&D, build innovation labs, hire Chief Innovation Officers, deploy design thinking, celebrate failure, and the results will follow. FF's evidence base consistently contradicts the mechanisms behind this prescription, even when it agrees with some of the surface conclusions.

The first departure is on the Chief Innovation Officer. Krishnan, in 'Why you need to have a chief innovation officer' (2015), argues for the role while simultaneously noting its limits: "Innovation has to move from being the province of a few to a more widely spread activity." The orthodox prescription — designate someone responsible for innovation — is a structural solution to a cultural problem. It doesn't work unless the organizational context is redesigned. Tata's Innovista program, with 2,700 teams across 65 companies in 18 countries over a decade, is the counter-model: innovation as a sustained institutional practice, not a designated function.

The second departure is on R&D tax breaks, discussed above. The orthodox policy recommendation — incentivize R&D through tax treatment — is directly contradicted by Joneja's analysis. The evidence says competitive intensity drives R&D. Tax breaks subsidize what would have happened anyway.

The third departure is on TQM and efficiency. Indrajit Gupta, in 'Revving up Innovation' (2015), made a point that management orthodoxy resists: "TQM helped cut out wastage and made firms a lot more efficient; innovation, on the other hand, actually requires a bit of organisational slack." The efficiency imperative that has driven most corporate management since the 1990s is structurally hostile to the conditions innovation requires. This is not a niche argument — it goes to the heart of why well-managed companies fail to innovate. The boards grappling with this need to recognise, as Gupta argued, that the CEO who ran a tight operation in the previous decade may not be the right leader for the next one.

The fourth departure is on the Steve Jobs / lone genius myth. Linda Hill's "collective genius" argument, cited in 'Everybody Has a Slice of Genius' (2015), demolishes it: "innovation is a collaborative effort, featuring many individuals' contributions, several missteps, false starts and combinations of several ideas." The management orthodoxy that celebrates the visionary leader produces organizations that wait for the next visionary. The evidence says innovation is a system property. Hill's argument is supported by the India LIGO paper — over a thousand authors, as N Dayasindhu noted in 'For ease of doing science, India must foster ease of collaborations' (2021) — and by the Finnish factory worker story and the Coimbatore textile roller-skate innovation, both cited in Ramnath's 'What to copy from China' (2020).

The fifth departure is on design thinking as a strategic tool. Baba Prasad's 'What we need to make design thinking strategic' (2016) named an orthodoxy precisely: "There is an inherent danger in the design thinking methodology that renders it tactical rather than strategic." Design thinking produces good products. It does not, by itself, produce good strategy. The OODA loop — John Boyd's framework — is introduced as a strategic complement: faster cycle time confers competitive advantage independent of the quality of individual innovations. The two tools do different things. Deploying design thinking while hoping it will also do strategy's job produces well-designed products that lose strategically.

The sixth departure is on metrics. In 'FF Insights #684: The enemy of creativity' (2022), Roger Martin's argument was endorsed: "You can't chart a course for the future or bring about change merely by analysing history. Customer behaviour will never be transformed by a product whose design is based on an analysis of past behaviour." The orthodoxy — measure everything, manage to metrics, celebrate what you can quantify — is the enemy of genuine innovation. Metrics encourage focus on the middle of the market. As the US military cockpit story illustrates, and as Rory Sutherland is quoted in 'FF Insights #630: Design for the extremes' (2022): "Metrics, and especially averages, encourage you to focus on the middle of a market, but innovation happens at the extremes."

The seventh departure is on the correlation between data volume and insight quality. In 'The human intelligence behind intelligent machines' (2021), Dr. Bandyopadhyay pushed back directly against the Google orthodoxy — "We just have more data" — arguing that "It's not just about how many data points you have, but also how many features." The assumption that scaling data solves reasoning problems is precisely what the AI godfathers' more recent skepticism about LLMs is now confirming. Yann LeCun, cited in 'Are LLMs Just Clever Parrots?' (2025) by Shishir Prasad, argues: "There's absolutely no way that autoregressive LLMs... will reach human intelligence." The orthodoxy that more data plus more compute equals intelligence is being challenged by the people who built the systems on which the orthodoxy rests.

The eighth departure is on Jack Welch as management icon. Sridhar Vembu, in 'Building Back Better: Lessons from Zoho's founder' (2022), names Welch explicitly as a "false idol" — arguing that financialisation over engineering R&D is precisely the trap India should avoid. "India is tempted to follow — not invest in R&D, manufacturing prowess, and basic capabilities, and instead go for cheap finance. India should be careful not to copy this approach." This runs directly against the GE-as-excellence-model orthodoxy that dominated management education for two decades.

**The Entrepreneurial Mindset Beyond Startups**

The entrepreneurial lens on innovation is available to anyone who chooses to exercise it — and FF's evidence shows it is most consequential precisely where it is least expected: inside large organisations, public institutions, and government.

The Tata Innovista program is the clearest evidence. 2,700 teams, 65 companies, 18 countries, 30,000 participants over a decade — not a startup incubator but a sustained institutional practice inside one of India's oldest conglomerates. As Krishnan noted in 'Lessons from the House of Tata on Technology and Innovation' (2015), the program is evidence that sustained institutional scaffolding, not one-off programs, creates innovation culture. The entrepreneurial act here was the programme design itself — a decision by Tata leadership to treat innovation as an organization-wide capability rather than a specialist function.

Harsh Mariwala's account of Marico is a further case. In 'It's time to destroy your business' (2015), Mariwala described his approach: "I knew it wouldn't be easy and I would inevitably have to look outside for help... Even now, as an organization, we never shy away from listening to people who challenge our world-view. Else we fear we will atrophy." This is entrepreneurial mindset applied at the leadership level of an established consumer goods company — not a startup pivot but a deliberate institutional choice to remain perpetually uncomfortable.

Isro is the most striking case. In 'The culture that led to Isro's world class capabilities' (2017), Dayasindhu showed that Isro mastered the practices of breaking silos, meritocratic culture, and pushing technical decision-making to subject matter experts in the 1970s — decades before management theory gave these practices names. The entrepreneurial act was institutional: Vikram Sarabhai and his successors built a culture of technical excellence inside a government organisation, against the grain of everything government organisations typically optimise for.

The EkStep model represents the entrepreneurial mindset applied to public infrastructure. EkStep's societal platforms model — as discussed in 'Beyond Business: Using platform thinking to drive societal change' (2018) — applies the logic of platform businesses to civic problems: building open infrastructure on which civil society organisations, government, and market can all innovate. The innovation here is the model, not the technology. The entrepreneurial insight is recognizing that some problems are too large for any single actor and require infrastructure that enables distributed innovation.

The policy windows story from Ramnath's 'The hyperlocal WhatsApp strategy, policy windows and AI food tasters' (2019) extends the entrepreneurial frame to government itself. John Kingdon's three streams — problem, solutions, political will — converge briefly to create windows that close. The Golden Quadrilateral happened because someone was ready with a proposal when the window opened. Aadhaar used the 2009 UPA 2 election window. The entrepreneurial mindset in a policy context means recognising and preparing for these windows, not waiting for the system to create the right conditions.

The Nokia Chennai factory story, from Ramnath's 'What to copy from China' (2020), shows the entrepreneurial mindset operating at the shopfloor level: an assembly-line worker devised a weight-based quality check that was eventually adopted globally. No innovation title. No innovation budget. Just someone close to the problem who asked a question and acted on the answer. This is what FF means when it argues, across its decade of coverage, that innovation must move from "the province of a few to a more widely spread activity."

**On Transformation: Process, Not Just Destination**

How does change actually happen? FF's evidence is surprisingly consistent on the mechanism, even though the individual stories vary widely.

The first consistent finding is that transformation requires a specific type of leader commitment that is different from ordinary strategic priority-setting. Haier's Zhang Ruimin, as described in 'Haier is disrupting itself' (2015), didn't just announce a strategy to transform Haier into a micro-enterprise network. He eliminated management layers, restructured incentives, and accepted a period of operational chaos while the transformation played out. The destination was stated, but the process was built in real time. The same is true of Tata Innovista: a decade of sustained commitment, with institutional processes that survived leadership transitions.

The second finding is that transformation rarely follows the sequence that strategy documents propose. The Golden Quadrilateral story illustrates this: an accidental announcement that received a standing ovation became one of India's greatest infrastructure projects, because the political window happened to be open at the moment the proposal was made. Aadhaar's trajectory was similarly contingent — using the 2009 UPA election window, as Ramnath noted in 'The hyperlocal WhatsApp strategy, policy windows and AI food tasters' (2019). Transformation, in FF's evidence base, is often a matter of recognising and using windows of opportunity rather than executing a predetermined plan.

The third finding — and perhaps the most important — is that the scientific method applied to entrepreneurship and innovation produces measurably better outcomes than intuition. The Italian startup study cited in 'WFH Daily #94: Are you measuring what matters?' (2020) — 116 firms, randomised trial — showed that entrepreneurs who followed the scientific method got more customers, higher revenues, and were more willing to pivot or shut down failing experiments. The transformation mechanism is iterative: frame a hypothesis, test it, measure the outcome, update. This sounds obvious until you see how rarely it is actually done.

The fourth finding is that frictions kill more innovations than lack of fuel. Nordgren and Schonthal's argument, endorsed in 'FF Daily #382: The battle between fuel and friction' (2021), is that innovators consistently diagnose their problem as insufficient enthusiasm — and add more fuel — when the real problem is accumulated friction: habits, inertia, institutional resistance, switching costs. Transformation programs that work are often not the ones that build the best case for change but the ones that systematically remove the reasons not to change.

The fifth finding is the systems redesign point. Sandith Thandasherry's Aditya solar ferry — documented in 'Sandith Thandasherry and what it takes to run a boat on solar power' (2020) — is the proof case. He didn't add solar panels to a wooden boat. He redesigned the entire system: hull material, weight distribution, energy management, route planning. The transformation happened at the system level, not the component level. FF returns to this repeatedly: genuine transformation requires redesigning the system, not optimizing within it.

The sixth finding concerns the role of purpose. As Gupta wrote in 'How to innovate for India' (2017): "The obsession with quarterly results has bred a culture of short-termism. Such long-term innovative solutions often get sacrificed at the altar of expediency." The organisations that have achieved sustained innovation in FF's evidence base — Aravind Eye Care, Isro, Narayana Health, Tata's Innovista program — are all driven by purposes that extend beyond quarterly earnings. This is not a soft observation. It is a mechanism: purpose creates the tolerance for the short-term cost of long-term investment that transformation requires.

**The Human and Cultural Dimension**

FF treats the human and cultural dimensions of innovation not as soft factors to be acknowledged before returning to the real analysis, but as first principles that constrain what is possible. The evidence for this is consistent across a decade.

The IIT fixed mindset observation is telling. Parul Gupta, Springboard co-founder and IIT-B alumna, as quoted in 'An edtech entrepreneur's learning journey' (2021), described her experience: "In India, how we grow up, an almost 'fixed mindset' is beaten down in our heads. It was only when I started my entrepreneurial journey that I really pushed my boundaries... It was unlearning of everything I had lived my life by." The institution that is supposed to produce India's most capable innovators is, by one of its alumni's accounts, systematically conditioning them against the risk-taking and hypothesis-testing that innovation requires. This is a human factor, not a strategy factor — and no amount of innovation framework training will change it without addressing the underlying conditioning.

Baba Prasad's 'Indian Innovation: Too much Valley Kool-Aid without the Valley' (2015) surfaces a related cultural problem: the one-directional flow of ideas. India's innovation culture absorbs Western frameworks as received truth rather than as tools to be interrogated, modified, and occasionally rejected. The father-in-law story in the same article — a Harvard PhD who returned to Poona University in 1961 because "there was magic in the air in India then" — and the observation that this magic is now gone — is a cultural diagnosis, not a strategic one. The magic that drives innovation is not created by policy. It is created by the quality of questions people feel free to ask, the tolerance for failure, the willingness to challenge orthodoxy.

The Harish Hande observation from 'FF Daily #311: What's common to Leonardo da Vinci, Steve Jobs & Albert Einstein' (2021) is one of FF's sharpest on this theme: "If I look at the collapse of our industry in the rural areas, rural businesses, none of them had the capability to help them, because they did not know what the ecosystem was like. IIM/IIT graduates described as 'the most useless characters for advice' in rural contexts." The human factor here is epistemic: what you know, and how you learned it, determines what problems you can solve. Elite-institution graduates have been systematically educated away from the knowledge that rural and semi-urban India requires. This is not a skills gap that training fixes. It is a knowledge-source problem that requires different processes of learning.

The "missed call" story in 'FF Daily #349: A history of missed calls' (2021) is the human dimension of innovation at its most elemental. ZipDial's founders didn't invent the missed call — Indian users did, in the early 2000s, before cheap smartphones, as a way of communicating without paying. The innovation was recognising a user behavior and building a business around it. More than half of Indian phone users were calling people with the expectation that they wouldn't pick up. This is a human fact, not a market insight produced by data analytics. The companies that saw it did so through observation, not modeling.

The "Moses trap" — Edwin Land's story, told in 'FF Insights 681: The Moses trap' (2022) — is a human failure mode that no innovation methodology prevents. Land built Polaroid, produced genuinely radical technology, and then couldn't let go: "Polaroid declined, descended into debt, and eventually filed for bankruptcy" precisely because its founder was too much in love with his ideas. This is an emotional and psychological constraint, not a strategic one. FF names it as such, and the implication for Indian innovators — who often build companies around personal vision — is direct and uncomfortable.

The collective genius framework from Linda Hill, cited in 'Everybody Has a Slice of Genius' (2015), is finally a human argument about organizational culture: the conditions under which diverse contributions combine into genuine innovation are social and emotional, not structural. What Hill calls the "creative abrasion" that generates innovation — the productive collision of different perspectives — requires psychological safety, tolerance for dissent, and leaders who are willing to be wrong in public. These are not competencies that training produces. They are cultural conditions that leadership models over time.

**The Systems View**

Innovation in India, FF's evidence shows, is never a firm-level phenomenon. It is always embedded in ecosystems, policy environments, and institutional interdependencies that determine whether individual innovations survive, scale, or stall.

The IndiaStack story is the clearest evidence. UPI crossed 1.3 billion transactions in December 2019, as Ramnath noted in 'Lessons from India's tortuous path to make tech work for the people' (2020). But this number was not produced by any single company's innovation. It was produced by a system: Aadhaar providing identity verification, NPCI providing the payment rail, Jio providing cheap data access, a regulatory environment that mandated interoperability. Remove any one element and the system doesn't work at the scale it achieved. The innovation was the system design, not any individual component.

Nilekani's "billion-user bet" thesis, as Ramnath reported in 'The Billion-User Bet' (2025), was precisely a systems argument: that the combination of identity (Aadhaar), network effects, and falling smartphone costs would create a virtuous cycle of adoption. The prediction came true. But the prediction was only possible because Nilekani understood the system, not just the product.

The industry-university collaboration data is a system-level finding. As Dayasindhu reported in 'For ease of doing science, India must foster ease of collaborations' (2021), India's share of papers from industry-university collaboration declined at -6.1 percent CAGR between 2009 and 2013, compared to a global decline of -3.7 percent and China's growth of +1.3 percent CAGR. This is the structural problem behind India's inability to translate scientific talent into industrial innovation. No individual company's R&D investment can fix this. It requires changes to the institutional interface between universities and industry — funding models, IP regimes, researcher incentive structures.

The GCC story is a system-level observation too. GCCs were, as Krishnan noted in 'India is an R&D hub for MNCs. Will global protectionism play spoilsport?' (2019), "the learning ground for the entrepreneurs behind India's leading tech startups." The system produced an outcome — trained a generation of product managers and engineers — that no policy designed this as an objective. But the system is fragile: global protectionism threatens the GCC model, and India has not built enough alternative channels for this kind of technical training.

The Aditya solar ferry story illustrates the second-order effects of system redesign. In 'Sandith Thandasherry and what it takes to run a boat on solar power' (2020), the implications extend beyond transportation: if the hull design and weight specification that made solar power viable can be standardised, the innovation generalises to the entire ferry system, not just one vessel. The third-order effect is a change in what propulsion infrastructure is built — once lighter vessels are the norm, the economics of solar propulsion change for the entire category.

The policy window model — John Kingdon's three streams, cited in Ramnath's piece — is explicitly systemic: the problem stream, the solutions stream, and the political will stream run independently, and innovation policy happens when they converge. India's challenge is not that it lacks solutions — it has abundant solution proposals in circulation at any given time. It lacks the institutional capacity to hold problem and solution streams ready so that when political windows open, the country can move. The Golden Quadrilateral worked because someone was ready. Most windows close before anyone is ready.

The EkStep societal platform model takes the systems view furthest. As described in 'Beyond Business: Using platform thinking to drive societal change' (2018), the argument is that some problems — education, health, agriculture — require infrastructure on which multiple actors can innovate simultaneously, rather than a single best solution deployed at scale. This is a systems design argument that runs directly against the program-delivery model of most government and NGO intervention. The innovation is the architecture, not the content.

The food security R&D story adds a climate systems dimension. As reported in 'The race for food security' (2022), national farm output could fall 15-18 percent this century due to climate change — undoing the Green Revolution's gains. Usha Zehr's deployment of CRISPR, gene guns, and bacteria-based gene insertion at Mahyco is a firm-level innovation response to a systems-level problem. Whether it works depends not just on the science but on regulatory approval regimes, farmer adoption economics, seed distribution systems, and political tolerance for biotechnology — all system-level variables outside any single innovator's control.

**The Evolution of FF's Thinking**

FF's thinking on innovation and R&D has shifted in several important ways over its decade of coverage. The shifts are not reversals — core arguments made in 2015 hold in 2025 — but there have been genuine deepenings, sharpenings, and corrections.

The earliest coverage, from 2015, was more optimistic about frameworks. Design thinking was presented as "a systematic approach to problem solving that starts with customers" — and while caveats were present, the general tone was: here is a tool that works, deploy it. By 2016, Baba Prasad's critique in 'What we need to make design thinking strategic' had introduced a sharper note: design thinking is tactical, not strategic, and deploying it without strategic framing produces good products that lose strategically. By the Batch 4 and 5 periods, design thinking is treated as one tool among many — useful but insufficient.

The early coverage was also more focused on individual company and leader stories, with the systemic argument running as a thread but not always as the main frame. From 2017 onwards, the systems view becomes more central: Arun Maira's "Transforming Systems" framework, the societal platforms argument, the policy windows model, the industry-university collaboration data. FF's view of innovation became progressively less about what individual companies do and more about what ecosystems enable.

The China comparison deepened significantly between 2015 and 2020. The earliest references to China were comparative benchmarks — R&D spend at 2 percent of GDP versus India's sub-1 percent. By Batch 3, the China analysis is richer: the entrepreneurship culture, the speed of iteration, the quality of private-sector R&D, the implication that India faces a structural catch-up problem. Ramnath's 'What to copy from China' (2020) is a more nuanced treatment than the simple GDP comparisons of 2016.

The skepticism about AI hype sharpened dramatically in the Batch 5 period. Early coverage was exploratory — presenting AI capabilities, asking what they mean for work, citing both optimistic and skeptical voices. By 2025, FF's position is much more specific: LLMs are mimetic machines, not reasoning machines; the AI godfathers are stepping back from them; and the structural pull of generative AI toward the statistical average is a threat to breakthrough innovation, not an enabler of it. 'Are LLMs Just Clever Parrots?' (2025) by Shishir Prasad and 'Revolution Inside AI' (2025) by Sundeep Waslekar represent a clearer position than anything in the earlier batches.

The India product software argument has become more urgent. In 2015, the problem was named — Murthy's question, the absence of global household-name products. By 2019, Amit Ranjan's detailed analysis in 'Paving the road for world-beating software products from India' had given the argument structural depth. By 2025, the "bazaar to breakthrough" framing in Shrinath V's article represents a more positive version of the same argument — acknowledging India's genuine innovation in context adaptation and use-case discovery, while still pointing toward the product frontier as the unfulfilled aspiration.

What FF believed in 2015 that it no longer holds: a somewhat undifferentiated enthusiasm for startups as India's innovation vanguard. The 2016 Chawla piece on mega-funding destroying innovation was an early correction. By 2019, the critique of Indian unicorns as market gatekeepers rather than genuine product innovators was explicit. The startup is no longer, in FF's framing, the default innovation vehicle — it is one model among several, often inferior to the GCC, the public institution, or the established company operating with entrepreneurial intent.

**Key Voices**

Several contributors have shaped FF's thinking on innovation with unusual distinctiveness and consistency.

**Rishikesha T Krishnan** is the most prolific and structurally rigorous voice on India's innovation problem. His contributions span the full range: the foundational question (why no earth-shaking Indian invention?), the measurement problem (innovation indices are biased), the sector-level analysis (healthcare, semiconductors, GCCs, motorcycles), and the comparative frame (China, Israel, global benchmarks). At his sharpest, Krishnan refuses comfort — 'Tech-savvy entrepreneurs, can you put India on the global innovation map?' (2015) is an uncomfortable call to action, and 'India is an R&D hub for MNCs. Will global protectionism play spoilsport?' (2019) holds the complexity of India's R&D situation without collapsing it into either triumphalism or despair. His distinctive angle is the combination of academic rigour with practitioner relevance — he uses frameworks precisely when they illuminate and sets them aside when they don't.

**N S Ramnath** brings the sharpest systems and mechanisms thinking in FF's innovation coverage. His work on IndiaStack and UPI — from 'Aadhaar: A quiet disruption' (2016) through 'The Billion-User Bet' (2025) — traces a decade-long argument about India's DPI stack as a global template. His 'The culture that led to Isro's world class capabilities' (2017) and 'What to copy from China' (2020) show a distinctive willingness to take Indian institutional success stories seriously and learn from them precisely. At his sharpest, Ramnath holds both the aspiration and the specific mechanism together — he doesn't just say UPI is innovative, he explains the system conditions that made it possible.

**N Dayasindhu** represents the evidence-first, patient approach to Indian innovation's structural problems. His work on the Simputer ('What lessons does the Simputer hold for Make in India?', 2017), the GCC ecosystem ('India is an R&D hub for MNCs'), the DARPA model ('A mission to solve grand challenges', 2018), and the industry-university collaboration deficit ('For ease of doing science, India must foster ease of collaborations', 2021) builds cumulative evidence on specific structural problems without over-generalising. His distinctive angle is the case study as diagnostic tool — he uses Indian cases that are failures as carefully as cases that are successes.

**Charles Assisi** contributes the cultural and human dimension at its most unsparing. His 'The nonsense that is gut instinct' (2015), his coverage of Theranos, his treatment of the jugaad paradox, and his 'Aadhaar and tech entrepreneurs' (2018) — exploring Calestous Juma's "Innovation and its Enemies" — represent a consistent refusal to take innovation discourse at face value. Assisi is at his sharpest when he is naming what is not being said: that gut instinct is often rationalised groupthink, that celebrated innovations are often more contested than their origin myths suggest.

**Haresh Chawla** is the sharpest practitioner voice on what large-scale capital does to innovation culture. 'How India's digital economy can rediscover its mojo' (2016) remains one of the most prescient pieces in FF's innovation archive — a post-mortem written before the actual death. Chawla's angle is the insider's view: someone who has watched capital markets reward the wrong things, who can name the mechanism precisely, and who resists the temptation to substitute structural critique for actionable advice.

**Baba Prasad** brings the most distinctive conceptual contributions — the Valley Kool-Aid critique, the design thinking/strategy gap, the strategic agility framework. His work consistently pushes against the comfortable adoption of imported frameworks, insisting on asking what the framework is actually designed for before deploying it. At his sharpest, in 'Indian Innovation: Too much Valley Kool-Aid without the Valley' (2015), he articulates a cultural critique that no purely strategic analysis can reach.

**Sridhar Vembu** (as interview subject and intellectual referent in Batch 5) represents the practitioner-thinker archetype FF most consistently celebrates. His first-principles approach, his willingness to name Jack Welch as a false idol, his argument that the Indian software sector's "core competence" framing has kept it from investing in semiconductors and cloud infrastructure — these positions represent the kind of uncomfortable truth-telling that FF uses as a benchmark for quality thinking. As quoted in 'Building Back Better: Lessons from Zoho's founder' (2022): "I don't accept something as the revealed truth. I like to understand things from first principles, and sometimes a new pathway emerges."

**Shrinath V** represents the most recent evolution in FF's thinking on India's innovation identity. 'From Bazaar to Breakthrough' (2025) and 'Maps, History, and Finding the Throughline' (2025) articulate a frame that is both honest about India's failure to produce global breakthrough products and generous toward India's genuine innovation in use-case discovery and contextual adaptation. His distinctive angle is the specific case studied long enough to reveal its structural implications — the mobile telephony arc, the navigation product, the caller ring back tone as digital self-expression.

**What FF Is Skeptical Of**

FF's skepticism is consistent, specific, and in several cases proved right by events.

**Startup funding as innovation proxy.** The equation of fundraising with innovation is one of FF's most persistent targets. Haresh Chawla named it directly in 2016; by 2019, Amit Ranjan had made the structural case. India's most celebrated unicorns are market gatekeepers, not product innovators. Their valuations reflect access to a large market, not genuinely original technical capability. FF is not anti-startup — it has celebrated Team Indus, FreeCharge (for its zero-marketing-spend growth), and InMobi's Miip. But it is consistently skeptical of funding rounds as evidence of innovation.

**Make in India as an innovation policy.** Kiran Karnik's "dated idea" verdict, delivered in 2017, is FF's consistent position. Manufacturing assembly generates employment but does not build the capability infrastructure for genuine technical innovation. India needs "Think in India," not just Make in India. This is a structural argument, not a political one: the skills and institutions required for assembly are different from those required for design and R&D.

**Silicon Valley techno-optimism applied to India.** The question from 'With patient capital to fix basic problems, India too can think big' (2016) — "We know that the Indian government spends less than 1% of the GDP on healthcare. How can AI solve the problem that we have less than one hospital bed for 1,000 Indians?" — remains FF's template for this skepticism. Technology does not solve infrastructure deficits. AI does not replace hospital beds. Digital tools do not reach people without data access and digital literacy. The enthusiasm for technology-first solutions to India's development problems consistently underestimates the infrastructure prerequisites.

**LLM hype, specifically.** By Batch 5, FF's AI coverage has shifted from exploratory to specifically skeptical of the claims made for large language models. The godfathers themselves — LeCun, Hinton, Sutton — are skeptical. The structural argument from 'When AI Raises the Average, Who Protects the Breakthrough?' (2026) by Arjo Basu and Debleena Majumdar is more fundamental: generative AI optimises around statistical averages, pulling toward the middle of what has already been done. In a domain where innovation happens at the extremes, this is not a feature — it is a liability.

**Personalized medicine as near-term reality.** In 'The state of cancer research and the future of innovation' (2016), Krishnan named it directly: "At this point of time, personalised and targeted medicines seem to be more hype than reality." This skepticism, expressed early, was vindicated by the subsequent decade's experience with targeted therapies — genuine for specific cancer types, overstated as a general paradigm.

**Jugaad as a sufficient innovation strategy.** FF is supportive of jugaad as a starting point — the frugality impulse, the constraint-driven creativity, the willingness to make do. But it has consistently pointed to jugaad's limits as a scalable, reproducible, globally competitive strategy. The celebration of "Saying No To Jugaad" in Batch 3 is the clearest expression of this position: jugaad is a workaround for absent systems, not a substitute for building them.

**Correlation-based data science as innovation driver.** The 'Innovation: Starting with the real jobs that need to be done' (2017) piece made the case explicitly: "If you are a specific type of customer, you will buy a certain type of product, because there is a correlation between the two" is exactly the wrong frame for innovation. Christensen's jobs-to-be-done framework, contrasted with Tyler Vigen's spurious correlations, illustrates the point. Correlation finds patterns in existing behavior. Innovation changes behavior.

**What FF Celebrates**

FF's admiration has a consistent architecture. What it celebrates are not the largest companies or the highest valuations — it is the combination of genuine technical ambition, deep contextual understanding, and institutional persistence.

**Aravind Eye Care, Narayana Health, Jaipur Foot.** These appear across at least four separate batches as India's most important innovation stories. What they share: world-class clinical outcomes at radically lower cost, achieved by redesigning the care delivery system rather than importing Western models. They are not frugal versions of Western healthcare — they are architecturally different systems. As Krishnan noted in 'Affordable healthcare: India's big opportunity' (2017), they represent India's clearest claim to genuine global innovation leadership.

**Isro.** Not for its rockets but for its institutional culture. The fact that Isro mastered practices that management theory is still recommending — meritocracy, broken silos, technical decision authority at the problem — in the 1970s is, for FF, evidence that India can build world-class innovation institutions when the cultural and leadership conditions are right. The earth observation data pricing — one-sixth of international comparators — is the proof of competitive excellence.

**Team Indus.** Celebrated in multiple pieces for what it represents rather than what it achieved (it didn't land on the moon). The "audacious spunk" of a multi-disciplinary team with no aerospace background attempting an XPRIZE mission is, for FF, evidence of what India's innovation culture could produce if the ecosystem rewarded this kind of ambition. As Krishnan wrote in 'The importance of being Team Indus' (2016), Team Indus mattered precisely because most Indian startups were "centred around apps, offering convenience as their value proposition."

**Zoho.** The counter-model to VC-backed Indian tech. Bootstrapped, serving 50 million employees, built on first principles rather than imported frameworks. Vembu's willingness to challenge his own sector's "core competence" framing — arguing that it kept the Indian software industry from investing in semiconductors and cloud infrastructure — represents exactly the kind of self-interrogation FF treats as the beginning of genuine innovation. "You don't hear much about Zoho because it is bootstrapped and not funded by VC money," as the 2022 piece noted — and FF's celebration of this silence is deliberate.

**India's DPI stack.** The Aadhaar-UPI-IndiaStack architecture is FF's clearest example of India solving a problem at a scale and cost that the rest of the world is now studying. Not just the technology — the model: open infrastructure, interoperability mandates, government as platform architect rather than service provider. As Ramnath documented across a decade of coverage, from 'Aadhaar: A quiet disruption' (2016) to 'The Billion-User Bet' (2025), this represents India's most significant contribution to global innovation discourse.

**The informal innovators.** The Nokia factory worker with the weight-based quality check. ZipDial's founders recognising the missed call as a business model. The homemaker using image-to-text to help her child with schoolwork. These are not celebrated as stories of scale — they are celebrated as evidence of what Ramnath's 'What to copy from China' (2020) called innovation "by people closest to the problem." FF's consistent argument is that this distributed innovation capacity — present everywhere, recognised nowhere — is India's most underutilised asset.

**The Historical Lens**

FF uses history not decoratively but diagnostically — to reveal the mechanisms that produced the present and to challenge the assumption that the present is inevitable.

The semiconductor history is the deepest wound. 'When India made its first computer' (2022) used the CDC 1965 proposal — a core-memory manufacturing venture that "went into cold storage" after Bhabha's death in 1966 — to ask: "Did we miss an opportunity in the 1960s?" The framing is not nostalgic but structural: the decisions made in the 1960s about hardware manufacturing produced the imbalance that India lives with today — globally renowned for software, absent from hardware. The history doesn't determine the future, but it explains why the path back to hardware capability is longer and harder than ambition alone can cover.

The Green Revolution analogy runs through the food security coverage. India avoided famine through Green Revolution hybrids — a product of systematic agricultural R&D, patient capital, and government commitment. As the 2022 coverage of Mahyco's CRISPR work noted, climate change is beginning to undo that progress. The historical arc is precise: the same systematic R&D approach that produced the Green Revolution needs to be applied to the climate adaptation challenge. History here is not comfort — it is a demand.

Bhabha's institution-building story, told in 'The birth of a model scientific institute' (2021) by Hari Pulakkat, is used to make a specific argument about prerequisites. Bhabha's realization that "scientific institutions in India were small, badly run, and short of funds and ideas" led to TIFR — and the Tata philanthropic network was the enabling condition. The historical lesson: world-class science requires world-class institutions, and world-class institutions require patient capital from sources that are not subject to quarterly return requirements. This is as true now as it was in 1945.

The Simputer case — told in Dayasindhu's 'What lessons does the Simputer hold for Make in India?' (2017) — is used as a historical failure that illuminates present challenges. The Simputer was a legitimate innovation: a low-cost computing device designed for Indian conditions, built by Indian researchers, before the smartphone made it redundant. Its failure is attributed partly to the "paucity of risk funding for entrepreneurs" and the "absence of sophisticated hardware manufacturing in India." The lesson is not that the Simputer was a bad idea but that the ecosystem it needed did not exist — and has still not been fully built.

Raychaudhuri's equation — the mathematical foundation for Penrose's singularity theorems, Nobel-winning work — is used in 'WFH Daily #199: Roger Penrose's India connect' (2020) to make a cultural argument: India has deep scientific contributions that go unrecognised, both by the world and by itself. The historical lens here is corrective — against the narrative that India's scientific tradition is shallow or derivative. Raychaudhuri's contribution happened in 1955, well within the period that Narayana Murthy declared a scientific wasteland. The history is more complex than the complaint.

**Unresolved Tensions**

FF's decade of coverage generates genuine contradictions that cannot be cleanly resolved. They deserve to be named honestly.

The first is between jugaad as asset and jugaad as obstacle. FF celebrates constraint-driven innovation — the missed call, the Jeevan Bindi, the Nokia factory worker's weight check — as evidence of embedded Indian innovation capacity. It simultaneously argues, through the "Saying No To Jugaad" reference and the healthcare outcomes analysis, that India needs systematic innovation institutions, not workarounds. Both arguments are true. The tension is real: there is no clear answer to when jugaad is the right response and when it is a substitute for the harder work of building systems.

The second is between frugal innovation as India's comparative advantage and frugal innovation as a ceiling. The GE MAC 400, Aravind Eye Care, Embrace Innovations' sleeping bag incubator — these are celebrated as genuine global contributions. But as Amit Ranjan argued in 'Paving the road for world-beating software products from India' (2019), India's aspiration cannot stop at being the world's frugal innovation laboratory. The risk is that India becomes permanently specialised in low-cost solutions while other countries capture the high-value product markets. FF holds both positions — frugal innovation is real and valuable, and it is not enough — without resolving the tension.

The third is between the GCC model as training ground and the GCC model as capability trap. GCCs trained a generation of Indian engineers and product managers, and the entrepreneurs who built India's leading startups often came out of GCCs. But GCCs also captured India's best technical talent in service of foreign companies' product roadmaps. The capability that India needs — to build genuinely original products — is precisely the capability that GCC work does not develop. FF acknowledges both sides of this; the resolution is unclear.

The fourth is between the DPI stack as India's greatest innovation and the DPI stack as a political project. Aadhaar's path was "tortuous," as Ramnath noted — contested in courts, contested in politics, challenged on privacy grounds. FF celebrates the outcome while acknowledging the process. But the celebration of UPI's 1.3 billion monthly transactions sits in tension with the ongoing unresolved questions about surveillance, exclusion, and data governance that the same infrastructure creates. FF holds this tension explicitly but does not resolve it.

The fifth is on AI: between AI as a platform for India to innovate on and AI as a structural threat to India's frugal innovation advantage. The 'From Bazaar to Breakthrough' (2025) argument is that India's strength lies in adapting technologies to local needs. But if generative AI, as argued in 'When AI Raises the Average, Who Protects the Breakthrough?' (2026), systematically pulls toward the statistical mean, then India's contextual adaptation capacity — which operates precisely by deviating from the mean — is structurally disadvantaged. Both are true. The implication for India's AI strategy is not clear.

**The Missing Voices**

FF's coverage of innovation and R&D, despite its breadth, has identifiable gaps. Naming them is not a criticism of what has been done — it is an invitation to what needs to be done.

The most significant absence is the factory floor and the agricultural field as sites of innovation. FF has nodded toward this — the Nokia factory worker, the Pusa decomposer, the agricultural trust deficit — but the systematic voices of manufacturing workers, agricultural scientists in government research institutions, and rural entrepreneurs are mostly absent. When Harish Hande calls IIT/IIM graduates "the most useless characters for advice" in rural contexts, the implicit critique is that FF's coverage is concentrated at the top of India's educational and economic pyramid. The innovation happening at the base of that pyramid — the daily problem-solving of people with no institutional support — is largely invisible in the evidence base.

The second absence is gender. FF's innovation coverage is almost entirely populated by male voices and male-led organisations. The Embrace Innovations sleeping bag incubator is an exception. The women's voices that appear in the evidence base — Parul Gupta on IIT conditioning, Usha Zehr on agricultural R&D — are present but rare. What women's experiences of India's innovation ecosystem reveal — about access to risk capital, about institutional culture, about the specific barriers and enablers that female innovators face — is largely unmapped in FF's archive.

The third absence is the vernacular tech ecosystem. FF's 'From Bazaar to Breakthrough' (2025) gestures toward this — the farmer checking mandi rates via voice AI in Marathi, the Tamil caller ring back tone building patient trust. But the innovators building in vernacular languages, serving markets that English-medium tech never reaches, are not yet central to FF's innovation narrative. Their innovations are less visible in the indices, less documented in the literature, less connected to the networks through which FF's evidence base is built — but they are arguably closer to the scale of India's actual innovation mandate than most of what gets reported.

The fourth absence is the public sector as innovator, beyond ISRO and a few celebrated cases. India's largest laboratories — CSIR, DRDO, ICMR — appear in FF's coverage mainly as evidence of dysfunction or as benchmarks that GCCs have displaced. The specific scientists working within these institutions, the structural constraints they face, and the innovations they have produced despite those constraints, are mostly absent. Whether the problem with India's public R&D is cultural, structural, or resource-related — or some specific combination — is a question FF's evidence base does not adequately answer.

The fifth absence is the perspective of India's tier-2 and tier-3 city entrepreneurs. FF's innovation coverage is predominantly about Bengaluru, Mumbai, Delhi, and occasionally Chennai. The specific challenges and innovations of entrepreneurs in Nashik, Coimbatore, Surat, Jaipur — cities that have produced significant industrial and commercial innovation by people who never appear in Global Innovation Index discussions — are underrepresented.

**What This Means For You**

**The belief to update:** You probably believe that India's innovation problem is primarily a talent or resource problem — that if you had more engineers, more capital, more R&D budget, more time, the innovation would follow. The evidence says this is wrong. India has world-class engineering talent. It has GCCs employing a million people doing R&D. It has produced UPI, Aravind Eye Care, the MAC 400 ECG machine. The bottleneck is not talent or resource — it is system design: the metrics you use to evaluate innovation, the organisational conditions you create for it, the questions you permit people to ask, the tolerance you extend to experiments that fail. As Krishnan has argued across a decade of FF's coverage, and as Chawla named in a single article, the problem is not raw material. It is what the raw material is pointed at, and what the incentive structure rewards. Update this belief before you update anything else — because as long as you think the problem is resources, you will keep solving the wrong problem.

**The thing to stop:** Stop treating fundraising rounds, valuation milestones, or award-show recognition as evidence of innovation. This is the most consistently destructive habit in India's innovation ecosystem. Chawla called it in 2016: "Mega-funding killed the innovation and hunger, the leanness and the sharpness." The fundraise announces ambition; it does not create capability. The valuation reflects market access; it does not reflect technical originality. As long as you use these as proxies for innovation, you will celebrate the wrong things, invest in the wrong companies, hire the wrong people, and measure the wrong outcomes. Stop. The measure of innovation is whether a genuinely hard problem is being solved in a way it could not be solved before — and that question is answerable without reference to term sheets.

**The thing to start:** Map the frictions, not just the fuel. In your next innovation initiative — whether it is a new product, a process redesign, an R&D programme, or a policy proposal — spend an equivalent amount of time identifying the habits, institutional resistances, switching costs, and vested interests that will prevent adoption, as you spend building the case for why it is a good idea. Nordgren and Schonthal's argument, endorsed in 'FF Daily #382: The battle between fuel and friction' (2021), is the most actionable finding in FF's decade of innovation coverage: innovators consistently over-invest in enthusiasm-building and under-invest in friction-removal. Name the specific friction — not "change management" as a category but the specific person who will resist, the specific process that will need to change, the specific incentive that currently rewards the old behaviour. Then address that friction directly, before you scale the idea. This is not a mindset shift. It is a Monday-morning practice.

The conviction to carry out: India's innovation advantage is not despite its constraints — it is because of them, but only if you build the systems that convert constraint-driven insight into reproducible, scalable, globally competitive capability. Jugaad without institutions is just survival. Institutions without jugaad's urgency are just bureaucracy. The work is building both at once.
