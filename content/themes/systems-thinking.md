# Systems Thinking

---

## PART 1 — THE OPENING

Every significant policy failure in India follows the same script. A well-funded programme launches. Pilots succeed. Funding scales. And then, somewhere between ambition and execution, the thing collapses — not because the idea was wrong, but because the intervention touched one lever in a system that had twenty others moving simultaneously in directions nobody tracked. India is, as Kiran Karnik has called it, a "graveyard of successful pilots." The graveyard is not a failure of intent. It is a failure of perception.

The problem is not that Indian practitioners lack intelligence or resources. The problem is that most of us are still operating with a mechanical model of the world — one where causes are near their effects, where interventions produce predictable outputs, where problems can be handed to specialists and solved in sequence. That model was always a simplification. In a country of 1.4 billion people, with the institutional complexity of India's federal structure, the caste and class and linguistic textures of its society, and the pace at which digital infrastructure is reshaping behaviour, it is now a dangerous one.

Systems thinking is not a management philosophy. It is a correction to a perceptual error. The error is this: we see edges, not connections. We see events, not feedback loops. We see side effects, not the effects of our own blindness. When India's health expenditure leaves it ranked 114th globally — just above Rwanda, sixty places behind China — that is not a resource problem. It is a systems design problem.

This page does not give you a framework to hang on a wall. It gives you the evidence for why your current mental model is failing, and what replacing it actually requires — in your organisation, your sector, and across the institutions that shape the conditions in which you operate.

## PART 2 — PRINCIPLES

---

### Principle 1

> There are no side effects in a system — only effects you failed to anticipate.

**What This Means & Why**
The phrase "side effect" is a confession. It means you built a model of the world, the world did something your model didn't predict, and you labelled the gap a side effect rather than a failure of your model. In complex systems, every intervention ripples outward through feedback loops, time delays, and interdependencies. The ripples are not side effects — they are the effects. The reason practitioners keep getting ambushed by them is that we draw the boundary of our analysis too narrowly, too early, and too comfortably.

**The India Reality**
India's public health system has been shaped by decades of narrow, vertical interventions — malaria programmes, polio drives, maternal health schemes — each optimised for its own metric, each generating second-order consequences that the next programme then had to navigate. The result is a system that spends less than 2% of GDP on health and still manages to produce perverse incentives at every level of delivery.

**The Evidence**
In 'FF Insights #703: System blindness' (2022), Founding Fuel draws on Daniel Goleman and MIT's John Sterman to make the point precisely: "In a system there are no side effects — just effects, anticipated or not. What we see as 'side effects' simply reflect our flawed understanding of the system." Sterman adds the darker corollary: short-term relief from a narrow fix makes the underlying problem return, often worse. The article cites the Nagaland example — where customary law, tribal bodies, and state government interact to produce zero women elected to the state assembly since 1963 — as a live instance of a system that legal interventions alone cannot unlock.

**The Implication**
Before you launch any intervention next week, map three second-order effects — not to prevent them all, but to know what you are choosing to accept.

---

### Principle 2

> Cause and effect are not neighbours. They are separated by time, distance, and the architecture of the system itself.

**What This Means & Why**
The human brain is built to connect events that are close together. If something goes wrong today, we look for what happened yesterday. If a business metric falls, we look at last month's decision. But in complex systems, the cause of what you are experiencing now may have been set in motion years ago, in a part of the organisation or ecosystem you are not watching. This is not just a cognitive inconvenience — it is the reason that most organisational interventions address symptoms and leave structures untouched.

**The India Reality**
India's agrarian distress has been attributed serially to monsoon failure, trader exploitation, input costs, and credit access — each one a proximate cause, each one attracting a targeted intervention, each one leaving the underlying system essentially unchanged. The structural causes — land fragmentation, absence of storage infrastructure, the gap between farm-gate and market prices — operate on timescales that make them invisible to the political and managerial cycles that govern most responses.

**The Evidence**
In 'FF Insights #703: System blindness' (2022), Founding Fuel quotes Sterman directly: "Much of the time, people attribute what happens to them to events close in time and space, when in reality it's the result of the dynamics of the larger system within which they are embedded." The article uses this to explain why systems thinkers must discipline themselves to trace causation across long chains rather than defaulting to the nearest visible event.

**The Implication**
When your organisation hits a recurring problem — one that keeps returning despite repeated fixes — stop diagnosing last quarter and instead map every decision made in the past three years that touched the system where the problem lives.

### Principle 3

> A complex system designed from scratch never works. You must begin with a simple system that already works, and let complexity emerge.

**What This Means & Why**
Gall's Law is one of the most violated principles in institutional design, particularly in government and large organisations. The impulse to design comprehensively — to account for every variable upfront, to build the full architecture before launching — feels like rigour. It is actually a way of importing the illusion of control into an environment that will not honour it. The working simple system is not a prototype to be discarded; it is the foundation from which complexity must be grown, iteratively, with feedback at every stage.

**The India Reality**
India's public institutions are replete with comprehensive frameworks designed to solve everything simultaneously — National Health Missions, Smart City projects, Skill India — each designed at scale from the centre, each underperforming relative to the small, locally adapted experiments that occasionally surface within the same system. The tension between the ambition to design for 1.4 billion people and the operational reality of implementation is a direct expression of Gall's Law in action.

**The Evidence**
In 'FF Daily #308: How systems fail' (2021), Founding Fuel presents Gall's Law in full — "A complex system that works is invariably found to have evolved from a simple system that worked. A complex system designed from scratch never works and cannot be patched up to make it work" — and immediately complicates it with the microcredit example: even simple financial products, when pushed without understanding user income variability, can fail systemically. The Aswan Dam case, where a dam designed to solve an irrigation problem ended up requiring more electricity than it generated to power the fertiliser plants made necessary by the irrigation changes it caused, stands as the canonical example of designed complexity unravelling.

**The Implication**
Before scaling any initiative, identify the smallest version of it that currently works in the field — not in a controlled pilot, but in messy reality — and build the scaling strategy from that foundation rather than from the original design.

### Principle 4

> Centralised command-and-control is not a response to complexity. It is a defence against having to understand it.

**What This Means & Why**
When a problem is large, the instinct to centralise is almost universal. It feels like coordination. It looks like leadership. But what it actually does is concentrate decision-making at the point furthest from where information about the problem is actually generated. In a complex system, the information that matters is local, contextual, and constantly changing. Centralised systems cannot process it fast enough, and so they substitute rules for judgment — and the rules, invariably, are written for the average case, which means they are wrong in almost every specific instance.

**The India Reality**
India's health system's central failure — identified by Nachiket Mor in his analysis for Founding Fuel — is not a shortage of money or intent. It is that detailed, line-item budgets are set centrally, leaving almost no room for local health administrators to respond to what they actually encounter. The result is a system that is technically funded but operationally paralysed: resources exist, but they cannot be directed where they are needed because the permission structure does not allow it.

**The Evidence**
In 'Local Systems Solutions: A way for India to provide universal healthcare' (2019), Nachiket Mor argues that India's DALY rate of roughly 35,000 places it 114th among 176 countries — just above Rwanda, which spends half as much — and 60 levels behind China. The root cause Mor identifies is not budget quantum but budget structure: centralised line-item allocations that eliminate local autonomy. Thailand's contrasting trajectory, built on both massive budget increases and a locally adaptive delivery model since the 1970s, demonstrates that the autonomy variable matters independently of the funding variable.

**The Implication**
Identify one decision in your organisation that is currently made centrally on the basis of rules but would be made better locally on the basis of context, and move the decision right to where the information lives.

### Principle 5

> The answer to a systems problem is never inside any single discipline. It lives at the intersection — which is precisely where no one is looking.

**What This Means & Why**
Modern institutions are built on specialisation. That specialisation has produced extraordinary advances. It has also produced a class of experts who understand their own domain deeply and the system within which their domain is embedded almost not at all. The result is that the most important problems — the ones that persist across decades despite sustained investment — are precisely the ones that no discipline owns, because they are generated by the interactions between domains, not within any one of them.

**The India Reality**
Kiran Karnik's observation from village health work captures this structurally: underweight children connected to public safety, gender issues, and caste discrimination — all because lower-caste communities were barred from drawing water from the village well. No nutrition specialist, no gender specialist, and no caste relations specialist would have arrived at that diagnosis working within their own framework. The diagnosis only emerged at the intersection.

**The Evidence**
In 'Lessons from a mutating world' (2017), Founding Fuel articulates the challenge directly: "Who is to know in what silo, in whose head, or the intersection of what discipline pointers to answers may lie in." The article uses the example of James Watson — the budding ornithologist who studied chemistry and then made landmark breakthroughs in genetics because he had read a paper on biology by a quantum physicist — to argue that the breakthroughs most needed are specifically the ones that require crossing disciplinary boundaries. The same point recurs in 'FF Daily #342: How all things are interconnected' (2021).

**The Implication**
When you convene the next working group on a persistent organisational problem, mandate that at least one participant has no professional background in the problem domain — and give them speaking time before the experts.

### Principle 6

> Scaling a solution before understanding the system it is entering is not ambition. It is the fastest route to a new, larger problem.

**What This Means & Why**
The startup culture has imported a dangerous idea into development and policy: that a successful pilot is proof of concept for scale. It is not. A pilot succeeds in a specific context, with specific people, under specific conditions. The system at scale has different feedback loops, different stakeholders, different constraints. When you push a solution into that system without understanding how the system will absorb, resist, or mutate it, you are not executing — you are experimenting without a hypothesis.

**The India Reality**
India's record as a "graveyard of successful pilots" — Karnik's phrase — is not random. It reflects a structural pattern: interventions designed and tested in controlled environments, then mandated at scale through the same centralised architecture that prevents local adaptation. The microcredit crisis is the sharpest version of this: a product that demonstrably helped some people in some contexts was pushed at scale into a population with unsteady income, and the system dynamics at scale were completely different from the dynamics in the pilot.

**The Evidence**
In 'Purpose-Driven Networks: Sustaining large-scale movements of change' (2019), Kiran Karnik names the graveyard explicitly: "India is a classic case of 'graveyard of successful pilots'. There are countless examples of pilot projects or micro-level efforts which work very well, but fail to scale." He contrasts this with business startups that scale — and argues that the reason societal problems fail to scale is precisely that they are treated as single-variable solutions to multi-variable, interconnected system problems.

**The Implication**
Before any pilot is declared ready for scale, document the three most important ways the context at scale differs from the context in the pilot, and design explicit responses to each before the rollout begins.

---

### Principle 7

> What looks like a governance problem is usually a systems design problem. Changing the people without changing the structure changes nothing.

**What This Means & Why**
The governance debate in India — and in most large organisations — defaults to personnel and intent. If we had better leaders, more honest officials, more accountable managers, the argument goes, things would work. But systems thinking reveals a different truth: structure determines behaviour. Put good people in a bad system and you get bad outcomes, reliably, because the incentive architecture, information flows, and feedback loops of the system shape what is possible regardless of individual quality. Changing people without changing structure is not reform — it is casting.

**The India Reality**
India's urban governance challenge illustrates this precisely. Swati Ramanathan's analysis of garbage in Indian cities identifies four interrelated systemic themes — urban planning and design, capacities and resources, political representation, and transparency and participation. None of these is a personnel problem. Each is a structural feature of how the system is designed to function. The people operating within it are responding rationally to the incentives the system creates.

**The Evidence**
In 'Local Systems Solutions: A framework for solving urban challenges' (2019), Swati Ramanathan argues from twenty years of practitioner experience in Indian urban transformation that garbage is "a classic 'systems' problem" — and that the failure to treat it as such leads to interventions that address symptoms while the structural generators of the problem continue operating unchanged. The Indian Constitution's specific amendments enshrining local governance empowerment exist — but structure has not followed constitutional intent because the system of political incentives and resource allocation has not been redesigned to match.

**The Implication**
The next time your organisation proposes a personnel change to fix a recurring problem, require that the proposal also names the structural feature that produced the problem in the first place and explains why the structural feature will not produce the same outcome with the new person.

### Principle 8

> A single story about a complex system is not a simplification. It is a distortion.

**What This Means & Why**
Every complex system has multiple valid descriptions, each of which captures something real and obscures something real. When decision-makers operate from a single description — a single model, a single metric, a single stakeholder's perspective — they are not simplifying for tractability. They are eliminating the information that would contradict their intervention. The danger is not that the single story is false. The danger is that it is partial, and in a complex system, partial is functionally false when used as the basis for design.

**The India Reality**
India's development narrative has periodically collapsed into single stories — GDP growth as the measure of everything, urban infrastructure as the face of modernity, digital inclusion as the solution to social exclusion. Each of these contains real insight. Each has also directed resources and attention away from the system dynamics that its own success was simultaneously generating: growth without redistribution, urbanisation without services, digitisation without data protection.

**The Evidence**
In 'FF Daily #276: The danger of single story' (2021), Founding Fuel draws on Chimamanda Adichie's concept and applies it directly to systems epistemology: multiple perspectives are not a courtesy to inclusion, they are a methodological requirement for understanding complex systems. The article draws on Timothy Wilson's perceptual research — the duck/rabbit and THE/MAT examples — to show that the same data, viewed from different contextual frames, produces genuinely different interpretations. The implication for systems design is that the frame you bring to the data is as consequential as the data itself.

**The Implication**
For your next major strategic decision, require that the briefing material include at least two contradictory interpretations of the same evidence — and resist resolving the contradiction before the decision is made.

---

### Principle 9

> The world is not evolving. It is mutating. Evolutionary models and mutational models require completely different responses.

**What This Means & Why**
Evolution is directional and cumulative — what came before informs what comes next, and adaptation is a process of refinement within a stable frame. Mutation is discontinuous — the rules themselves change, the categories that made sense become unreliable, and strategies optimised for the old system actively misalign with the new one. Practitioners who are running evolutionary playbooks in a mutating environment are not just behind — they are being actively misled by the quality of their past success.

**The India Reality**
India's technology sector, its media landscape, its retail economy, and its political communication infrastructure have all mutated — not evolved — across the last decade. The categories that structured strategy in 2010 (organised versus unorganised retail, cable versus digital media, party versus movement politics) are not just outdated; in several cases, the polarity has reversed. Organisations built for the old categories are not merely inefficient in the new environment — they are structurally misaligned with it.

**The Evidence**
In 'Lessons from a mutating world' (2017), Founding Fuel opens with a direct challenge to evolutionary framing: "Ours is no longer an evolving world, but a mutating one. What we are witnessing now is change of the kind unprecedented in human history." The article argues that mutation requires not better prediction within the existing model, but the abandonment of the model itself as the primary tool of navigation — and the development of the capacity to learn and synthesise across disciplines that the old model kept separate.

**The Implication**
Identify one strategic assumption your organisation has held for more than five years and test it against the current evidence — not to update it incrementally, but to ask whether the category it belongs to still exists in the same form.

### Principle 10

> The feedback loop is the unit of analysis. Not the event, not the decision, not the individual — the loop.

**What This Means & Why**
Events attract attention because they are visible and timestamped. Decisions attract analysis because they are attributable. But in complex systems, the event and the decision are both downstream of the feedback loops that generated the conditions in which they occurred. Changing the event without changing the loop produces another event of the same kind. Changing the decision without changing the loop produces another decision of the same kind. The loop is the structure — and structure is what persists.

**The India Reality**
India's media ecosystem demonstrates this with uncomfortable clarity. The reinforcing loop of outrage-engagement-advertising revenue has driven content choices at platform after platform, channel after channel — not because individual editors or algorithms are uniquely bad, but because the feedback loop rewards a specific kind of content regardless of who is operating within it. Personnel changes, editorial guidelines, and regulatory interventions that do not touch the loop leave the loop running.

**The Evidence**
In 'Is there a structural limit to growth?' (2017), Founding Fuel introduces Peter Senge's framework of reinforcing and balancing loops — explicitly naming them as the architecture of all systems. The article applies this to technology platforms and business models: a reinforcing loop of sales driving customer satisfaction driving more sales is the engine of growth, but every reinforcing loop has a balancing loop that eventually constrains it. Haresh Chawla's observation that platform optionality "resides in the habit they can create" is precisely a feedback loop argument — the value is in the loop the platform creates in user behaviour, not in the transactions it facilitates.

**The Implication**
Draw the feedback loop that generates the problem you are trying to solve — not the problem itself, but the loop — and identify which element of the loop, if changed, would change the dynamics rather than the outcome.

---

### Principle 11

> Purpose-driven networks outperform centralised programmes for systemic change — but only if the network is genuinely networked.

**What This Means & Why**
The instinct to build programmes — funded, staffed, governed, measured — is strong because programmes are legible. You can see where the money went, who was responsible, what the output was. But systemic change rarely moves through programmes. It moves through networks: webs of organisations, individuals, and institutions that share a purpose, adapt locally, and reinforce each other's work in ways that no central coordinator could design or direct. The catch is that most things called networks are actually hub-and-spoke structures in disguise — and hub-and-spoke structures reproduce the limitations of centralised programmes.

**The India Reality**
India's development sector has generated several genuine network-based change movements — the Self-Employed Women's Association, the Chipko movement, parts of the Right to Information movement — alongside many more that called themselves networks while operating as programmes. The distinction matters enormously for sustainability: genuine networks survive the departure of their founders; programme-dependent movements typically do not.

**The Evidence**
In 'Purpose-Driven Networks: Sustaining large-scale movements of change' (2019), Kiran Karnik argues that the reason India's successful pilots don't scale is not a funding or policy problem but a structural one: the interventions are designed as programmes rather than as networks. He identifies the characteristics of networks that actually sustain large-scale movements — shared purpose, local adaptation, mutual reinforcement — and distinguishes them from networks that are networks in name only.

**The Implication**
If you are building or funding a change initiative, map the actual information and decision flows to determine whether it is genuinely networked or whether one node controls everything — and redesign the governance structure accordingly before you invest in growth.

---

### Principle 12

> Digital platforms are not distribution channels. They are systems that create new feedback loops — and the loops they create are often more consequential than the products they carry.

**What This Means & Why**
Most organisations adopt digital platforms instrumentally — as a way to reach more people, move more goods, process more transactions. This is a category error. Digital platforms are systems-level infrastructure: they alter the feedback loops through which information, behaviour, and value flow across a market or society. The consequences of deploying them at scale are not extensions of the consequences of deploying them at small scale — they are qualitatively different, because the feedback loops they generate at scale operate in ways that small-scale deployment does not reveal.

**The India Reality**
India Stack — Aadhaar, UPI, and their extensions — is the largest systems-level digital infrastructure experiment in the world. Its consequences for financial inclusion, data governance, commercial competition, and state capacity are not additive versions of what each component did in pilot. They are emergent properties of a system interacting with 1.4 billion people across a social and economic landscape of extraordinary diversity. The debate about data handling that Founding Fuel flagged in 2017 has become, by 2024, one of the most consequential policy questions in Indian digital governance.

**The Evidence**
In 'Models, tech platforms and the hope for a better future' (2017), Founding Fuel describes Nandan Nilekani's translation of digital platform thinking from the tech world into politics and bureaucracy as a live systems experiment — and frames Aadhaar and Swachh Bharat as requiring not just technical implementation but systems understanding of how the feedback loops they generate will interact with existing social and institutional structures. The article explicitly warns that no single model can capture the dynamic complexity these platforms introduce.

**The Implication**
Before your organisation adopts or builds a digital platform at scale, map the three feedback loops it will create in user behaviour and market structure — not just the transactions it will enable — and design governance for the loops, not just for the transactions.

## FF's View

---

**The Core Conviction**

FF's central argument on systems thinking is not that India needs more of it. It is that India has been systematically miseducated against it — and is now paying the price at scale.

The miseducation runs deep. It was built into how colonial administration worked: a problem appears, a department is created, a budget is allocated, a target is set, a report is filed. The apparatus looks like action. The problem persists. Another report is filed. What this apparatus was never designed to do was ask why the problem recurs — what structure, what loop, what interdependency is generating it. That is a systems question, and for a century and a half, the structure of Indian governance actively suppressed it.

After independence, the template remained. India became what Arun Maira, across a remarkable body of work covered in FF's 2019 series on Local Systems Solutions, called a culture of command-and-control. When confronted with large-scale problems — poverty, sanitation, health, urban chaos — the instinct is to build a centralised programme. Define the problem at the top, design a solution at the top, implement it uniformly downward. As Maira argues in the piece documented across FF Daily #380 (2021), this approach does not work because the problems it targets are systemic. They are generated by local interactions, feedback loops, cultural norms, and institutional incentives that a centralised programme cannot see, let alone address.

But FF's conviction is not merely critical. It is constructive. The evidence FF has accumulated across eight years of reporting points to a specific, usable truth: systems thinking is not a methodology reserved for academics or management theorists. It is a practitioner's discipline, and India has generated some of its most important practitioners — in healthcare reform, in urban governance, in digital infrastructure, in aviation safety. What they share is a refusal to accept that a problem's visible symptoms are the problem's actual cause, and a willingness to trace consequences through time and across domains until the structure generating the problem becomes visible.

This is what FF believes. Not that India needs to import systems thinking from MIT Sloan or the Santa Fe Institute. But that the most important work of Indian practice — the work that actually sticks — has always been systems work, whether or not it was named that way.

---

**What India Means for This Theme**

India is not simply a context in which systems thinking is applied. India is, in several respects, the most demanding test environment for systems thinking that exists anywhere in the world. The density of interdependencies, the diversity of local conditions, the gap between formal institutions and lived reality, the scale at which everything operates — all of this makes the consequences of thinking mechanically more severe here than almost anywhere else.

Begin with scale. India's problems are not merely large — they are large and heterogeneous simultaneously. The central tension that Nachiket Mor identifies in his 2019 piece for FF, 'Local Systems Solutions: A way for India to provide universal healthcare,' is precisely this: India's health system is organised around detailed line-item budgets that leave almost no room for local autonomy. The result is a system designed for uniformity operating in a country where conditions vary so dramatically that uniformity is a structural guarantee of failure. India's DALY rate ranks 114th among 176 countries, barely ahead of Rwanda, which spends half as much on health. China, with comparable resources and colonial legacy, ranks 60 positions higher. The difference is not money. The difference is systems design — specifically, whether local actors have the authority and incentive to adapt.

The scaling failure problem is a uniquely Indian wound. As Kiran Karnik writes in 'Purpose-Driven Networks' (2019), India is a classic graveyard of successful pilots. Interventions that work beautifully at the micro level — in a village, in a ward, in a district — collapse when they encounter the machinery of national scaling. This is not bad luck. It is a systems property: the conditions that made the pilot work (local trust, specific relationships, adaptive leadership) are precisely the conditions that uniform national programmes destroy when they absorb the pilot. Karnik traces this through the history of rural development, health, and education programmes, and the pattern is consistent. India scales reach, not outcomes.

The Bharat dimension makes this worse and more urgent. Urban, English-speaking India — the India that reads FF, that populates boardrooms and policy offices — is embedded in a set of systems that are legible to Western frameworks. There are markets, there are metrics, there are institutions that roughly function. But most of India operates in a different regime: a regime where the formal and informal are deeply entangled, where the state is simultaneously present and absent, where social systems — caste, custom, kinship — are load-bearing infrastructure. Kiran Karnik's observation in 'Purpose-Driven Networks' that dealing with health problems in villages revealed the links between underweight children, public safety, gender issues, and caste discrimination — all because lower-caste communities were barred from safer water sources — is not a sociological observation. It is a systems observation. The interventions that fail in rural India consistently fail for the same reason: they address one variable in a system held together by many others they refuse to acknowledge.

The Nagaland case documented in FF Insights #703: System Blindness (2022) is particularly telling. Not a single woman has been elected to the Nagaland state assembly since 1963. The piece traces this not to a single cause but to a layered system of interaction between customary law, tribal bodies, and state electoral structures. The prescription — enact laws — is the mechanistic response. The systems response recognises that you cannot change one element of a self-reinforcing structure without understanding what all the other elements are doing to preserve it.

India's relationship with digital infrastructure adds another layer. Aadhaar and India Stack are not just technology programmes. They are structural experiments in systems design — attempts to create feedback loops between identity, transaction data, and public service delivery at a scale and in a context that has never been attempted. Shankar Maruwada's personal evolution, traced in Charles Assisi's 2017 piece 'How may Einstein have thought of God and Aadhaar?', is instructive: Maruwada came to EkStep and Aadhaar carrying an engineer's framework — mechanical, modular, solvable in parts. What the complexity of implementation taught him was the necessity of thinking dynamically and across disciplines. The platform forced the systems thinking.

And then there is the structural political economy. FF Daily #276 (2021) opens with Chimamanda Adichie's warning about the single story — the danger of reducing a complex reality to one narrative — and uses it to frame the epistemological challenge of Indian policymaking. Indian policy has an endemic tendency toward single-story thinking: the problem is sanitation, so build toilets; the problem is hunger, so distribute food; the problem is illiteracy, so build schools. The metrics are single-variable. The feedback loops are invisible. The second-order consequences — the ones that undo the first-order gains — arrive unannounced and are attributed to corruption, or culture, or bad luck, rather than to the systems blindness that generated them.

---

**Where Global Best Practices Break Down**

The global best practice literature on systems thinking — Senge's The Fifth Discipline, Meadows' Thinking in Systems, the whole apparatus of system dynamics developed at MIT — is genuinely useful. FF draws on it extensively. But it breaks down in India in at least three specific and consequential ways.

First, the feedback loop models assume that feedback is legible. In Senge's framework, documented in FF's 2017 piece 'Is there a structural limit to growth?', the reinforcing and balancing loops of a system become visible through data, through measurement, through the ability to trace effects back to causes. This assumption is fragile everywhere; in India, it is heroic. Data scarcity in rural systems, the opacity of informal institutions, the difficulty of attributing outcomes to causes in contexts where multiple interventions are running simultaneously — all of this means that the standard toolkit of systems dynamics runs into an evidence wall. The loops exist. You just can't see them. The practitioner's discipline of holding a systems frame without being able to complete the model is a specifically Indian skill that the global literature doesn't teach.

Second, the best practice literature on systems change tends to assume that the primary actors are either the state or civil society, with the private sector as a secondary figure. The Seelos, Farley, and Rose framework from Stanford Social Innovation Review, cited in FF Daily #276 (2021), is built around this architecture. In India, the private sector — particularly the informal private sector — is load-bearing infrastructure in health, education, transport, and credit in ways that neither the state nor civil society adequately replaces. The local chemist who functions as a de facto primary care provider, the private school in a rural town that is the only functioning educational institution within 20 kilometres, the moneylender whose terms are extortionate but whose response time is measured in hours rather than months — these actors are inside the system in ways the best practice frameworks don't model. Designing systems change without them, or against them, produces the graveyard of pilots that Karnik documents.

Third, and most fundamentally, the global literature bakes in an assumption of institutional good faith. Senge's learning organisation, Meadows' leverage points, the whole apparatus of deliberate systems intervention — all of it assumes that once actors understand the system, they will want to change it toward better outcomes. The political economy of Indian systems frequently contradicts this. As Swati Ramanathan argues in her 2019 piece 'Local Systems Solutions: A framework for solving urban challenges', the urban garbage problem in Indian cities is systemic precisely because it sits at the intersection of urban planning, political representation, resource allocation, and transparency — and some of the actors with the most leverage over these variables benefit from the current dysfunction. The system isn't broken; it is working exactly as the incentives embedded in it predict. Understanding this requires a different kind of systems thinking — not optimistic learning loops, but political economy analysis that the MIT Sloan framework was not designed to provide.

Where the global skeptics of the dominant narrative are right — and FF's evidence supports this — is precisely here: John Gall's warning, documented in FF Daily #308 (2021), that complex systems designed from scratch never work, and that the only path is to start with a working simple system and let it evolve, is validated again and again in Indian experience. The sophisticated, comprehensive, total-solution programmes fail. The modest, locally grounded, iteratively expanding interventions sometimes succeed. This is not a failure of ambition. It is a feature of how complex systems actually work.

**How FF Diverges from Mainstream Media**

Mainstream Indian business media covers systems in the way it covers most things: through the lens of the individual decision-maker. A policy succeeds or fails because a minister did or didn't act decisively. A company succeeds or fails because a founder had vision or lacked it. An institution works or doesn't work because of the people at the top. This is not wrong as far as it goes, but it goes nowhere near far enough.

FF's divergence is structural. From the earliest pieces — the 2017 Founding Fuel editorial 'Lessons from a mutating world' — FF has insisted that the correct unit of analysis is the system, not the individual actor within it. This is not a mild methodological preference. It has significant implications for how you assign causality, how you design interventions, and what you expect to be able to change.

The difference shows up most clearly in how coverage handles failure. When a public health initiative fails, mainstream media asks who was responsible. FF asks what loop was operating. The Aswan Dam example, used in FF Daily #308 (2021) to illustrate Gall's Law in action, is not primarily about the Egyptian government's decision-making — it is about what happens when you change one element of a complex system (generate electricity, enable irrigation) without modelling the downstream effects (eliminate Nile flooding, destroy natural fertilisation, require artificial fertilisers, require more electricity, require running the dam at full capacity simply to supply the electricity the dam's existence made necessary). The story is about system blindness, not about bad leaders.

The mainstream Indian business press has, over the past decade, become increasingly comfortable with startup and venture capital frameworks — the pivot, the product-market fit, the unit economics — as universal explanatory tools. These are, again, not wrong, but they are narrow. They model very specific kinds of systems (markets with rapid feedback, measurable metrics, motivated actors) and generalise poorly to the systems where India's deepest problems live. FF has consistently maintained that these frameworks are tools, not worldviews, and that applying them to health systems, urban governance, or rural poverty without modification is itself a form of system blindness.

Where consensus has eventually caught up with FF — and this is worth naming — is on the question of platforms and feedback loops. The 2017 FF piece 'Models, tech platforms and the hope for a better future' was describing the systems logic of digital platforms at a moment when mainstream coverage was still treating Aadhaar and India Stack as primarily technological or political stories. The systemic framing — what feedback loops does this create, who controls them, what are the second-order consequences — is now more widely applied, partly because the consequences (data privacy, digital exclusion, market concentration) have become impossible to ignore.

**Against Management Orthodoxy**

The received wisdom of business schools and consulting firms on organisational complexity runs something like this: define the problem clearly, decompose it into components, assign each component to a specialist, coordinate the specialists, measure outputs, iterate. This is the McKinsey framework, the BCG framework, the standard MBA toolkit applied to the real world. It works well for a certain class of problem — problems that are genuinely decomposable, where the components are loosely coupled, where measurement is reliable, and where feedback is fast. For that class of problem, it is excellent.

FF's evidence base shows that India's most important problems — health, urban infrastructure, education, poverty, governance — are not in that class. They are what Swati Ramanathan in her 2019 piece calls 'systemic problems with multiple interconnected causes,' and what Fritjof Capra, cited in FF's July 2020 WFH Daily, describes as 'different facets of one single crisis, which is largely a crisis of perception.' The management orthodoxy misclassifies them. It treats them as complicated problems when they are complex ones. Complicated problems have solutions that can be engineered. Complex problems have dynamics that have to be navigated.

The specific management orthodoxy FF most consistently challenges is the expertise model. As FF Daily #380 (2021) bluntly states, an abundance of experts has become part of the problem rather than the solution, because experts are too narrowly focused on separate pieces of the system. The healthcare system expert who doesn't understand political economy. The urban planning expert who doesn't understand community governance. The technology expert who doesn't understand the social systems their platform is entering. Each expert optimises their piece. The interactions between the pieces generate outcomes no expert anticipated and no expert owns. This is not a failure of intelligence or competence. It is a structural consequence of organising knowledge in silos and then applying it to systems that don't respect those boundaries.

The orthodoxy also gets incentives wrong. Standard management frameworks assume that incentive design is the primary lever of behaviour change: get the incentives right and the system will behave correctly. FF's evidence — particularly from the health and urban governance reporting — shows that this assumption fails in systems where social norms, power structures, and informal institutions are co-equal determinants of behaviour. Changing a financial incentive without changing the social system in which it operates often produces gaming rather than the intended behaviour. The detailed line-item budget structure in India's health system that Nachiket Mor identifies in his 2019 piece is ostensibly an incentive design — money flows to specific activities — but the actual effect is to eliminate local autonomy and enterprise, because the incentive structure is optimising for accountability to the centre rather than outcomes for the patient.

What the evidence actually shows, against the management orthodoxy, is that the leverage in complex systems is almost never where it looks like it should be. John Sterman's observation, cited in FF Insights #703 (2022), that people systematically attribute their situation to events close in time and space when the actual cause is the dynamics of the larger system, is a direct challenge to the orthodoxy of proximate cause analysis. Proximate cause analysis is how boards examine failures, how consultants diagnose organisations, how governments design accountability frameworks. It produces action on the wrong variables, leaves the generative structure untouched, and ensures that the problem returns.

**The Entrepreneurial Mindset Beyond Startups**

The entrepreneurial relationship to systems thinking is specific and underexamined. It is not the same as the startup relationship to scale. Startups scale by finding a repeatable process and running it faster. That is not systems entrepreneurship. Systems entrepreneurship is something closer to what Shankar Maruwada describes — in Charles Assisi's 2017 profile — as the discipline of operating in a dynamic, cross-disciplinary space where the rules are not fixed, the feedback is often ambiguous, and the relevant variables keep changing.

This kind of thinking is available to anyone. It is available to the district health officer who decides to treat her district not as a collection of facilities to be inspected but as a set of interdependencies to be understood. It is available to the corporate strategist who asks not 'what is our product's market share' but 'what feedback loops are we participating in and which ones are we inadvertently strengthening against ourselves.' It is available to the urban planner who reads Swati Ramanathan's four-part framework — urban planning and design, capacities and resources, political representation, transparency and participation — and recognises that working on any one of these without the others is an intervention that will produce unintended consequences.

Haresh Chawla's analysis, cited in FF's 2017 piece 'Is there a structural limit to growth?', on the systems logic of platform businesses is relevant here beyond the startup context. His observation that the optionality in a platform resides in the habit it creates and whether teams have the vision to expand into adjacencies is a systems insight applied to strategy. The habit is a feedback loop. The adjacency is a leverage point. The question is whether you can see the structure you are embedded in clearly enough to act on it before competitors or circumstances force the issue.

What distinguishes the entrepreneurially minded practitioner — in a government ministry, in a large corporation, in a civil society organisation — is precisely this capacity for structural vision. Not the ability to move fast. Not the tolerance for risk. The capacity to hold in mind simultaneously the current state of a system, the feedback loops generating that state, the leverage points where intervention could shift the dynamics, and the second-order consequences that intervention might produce. This is what Arun Maira is describing, across his entire body of work covered in FF's 2019 series, when he argues for locally adaptive systems over centrally designed programmes. The local actor who can see the system they are embedded in and adapt their action accordingly is exhibiting entrepreneurial systems thinking. The central planner who cannot see local variation is not.

The aviation crash investigation methodology documented in Anmol Shrivastava's 2025 FF piece 'Part 3: Unraveling AI 171' is worth drawing into this frame. The investigator's discipline — building complete chains of cause and effect, testing each element against the evidence, refusing to accept isolated explanations — is precisely the discipline that entrepreneurial systems thinking requires in any domain. You do not accept the first explanation that fits. You build the chain until you find the structure. That is not a methodology specific to aviation safety. It is the practitioner's form of systems thinking.

**On Transformation: Process, Not Just Destination**

The dominant image of transformation in Indian business and policy discourse is punctuated: a moment of change, a turning point, a leader who decides, a technology that disrupts. FF's reporting on systems thinking consistently reveals that this image is wrong — not just incomplete but actively misleading. Transformation in complex systems is not punctuated. It is structural, cumulative, and deeply dependent on what happens at the margins and over time rather than at the centre and in the moment.

Gall's Law, which FF returns to repeatedly across multiple years of reporting, is the foundational principle here. As documented in FF Daily #308 (2021), complex systems that work have invariably evolved from simple systems that worked. This is not an observation about caution or incrementalism. It is an observation about how complexity actually comes into being. The implication for transformation is that you cannot design the end-state and build toward it. You have to find the working simple system, protect it, let it evolve, and allow the complexity to emerge from interaction rather than imposing it from above.

This principle has a difficult corollary that FF's reporting does not shy away from. The 2021 FF Daily piece on systems failure immediately complicates the Gall's Law triumph by noting the microcredit crisis: a simple, working product — small loans for poor borrowers — that failed systemically when it encountered users with irregular income flows. The simplicity of the product was real. The simplicity of the system it was entering was illusory. Transformation requires holding both truths simultaneously: start simple, but remain acutely alert to the complexity of the system the simple thing is entering.

The Local Systems Solutions framework — across Ramanathan's urban piece, Mor's health piece, and Karnik's networks piece from FF's 2019 series — describes transformation as a process that requires at minimum four simultaneous movements: changing the structural conditions (the design and rules), building the capacities of local actors, ensuring political representation of affected communities, and creating mechanisms for transparency and participation. You cannot sequence these. Doing the structural reform without the capacity building produces change that cannot be implemented. Doing the capacity building without the structural reform produces skilled actors constrained by a system that won't yield. This simultaneity is what makes transformation hard, and it is what the single-variable approaches — new technology, new leadership, new funding — consistently underestimate.

The Aadhaar experience, traced through Shankar Maruwada's evolution in Assisi's 2017 piece, illustrates the process dimension further. Maruwada's shift from engineering to systems thinking was not a moment of revelation. It was a gradual accumulation of evidence that the problem he was working on was not decomposable, that the feedback between technology design and social adoption was non-linear, and that the disciplines he needed to draw on were not the disciplines his training had given him. This is how transformation happens in practice: not as a design decision at the top, but as a series of encounters with system behaviour that forces a reframing of the problem.

The Purpose-Driven Networks framework that Kiran Karnik describes in his 2019 FF piece is the process architecture for this kind of transformation at scale: networks rather than hierarchies, purpose rather than compliance, adaptive management rather than fixed plans, and the explicit recognition that the graveyard of pilots exists because pilots are extracted from the systems that made them work. Transformation sustains when the network that generated it is also the network that runs it.

**The Human and Cultural Dimension**

Management frameworks, including most systems thinking frameworks, treat culture as a contextual variable. It is something you account for, perhaps, in the implementation phase — after the architecture has been designed, after the strategy has been set. FF's evidence base consistently treats this as an inversion of the actual relationship. Culture is not context. Culture is structure. It is one of the primary feedback-generating mechanisms in social systems, and it cannot be bolted on after the design phase because it was operating before you entered the room.

The Nagaland example, documented in FF Insights #703 (2022), is among the starkest illustrations in FF's archive. The structural absence of women from the Nagaland legislative assembly is not a cultural inconvenience attached to an otherwise functioning electoral system. The customary law, the tribal bodies, and the electoral structure are a unified system, and the cultural elements — the legitimacy of tribal authority, the definition of customary governance, the social consequences for women who challenge these norms — are load-bearing. Enacting laws, as FF's piece notes, won't move the needle much. Not because culture is immovable, but because changing a formal rule without understanding its relationship to the cultural structure that gives it legitimacy (or strips it of legitimacy) is interventional noise.

Fritjof Capra's framing, cited in FF's July 2020 WFH Daily, is foundational here: the major problems of our time are ultimately different facets of one single crisis, which is largely a crisis of perception. This is a human and cultural claim as much as it is a systems claim. The perception that problems are separate, that causes are proximate, that specialists solve complex challenges — this perception is not merely an intellectual error. It is culturally reproduced, institutionally reinforced, and genuinely difficult to dislodge because the people operating within it have careers, incentives, and identities built around it.

The Timothy Wilson perceptual framing, cited in FF Daily #276 (2021) — the duck/rabbit illusion, the THE/MAT example — makes the same point in miniature. Context shapes interpretation. Two people looking at identical data about a system failure will see different things depending on the frame they carry into the observation. This is not a flaw to be corrected by better data. It is a feature of human perception that any systems practitioner must work with. The implication, drawn from Chimamanda Adichie's single-story warning in the same piece, is that exposure to multiple perspectives is not merely politically virtuous. It is epistemically necessary for systems understanding. You cannot see a system from one position within it.

The human dimension that FF's reporting most consistently foregrounds, however, is the dimension of trust. Arun Maira's framework — particularly as it applies to urban governance and healthcare — is not primarily about technical architecture. It is about whether local communities have enough trust in the system to participate in it, provide it feedback, adapt it to their needs, and hold it accountable. Trust is a feedback loop. Where it exists, it enables the adaptive local behaviour that makes systemic solutions work. Where it has been eroded — by programmes that extracted resources and delivered nothing, by pilots that were cancelled when funding cycles ended, by reforms that required community participation and delivered community betrayal — it takes a long time to rebuild, and the technical solution cannot substitute for it.

Kiran Karnik's village health example in 'Purpose-Driven Networks' (2019) names this most clearly. The links between underweight children, public safety, gender issues, and caste discrimination were not visible to the programme designers who came from outside. They were visible to the community members who lived with them. The system's most important data was held by people with the least formal authority in it. This is not an accident of this particular case. It is a structural feature of complex social systems, and it means that any approach to systems change that doesn't build mechanisms for the marginalised to surface information will consistently misunderstand what is generating the problems it is trying to solve.

**The Systems View**

The most important insight in FF's systems thinking coverage — the one that runs beneath all the individual principles and cases — is about interdependency, not complexity. Complexity is the noun people use when they want to signal that something is difficult. Interdependency is the analytical term that names the mechanism. Everything is connected, not metaphorically but mechanically: through feedback loops, through resource flows, through information pathways, through cultural and institutional norms that shape behaviour across domains simultaneously.

The Aswan Dam story, deployed in FF Daily #308 (2021) as an illustration of Gall's Law, is actually a story about interdependency. The Egyptian agricultural system was interdependent with the Nile flood cycle. The dam disrupted the flood cycle. The agricultural system required artificial fertilisation. The fertiliser plants required electricity. The electricity requirement meant running the dam at full capacity. The dam became necessary to supply the need its own existence created. This is a closed loop. The planners who designed the dam were thinking about energy and irrigation. They were not thinking about the soil ecology, the fertilisation cycle, or the economic chain from agricultural productivity to energy demand. They paid for that failure of systemic vision at scale.

FF's coverage consistently uses this type of illustration to make the same fundamental point: the second-order effects of systemic intervention are not supplementary. They are often the primary story. The rehabilitation centre that solves a problem for one group of residents by creating a larger problem for the broader community — documented in FF Daily #538 (2021) from Rosalind Armson's case study — is not an unusual outcome. It is the predictable outcome of optimising locally without modelling systemically.

The systems view also requires engaging with scale in a specific way. What Kiran Karnik identifies as the graveyard of successful pilots in 'Purpose-Driven Networks' (2019) is a scaling failure with a systemic explanation: the conditions that made the pilot work — the specific feedback loops, the trust relationships, the local knowledge — are not properties of the intervention. They are properties of the system in which the intervention was embedded. When scaling absorbs the intervention into a different system, it destroys the very properties that generated success. This is why, as Karnik argues, the only interventions that scale successfully are those which scale the network, not just the programme — which extend the system rather than replacing it with a larger, uniform structure.

The policy dimension of this view is politically uncomfortable. Nachiket Mor's analysis in his 2019 FF piece on universal healthcare in India names the structural limit directly: no state government since 1947 has shown any real inclination to increase health budgets toward what is needed for a functional system. This is not an oversight. It is a systemic choice, made and remade across decades, by actors who face a political economy in which health outcomes are not efficiently convertible into electoral outcomes. The system is working exactly as its incentive structure predicts. Understanding this does not make reform easier. But it makes reform legible in a way that moral exhortation never can. You cannot change a system by demanding that its actors behave inconsistently with the incentives they face. You have to change the incentives.

Nandan Nilekani's work, cited in the 2017 FF piece 'Models, tech platforms and the hope for a better future', represents perhaps the most ambitious attempt in Indian practice to think through this systems logic at national scale: using digital infrastructure to create new feedback loops between citizens and the state, between economic activity and governance, between individual identity and public service. Whether India Stack succeeds in this will depend not on the technology — the technology works — but on the governance structure that controls it and the cultural and political conditions that determine whether the feedback loops it creates are used to empower or to control. That is a systems question about trust, power, and institutional design, and it remains open.

**The Evolution of FF's Thinking**

The earliest FF pieces on systems thinking, from 2017, are primarily intellectual introductions. 'Lessons from a mutating world' (May 2017) sets the framing: the world has mutated, old frameworks no longer apply, the answers lie at intersections. 'Is there a structural limit to growth?' (July 2017) introduces Senge's framework as a usable analytical tool for practitioners. These pieces are doing the work of installing vocabulary — feedback loops, reinforcing and balancing dynamics, structural limits — in an audience that may not have encountered it.

By 2019, FF's thinking has moved from introduction to application. The Local Systems Solutions series — Ramanathan on urban governance, Mor on healthcare, Karnik on purpose-driven networks — is FF at its most specific and most India-anchored. These are not theoretical pieces. They are practitioner frameworks built from decades of work in Indian conditions. The shift in register is significant: FF is no longer translating global systems thinking for an Indian audience. It is generating systems thinking from Indian practice.

The 2020-2021 period — the WFH Daily and FF Daily pieces, written in the compressed format of the pandemic — shows a different kind of evolution. The daily format forced FF to make the connections faster, to use shorter examples, to apply systems thinking to the immediate crisis (the pandemic, the lockdowns, the economic disruption) in real time. This produced some of the sharpest writing in FF's archive on the theme: FF Daily #308 on Gall's Law, FF Daily #380 on the limits of command-and-control, FF Daily #413 on the nature of complex systems. The urgency of the moment stripped away the hedging and produced positions.

By 2022, with FF Insights #703 on System Blindness, the thinking has sharpened further to focus on the cognitive dimension — why humans systematically fail to see the systems they are embedded in. The Sterman quote at the centre of that piece — 'It's insidious. You get short-term relief, and then the problem comes back, often worse than before' — is not just a warning about systems dynamics. It is a description of the standard operating procedure of Indian institutional management: address the symptom, claim success, wait for the deeper problem to resurface, address the symptom again.

What has changed most significantly from the 2017 pieces to the 2025 aviation crash investigation piece is the level of confidence with which FF applies systems thinking to domains beyond policy and governance. The Anmol Shrivastava piece on AI 171 in 2025 is using systems thinking — specifically the chain-of-causation methodology — in a technical investigative context. This represents a maturation: systems thinking is no longer being presented as a framework to consider. It is being used, implicitly, as the only methodology adequate to the problem.

What FF believed in 2017 that it has substantially complicated by 2025 is the possibility of a clear, actionable model of any given system. The earliest pieces are optimistic about the map — if you understand the feedback loops, you can identify the leverage points, you can design the intervention. The later pieces are considerably more humble about this. Gall's Law complicates it. The political economy of health systems complicates it. The cognitive biases that prevent actors from seeing their own system complicate it further. FF has moved from 'here is the framework' to 'here is why the framework is necessary but not sufficient' — which is a more mature, more Indian, and more honest position.

**Anmol Shrivastava** represents the most recent and in some ways most striking contribution: using investigative journalism to demonstrate systems thinking methodology in action. His 2025 piece on the AI 171 crash investigation does not announce that it is using systems thinking. It uses it — the chain-of-causation analysis, the refusal to accept isolated explanations, the methodical building of evidence from multiple domains toward a structural conclusion. **What FF Is Skeptical Of**

FF is skeptical, consistently and specifically, of the claim that expertise solves complex problems. Not expertise generally — expertise in decomposable, well-defined problems is genuinely useful. But the institutional assumption that assembling a group of domain specialists constitutes adequate response to a systemic challenge is one that FF's evidence base consistently challenges. FF Daily #380 (2021) names it directly: an abundance of experts has become part of the problem. The more specialised the expertise, the narrower its view of the system, and the more likely its interventions are to optimise one subsystem at the expense of the others.

FF is skeptical of the scaling imperative as applied to social interventions. The business media celebration of scale — the unicorn, the national programme, the government scheme with crore-level reach — is a category error when applied to systems change. Karnik's graveyard of pilots observation, from his 2019 piece, is not just a critique of poor implementation. It is a structural argument that the features which make an intervention work are systematically destroyed by the processes that scale it. FF does not celebrate reach. It asks whether the thing being scaled is still doing what the original thing was doing.

FF is skeptical of the single-cause diagnosis. Every FF piece on systems thinking, from the earliest to the most recent, pushes against the tendency to identify one variable as the explanation for a complex outcome. The microcredit crisis, the Aswan Dam paradox, the Nagaland gender exclusion, the Indian health system's underperformance — none of these have single causes. They have structural causes: feedback loops, incentive architectures, cultural norms, and institutional designs that interact to produce outcomes no single actor chose and no single intervention can fix. The press release that announces a new programme to solve one of these problems with one mechanism is, from FF's perspective, evidence of the problem rather than a response to it.

FF is skeptical of imported management frameworks applied wholesale to Indian conditions. This is not nativism. It is empiricism. The evidence, accumulated across FF's eight years of reporting on this theme, shows that the assumptions embedded in management orthodoxy — about data availability, institutional good faith, cultural neutrality, the primacy of financial incentives — are regularly violated in Indian contexts. The frameworks need to be tested against Indian evidence and modified where the evidence demands it. FF has consistently applied this standard, which is why its coverage of systems thinking reads differently from a business school case study.

FF celebrates practitioners who have learned systems thinking from experience rather than from textbooks, and who have the intellectual honesty to name what they don't understand. Shankar Maruwada's evolution — from engineering certainty to cross-disciplinary curiosity — is celebrated in Assisi's 2017 profile not because it produced a finished system but because it represents the kind of thinking that complex problems actually require. The humility is not weakness. It is the epistemically correct response to genuine complexity.

FF celebrates the Local Systems Solutions framework as a body of work. The 2019 series — Ramanathan, Mor, Karnik — represents the kind of practitioner-generated knowledge that FF most consistently values: rooted in Indian conditions, built from failure as much as from success, structured enough to be actionable but honest about its limits. That this framework emerged from decades of work in Indian urban governance and health is significant. It was not derived from Western theory and applied downward. It was built upward from Indian evidence.

FF celebrates the use of concrete, memorable examples as vehicles for systems insight. The Aswan Dam, the seaside rehabilitation centre, the village well and the underweight children, the Nagaland assembly — these examples appear across FF's archive not as anecdotes but as working models. They carry the principles across to readers who haven't encountered the formal vocabulary. This is a specific editorial choice, and it reflects FF's conviction that systems thinking is not an academic discipline. It is a practitioner discipline, and its transmission requires illustration, not just theorisation.

FF celebrates what it calls — following Kiran Karnik's framing — the Internet of Things for villages: the recognition that in rural India, the interdependencies between health, water, gender, caste, and economic security are not separate problem sets but a single system that can only be changed as a system. This celebration is not romantic. It is analytical. The interventions that work in rural India are the ones that see this system. The ones that fail are the ones that isolate one variable.

**The Historical Lens**

FF's use of history in its systems thinking coverage is not decorative. It is structural. The historical examples — the Aswan Dam, the English sparrow introduction to America, the insecticide-resistant insects — are used by FF not to illustrate that complex systems can surprise us, but to demonstrate that this is not a new discovery. Human beings have been encountering the second-order consequences of systems interventions for as long as they have been intervening in systems. The novelty is not the phenomenon. The novelty is the vocabulary for describing it — and the tools for anticipating it.

The Indian historical frame in FF's coverage centres on a specific set of failures: the post-independence development model, the command-and-control governance structure, the centralisation of decision-making that the constitutional amendments on local governance were designed to correct but largely didn't. Ramanathan's 2019 piece explicitly names the constitutional provisions for local governance as evidence that the systemic problem — over-centralisation — has been politically acknowledged for decades. The gap between the acknowledgement and the practice is not ignorance. It is a political economy: the actors with the power to decentralise are the actors who lose power by decentralising. History explains why the reform that is obviously necessary keeps not happening.

The Thailand-India comparison in Nachiket Mor's 2019 piece is historical in a specific way. Thailand and India had comparable health infrastructure in the 1970s. Thailand invested, sustained the investment, and built local systems that work. India did not. The outcome — India's DALY rate ranking behind countries that spend far less — is fifty years of compounding difference. This is a historical argument for the cumulative power of systemic choice. The gap is not about the present. It is about a series of choices, made and remade over decades, that have compounded into a structural difference in outcomes.

The aviation safety lens introduced in Shrivastava's 2025 piece also has a historical dimension. Aviation crash investigation methodology developed specifically because mechanical attribution — this component failed, so that caused the crash — kept producing interventions that didn't prevent the next crash. The shift to chain-of-causation analysis, the development of fault tree analysis and systems safety methodology, was a historical learning process over decades. It worked. The reduction in aviation fatalities per passenger kilometre over the past fifty years is among the most significant safety achievements in any complex system. FF's use of this historical precedent is not accidental. It is an argument by example: when a field commits to systems thinking as methodology, it gets better outcomes.

**Unresolved Tensions**

The deepest unresolved tension in FF's systems thinking coverage is between the argument for local adaptation and the argument for structural reform. These are not the same thing, and they sometimes pull in opposite directions. Arun Maira's case for local systems — give communities the authority and resources to solve their own problems, build adaptive networks rather than uniform programmes — is compelling as a corrective to top-down command-and-control. But it carries an uncomfortable implication: some communities will adapt in ways that reproduce existing hierarchies, exclusions, and power structures. Local autonomy in the absence of structural change can be autonomy for the already powerful to continue marginalising the already marginal. The Nagaland women's exclusion from the legislative assembly is precisely a case where 'local systems' — customary law, tribal governance — are the mechanism of exclusion. More local autonomy is not obviously a solution here.

Nachiket Mor's health piece gestures at this tension without fully resolving it. His argument for local health systems in India is structurally sound. But he also acknowledges that without a significant increase in public health budgets — a structural, national-level commitment — local systems will adapt to scarcity rather than to the health needs of communities. The local and the structural need each other. But the mechanisms through which they reinforce rather than undermine each other are not fully worked out in FF's coverage.

The second unresolved tension is between Gall's Law and the urgency of India's problems. Start with a simple working system and let it evolve: this is sound systems design advice. It is also politically very difficult advice to give to communities living in inadequate health systems, without clean water, in urban environments generating disease. The luxury of evolutionary development is unevenly distributed. The people most affected by systemic failures are the ones who can least afford to wait for systems to evolve. FF's coverage acknowledges this tension — the microcredit example in FF Daily #308 (2021) is precisely a case of a simple system that worked at the design level but failed the users who needed it most — but does not resolve it into a clear prescriptive framework.

The third tension is within the systems thinking framework itself. The argument that cause and effect are distant in time and space — that you cannot understand what is generating your current situation by looking at proximate events — is epistemically correct and practically debilitating in equal measure. If the causes of India's health crisis are fifty years of compounding under-investment, embedded in political economy, reproduced by institutional incentive structures, and interwoven with social norms around gender and caste, then what exactly is the practitioner in a district health office supposed to do on Monday? FF celebrates the systems view without always providing the action framework. This is not a failure of intellectual honesty — it may be the honest acknowledgement that the action framework is genuinely uncertain. But it leaves a gap.

---

**The Missing Voices**

The most significant absence in FF's systems thinking coverage is the perspective of frontline public sector workers. The health systems analysis, the urban governance analysis, the Local Systems Solutions series — all of these are framed by thinkers who have engaged with Indian public systems from outside: as policy advocates, researchers, former corporate leaders, civil society figures. The perspective of the ASHA worker navigating a healthcare system whose design was made above her pay grade, the municipal sanitation worker who can see the interdependencies between garbage collection, political patronage, and neighbourhood poverty more clearly than any urban planner — these perspectives are structurally absent from FF's coverage. This is not a failure unique to FF; it is a failure of the entire English-language Indian intellectual media ecosystem. But it means that the systems thinking coverage, however sophisticated, is built on an incomplete model of the systems it is describing.

The absence of economists is also notable. Systems thinking, as FF covers it, is primarily drawn from management science, sociology, and complexity theory. The political economy lens — the microeconomic analysis of incentive structures, the macroeconomic analysis of resource allocation — appears in Mor's health piece but is largely absent from the broader coverage. This matters because the most powerful levers in many of the systems FF describes are economic levers, and understanding how they work requires economic analysis that the systems thinking framework, as deployed in FF's coverage, doesn't fully incorporate.

Rural India is physically present in several FF pieces — Karnik's village health example, Mor's healthcare analysis — but it enters the analysis primarily as a site of problems rather than as a source of solutions. There is a significant body of practice knowledge in rural India about managing complex interdependencies — in water management, in common land governance, in cooperative agriculture — that does not appear in FF's coverage. Elinor Ostrom's work on common pool resource governance, which is essentially a systems thinking framework built from rural community practice, is not cited. The knowledge systems of communities that have been managing complex interdependencies for generations — without management frameworks or MIT Sloan concepts — remain invisible in FF's version of systems thinking.

---

**What This Means For You**

**The one belief to update.** You probably believe that you can solve a complex problem by getting the right experts in the room and coordinating their recommendations. This belief is wrong in a specific, consequential way. Expertise is a silo. Coordination of silos is not the same as systems understanding. The evidence across eight years of FF's reporting — from Arun Maira's Local Systems Solutions framework to the Nagaland gender exclusion case to the Aswan Dam — points to the same structural reality: the thing generating your problem is almost never located in a single domain, and the intervention that addresses it in a single domain will produce consequences in the others that you didn't model. Getting better experts is not the answer. Building the capacity to see across their silos is.

**The one thing to stop.** Stop measuring the success of your interventions only at the point of delivery. The rehabilitation centre that housed its residents successfully was a success by the metrics of the programme. The chaos it created for the surrounding community was invisible to those metrics. You are almost certainly running the same mistake in your organisation or policy domain: measuring the first-order output (the pilot worked, the programme hit its targets, the product launched) while the second-order consequences accumulate in parts of the system you're not watching. Build a mechanism — however informal — for tracking what happens downstream and laterally from your interventions, over a longer time horizon than your reporting cycle.

**The one thing to start.** Start mapping the feedback loops in the system you are trying to change. Not as an academic exercise. As a pre-condition for action. Before the next intervention, ask: what is currently generating this outcome? Not who is responsible — what feedback loop is producing it? And what will happen to the other parts of the system if I change this one? This does not require a formal system dynamics model. It requires thirty minutes with a whiteboard and the discipline to trace causes and consequences rather than jumping to solutions. The practitioners who do this — Ramanathan in urban governance, Mor in health, Maruwada at EkStep — are the ones whose interventions are less likely to generate the next problem while solving the current one.

The conviction to carry out of this page: every system you are embedded in is producing the outcomes it was designed to produce, whether or not you designed it — and the question is never whether you are in a system, only whether you can see it clearly enough to act on its structure rather than its symptoms.
