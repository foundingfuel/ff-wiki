## Learning & Education

Every year, India produces roughly 1.5 million engineers, 600,000 MBAs, and millions more graduates from colleges whose names no one outside their district has heard of. And every year, employers report that fewer than half of them are hireable. The system is working exactly as designed — which is the problem.

The conventional diagnosis points to quality: better faculty, better infrastructure, better curriculum. Fix the inputs, fix the output. This is wrong. The deeper failure is structural and philosophical. Indian education — from JEE coaching centres to IIM classrooms — is built around a single premise: learning is the transfer of information from authority to recipient. The student's job is retention. The institution's job is delivery. What this produces is not knowledge. It is the performance of knowledge.

Meanwhile, the world is shifting in ways that make this failure catastrophic. Every rule of business that used to turn over every twenty years now turns over every five. The skills that got you here will not get you there. The specific content you learned is less important than your ability to unlearn it and build again. And India's educational institutions — with rare, celebrated exceptions — are not built for this. They are built for a world of stable knowledge and predictable careers that no longer exists.

This page is not about what is wrong with Indian education in the abstract. That is a conversation without end. This page is about how learning actually works — for individuals, for organisations, for institutions — and what the evidence from a decade of reporting says practitioners must understand to stay relevant, build capability, and lead transformation. The principles here will be uncomfortable. Some contradict what you were taught. That is the point.

## Principles

---

### Principle 1

> The education system is not broken — it is doing exactly what it was designed to do, which is the problem.

**What This Means & Why**
When we say a system is broken, we imply it is failing to meet its own goals. But India's education system is meeting its designed goal with precision: it selects, sorts, and credentialises. It was never designed to produce curious, capable, independent thinkers. It was designed to produce ranked, certified candidates for a job market that itself no longer knows what it wants. Calling this broken misses the point — it is a machine doing its job while the job itself has become irrelevant.

**The India Reality**
India has more business schools than any country on earth — a quarter of the global total — but only three have international accreditation. This is not a resource problem. The schools are producing what they intended to produce. The problem is the intention itself.

**The Evidence**
In 'How to create an Indian MBA' (2015), Rishikesha T Krishnan argues that Indian management education has copied the American model without asking what India actually needs: execution in complex, multi-stakeholder environments; managing government; leading aspirational, culturally diverse workforces. The American MBA was designed for American capitalism. The Indian MBA was designed to look like the American MBA. Neither was designed for what an Indian manager actually faces on Monday morning.

**The Implication**
When evaluating a learning programme — for yourself or your organisation — ask not whether it is accredited or well-ranked, but what specific capability it builds that is measurably absent today.

### Principle 2

> The goal of education was always to teach thinking; somewhere along the way, we confused the measure with the goal.

**What This Means & Why**
Knowledge was a proxy for thinking — a way to tell whether thinking had occurred. At some point, institutions stopped pursuing the underlying goal and started optimising the proxy. The result is students who can pass exams about experiments they have never performed, remember the description of dahi-making without knowing that dahi is not made in a fridge, and quote frameworks they have never applied. The map replaced the territory so thoroughly that most people no longer notice the territory is gone.

**The India Reality**
Jyotsna Vijapurkar, documenting science education in Indian schools, found students who told her they had "done" an experiment when they meant they had "covered" the description in the textbook. These were not weak students. This is the mainstream. The equation of books with reality is not a bug — it is the core feature of an examination-oriented system.

**The Evidence**
In 'AI in Academia: The Grind, the Gain, and the Great Recalibration' (2025), Kavi Arasu names this directly: "The goal of education was always to teach thinking — knowledge was simply a measure of that thinking. Somewhere along the way, we confused the measure with the goal. Instead of focusing on fostering deep thought, we turned education into a test of memory." The arrival of AI — which can ace memory tests in seconds — has made this confusion suddenly, painfully visible.

**The Implication**
In your next team learning initiative, remove every assessment that can be passed by someone who has not actually changed their behaviour, and replace it with one that requires demonstrable application.

### Principle 3

> Deliberate practice and mere repetition are not the same thing — only one builds expertise, and most training programmes deliver the other.

**What This Means & Why**
Hours logged are not expertise accumulated. What matters is not how long you practise but how you practise: with a specific goal, with immediate feedback, with attention on the gap between current performance and target performance. Unconscious repetition — the kind that produces the feeling of fluency — is actually the enemy of expertise. Once a skill moves into automatic execution, it stops developing. You feel competent because you have stopped learning.

**The India Reality**
The misquoted "10,000-hour rule" — Gladwell's version of Anders Ericsson's research — has been absorbed into Indian corporate learning culture as a justification for time-in-seat metrics. Ten thousand hours of unconscious repetition produces ten thousand hours of practice, not expertise. Ericsson himself was explicit: the quality of practice, not the quantity, is what separates masters from journeymen.

**The Evidence**
In 'The Beatles, and mastery through deliberate practice' (2018), Founding Fuel traces the correction back to Ericsson's original research, noting that the Beatles' Hamburg years are celebrated for their hours but were actually a crucible of deliberate, goal-oriented, feedback-rich practice — playing for demanding audiences who told them immediately whether they were good. Without that feedback loop, the hours would have produced nothing. The same insight appears in 'Only perfect practice makes perfect' (2016) by Indranil Chakraborty, which uses Benjamin Franklin's writing practice as the canonical case.

**The Implication**
Redesign every recurring training programme your organisation runs by identifying the specific feedback mechanism that will tell participants — in real time — whether their performance has improved.

### Principle 4

> Training programmes separated from work are almost always a waste of time and money.

**What This Means & Why**
The dominant model of corporate learning is extractive: pull people out of their work, put them in a room or on a screen, transfer information, return them to work. The assumption is that the learning happens in the room and transfers to the work. The evidence consistently says otherwise. Learning that is not embedded in actual work — with real stakes, real feedback, and real consequences — decays fast. The training event is not the learning. At best, it is an introduction to the learning.

**The India Reality**
Indian corporates spend significantly on leadership programmes, offsite workshops, and L&D calendars, and then wonder why behaviour does not change. The issue is not the quality of the programmes. It is the model itself. As K Ramkumar's apprenticeship model in credit learning demonstrated, the deepest professional capability is built through supervised real-work exposure, not classroom simulation.

**The Evidence**
In 'The end of training' (2015), Founding Fuel documents Asian Paints' response to this problem: the company disbanded its annual training calendar and replaced it with six focused programmes directly linked to specific business outcomes. The standard model — training as entitlement, as ritual, as HR deliverable — was replaced with training as performance intervention. The test was not whether people attended. The test was whether the business outcome changed.

**The Implication**
Before approving any learning programme, require its sponsor to name the specific, measurable business outcome it will change and the mechanism by which the learning will transfer to actual work.

### Principle 5

> Curiosity is not a personality trait — it is a cognitive skill that atrophies without practice, and India's educational culture is systematically destroying it.

**What This Means & Why**
Children ask thousands of explanatory questions before they enter school. By the time they leave school, most have stopped. This is not because they have found all the answers. It is because they have been trained — relentlessly, across years — to produce answers rather than generate questions. In an environment where the teacher is the sole source of truth and questions outside the syllabus are a distraction, curiosity becomes professionally risky. Students learn that questions mark ignorance. They stop asking.

**The India Reality**
In Indian classrooms today, the hierarchy is explicit: the teacher holds knowledge, the student receives it. When AI tools began giving students instant answers to questions outside the syllabus, teachers' first response was often restriction, not engagement. As reported in 'AI in Schools: Not a Generational Divide, but a Dialogue Waiting to Happen' (2025) by Ipsita Bandyopadhyay and Ishani Ray, students said plainly: "If I ask a question in class, the teacher might say it's not in the syllabus. Why wait a year when ChatGPT gives me the answer in seconds?"

**The Evidence**
In 'Why questions score over answers' (2015), Founding Fuel documents the Right Question Institute's Question Formulation Technique — a structured pedagogy for rebuilding the question-asking capacity that schooling has suppressed. The article's central claim: "We are taught to answer, rather than question; we are taught to equate knowledge with knowing answers." The Titanic case, documented in 'The power of curiosity' (2021), makes the cost concrete: engineers who had safety questions about the ship did not raise them, for fear of appearing foolish. Questions weaponise curiosity. Suppressing questions is not just a pedagogical failure — it is an organisational risk.

**The Implication**
In your next meeting — with your team, with your board, with your students — explicitly invite the question that someone in the room is afraid to ask, and make the asking of it as safe as the answering.

### Principle 6

> Tacit knowledge is the most valuable knowledge in any organisation, and the most systematically ignored.

**What This Means & Why**
Organisations build knowledge management systems, document processes, create training manuals — and then lose the knowledge that actually runs the place when experienced people leave. This is because the knowledge that matters most — the kind that comes from years of practice in context — cannot be captured in documentation. It lives in judgment, in instinct, in the ability to read a situation that looks like other situations but is not. Polanyi's Paradox: we know more than we can tell. The organisation that recognises this invests in mentorship and apprenticeship. The one that doesn't keeps relearning the same lessons.

**The India Reality**
India's manufacturing and service sectors are full of this knowledge — in shop floors, in family businesses, in the operational layers that educated managers routinely look past. The engineer who can quote thermodynamics but cannot measure furnace temperature stands next to the illiterate worker who knows how to do it precisely, learned from the person before him. The knowledge is real. The institutional willingness to value it is rare.

**The Evidence**
In 'The ability to tap tacit knowledge' (2020), Founding Fuel cites Russell Ackoff's story of trained engineers unable to measure furnace temperature, while an illiterate old worker does it by timing his spittle evaporation — a method taught to him by his predecessor. Ackoff's point, as FF frames it, is not about shop-floor wisdom in the abstract: it is about an organisation's willingness to look for solutions in places where its hierarchy tells it not to look. "Often leaders look at one space for solutions, thereby precluding some of the most creative and effective solutions."

**The Implication**
Before solving a recurring operational problem, spend one hour talking to the people closest to the problem — not their managers — and ask them what they know that is not in any document.

### Principle 7

> The "digital native" is a myth — and treating students as one is actively harmful.

**What This Means & Why**
The assumption that young people are naturally fluent with digital learning tools because they grew up with smartphones is wrong in two ways. First, fluency with social and entertainment apps does not transfer to disciplined learning in digital environments. Second, most of the learning that shaped today's students — including their study habits, their attention spans, their relationship with teachers — happened in physical classrooms. Projecting digital fluency onto them is a category error that justifies lazy pedagogical transplants.

**The India Reality**
India's pandemic-era schooling crisis made this visible at scale. Schools moved online and performed the single most expensive test of the digital native hypothesis in history. The results were catastrophic. As reported in 'Unplanned online education is short-changing our children' (2021) by Piyul Mukherjee, the real culprit was not connectivity — it was pedagogy: "An education and schooling system that has thoughtlessly and lazily, without any creativity and empathy, simply transplanted the traditional mode of physical learning onto the online platform."

**The Evidence**
In 'FF Recommends: How to think about higher education' (2021), Founding Fuel states it plainly: "It is actually incorrect to suggest that the generation studying in virtual classrooms are digital natives. Almost all of their learning and schooling was in a world away from the internet. Screens were places where they went to unwind." The students who chose Montessori and Waldorf schools specifically to escape rote learning found, as documented by Piyul Mukherjee in 'They chose schools for creative learning. What's the benefit now, they ask' (2021), that online schooling transferred the pedagogical burden to parents — who had not signed up to be teachers.

**The Implication**
If you are designing or evaluating any digital learning programme, audit whether it has rebuilt pedagogy for the medium or simply moved physical content onto a screen, and restart it if it has done the latter.

### Principle 8

> Your learning style is the most important piece of self-knowledge you can act on — and most people never acquire it.

**What This Means & Why**
People differ significantly in how they learn best: some through reading, some through listening, some through doing, some through talking, some through writing. These differences are not preferences to be accommodated when convenient. They are performance fundamentals. Ignoring them is not just inefficient — it condemns you, in Drucker's words, to nonperformance. Most people spend decades in environments designed for someone else's learning style and conclude they are not intelligent, when the truth is they have never been taught in a way that works for them.

**The India Reality**
Indian classrooms are designed almost exclusively for one learning style: listening to authority and reproducing what was heard. Churchill, who could not perform in school, became one of the greatest communicators of the twentieth century — a writer who learned by writing. Beethoven left no lecture notes but composed by doing. The system that could not accommodate them would today label them remedial.

**The Evidence**
In 'What's your learning style?' (2021), Founding Fuel draws on Peter Drucker's 'Managing Oneself' to make the case: "Of all the important pieces of self-knowledge, understanding how you learn is the easiest to acquire… yet, acting on this knowledge is the key to performance; or rather, not acting on this knowledge condemns one to nonperformance." Drucker's examples — Churchill learning through writing speeches he never delivered, Beethoven keeping messy notebooks not for reference but for process — illustrate that elite performance often depends on finding the mode of learning that matches the person, not conforming to the institution's preferred mode.

**The Implication**
This week, identify one learning project you have been avoiding and ask honestly whether you have been approaching it in the wrong modality — then switch the medium before you switch the material.

### Principle 9

> The plateau is not failure — it is where mastery actually lives, and refusing to accept it is why most people stop growing.

**What This Means & Why**
Every skill-building journey has the same arc: rapid early improvement, followed by long stretches of almost imperceptible progress. Our schooling and career systems are designed around the early arc — feedback, grades, promotions, visible progress. They have no mechanism for the plateau. So people interpret the plateau as evidence that they have hit a ceiling, or chosen the wrong path, or run out of talent. They quit precisely at the point where mastery would begin to accumulate if they stayed.

**The India Reality**
India's obsession with rank — JEE rank, board percentage, company valuation — creates a culture where anything that is not visibly improving is treated as failing. The practitioner who is deepening quietly, consolidating slowly, building the undramatic substrate of real expertise, has no institutional support for that process. The culture celebrates the spike and punishes the plateau.

**The Evidence**
In 'Plateauing out matters much' (2022), Founding Fuel draws on George Leonard's 'Mastery' to name the problem directly: "If our life is a good one, a life of mastery, most of it will be spent on the plateau… Where in our upbringing, our schooling, our career are we explicitly taught to value, to enjoy, even to love the plateau?" Leonard's argument is that the plateau is not the gap between peaks of progress — it is the substance of a practised life. The "real juice of life," as he puts it, is in the process, not the product.

**The Implication**
Name one skill or practice you abandoned because progress felt too slow, and recommit to it for eight weeks without measuring outcome — only consistency.

### Principle 10

> B-school research in India is written by academics for academics — and this is not a niche problem, it is a systemic failure that impoverishes practice.

**What This Means & Why**
The assumption embedded in most business school structures is that research leads practice: academics discover truths, practitioners apply them. In India, the opposite is closer to reality. Practice is moving faster than theory; the most interesting management experiments are happening in companies, not in papers. And yet Indian b-schools, caught between copying American research models and the pressure of rankings, have produced a research culture that is largely invisible to the people it is supposed to serve.

**The India Reality**
India's b-schools have produced management thinkers of global stature — but CK Prahalad, Sumantra Ghoshal, and Jagdish Sheth all had to leave India to achieve that status. The question, as Rishikesha T Krishnan frames it in 'What does it take to become a Management Guru?' (2015), is not why they succeeded abroad — it is why they could not have risen in India. The institutional conditions for rigorous, practice-relevant management research simply do not exist at scale here.

**The Evidence**
In 'How business schools in India have killed off research' (2018), Harsh Vardhan documents the mechanisms of this failure: research incentive structures that reward publication in journals no practitioner reads, a disdain for consulting that disconnects faculty from real management problems, and the absence of the cross-disciplinary intellectual culture that produces original management thought. The founding model of IIM-A — where every faculty member was required to research, consult, and teach, with no exceptions for seniority — is cited in 'Untold stories about how India built its higher ed institutions' (2021) as the counter-example: the Nirma/HUL marketing consulting work fed directly back into the classroom, and the classroom fed the consulting.

**The Implication**
If you lead an organisation, invite one b-school researcher to spend three months embedded in a real operational challenge — not as a consultant, but as a learner — and see what happens to the quality of the research they produce afterward.

### Principle 11

> Learning requires making mistakes — not as an unfortunate side effect, but as the primary mechanism.

**What This Means & Why**
The culture around learning in most organisations treats mistakes as failures of execution, not as data about reality. The result is a systematic avoidance of the conditions under which learning actually occurs. The students who arrive at elite universities having never made a significant mistake are the most brittle when they finally make one — because they have been trained to avoid the very experience that would have taught them how to recover. Dennett's argument is precise: mistakes are not just opportunities for learning, they are "in an important sense, the only opportunity for learning or making something truly new."

**The India Reality**
India's fixed-mindset culture — absorbed from families, reinforced by schools, calcified by competitive examinations — is particularly hostile to visible failure. Parul Gupta of Springboard, as documented in 'An edtech entrepreneur's learning journey' (2021), describes it directly: "In India, how we grow up, an almost 'fixed mindset' is beaten down in our heads. It was only when I started my entrepreneurial journey that I really pushed my boundaries… It was unlearning of everything I had lived my life by."

**The Evidence**
In 'Why mistakes matter' (2021), Founding Fuel quotes Daniel Dennett: "Many of the students who arrive at very competitive universities pride themselves in not making mistakes… I often find that I have to encourage them to cultivate the habit of making mistakes, the best learning opportunities of all." The article links this to Carol Dweck's research on entity versus incremental theories of intelligence, documented in 'Incremental learning' (2021): children praised for being "smart" — rather than for effort or process — become the most brittle under pressure, because any failure threatens the identity that "smart" has built.

**The Implication**
In the next performance review conversation you have with someone on your team, identify one mistake they made in the last quarter and ask what they learned from it before you say anything else about performance.

### Principle 12

> Self-directed learning has been superior to institutional credentialing for centuries — AI has merely made this gap impossible to ignore.

**What This Means & Why**
The credential is a signal — a shorthand for "this person has been exposed to this material and passed the tests." It has never been a reliable indicator of what someone can actually do. For most of history, serious learning happened outside institutions: through books, through mentors, through practice, through correspondence with other serious thinkers. Institutions provided access to resources and social proof. They did not monopolise learning itself. The disruption AI is forcing on education is not new in kind — it is just suddenly, pressingly obvious.

**The India Reality**
The Kerala paradox remains instructive: high mass literacy, high unemployment, a ghost-writing economy for doctoral dissertations. Literacy without intellectual independence is not learning — it is the credential stripped of its substance. India's coaching culture for JEE and NEET produces students who can pass specific tests with extraordinary precision and who are often genuinely unprepared for what comes after.

**The Evidence**
In 'Teaching yourself' (2022), Founding Fuel draws on Susan Wise Bauer's graduate school experience to argue: "For centuries, women and men undertook this sort of learning — reading, taking notes, discussing books and ideas with friends — without needing institutional certification." Thomas Jefferson's advice to his nephew is cited: "It would be a waste of time to attend a professor of this. It is to be acquired from books." The principle is not anti-institutional — it is a reminder that the institution serves the learning, not the reverse.

**The Implication**
Identify one capability gap in your work right now and design a six-week self-directed learning sprint for it — books, practitioners to talk to, things to try — before you look for a course to take.

### Principle 13

> Writing is thinking made visible — and organisations that do not build writing cultures are organisations that do not build thinking cultures.

**What This Means & Why**
The assumption is that you write down what you already know. The reality is that writing is how you find out what you know and — crucially — what you do not know. The discipline of putting thought into complete sentences forces the exposure of gaps that fluent speech conceals. Organisations that communicate through slides and talking points are systematically hiding their thinking from scrutiny, including from themselves. This is comfortable. It is also how organisations make bad decisions confidently.

**The India Reality**
India's linguistic diversity makes this principle both more complex and more urgent. As documented in 'Why writing matters' (2022), India's 1,200-plus languages mean that the knowledge locked in those languages — ways of thinking, framing, understanding — is largely inaccessible to a learning culture that operates primarily in English. The Bhashini project, described as "a global first" model of collaborative AI translation, is an attempt to unlock this. But the organisational implication is immediate: a team that does not write, in whatever language, is a team that is not thinking out loud together.

**The Evidence**
In 'Why writing matters' (2022), Founding Fuel cites Paul Graham's formulation: "A company asked why it was so hard to hire a good writer. I told them it was because good writing is an illusion: what people call good writing is actually good thinking." In 'The rewards of writing' (2024), Krishna Jha extends this: "Writing shines a light on my ignorance." The discipline is not cosmetic — it is epistemic.

**The Implication**
Replace the next internal presentation in your organisation with a two-page written brief that the audience reads before the meeting — and watch what changes in the quality of the discussion.

### Principle 14

> India's vast talent base is not being wasted by bad luck — it is being wasted by systemic design, and isolated islands of excellence prove the design can be changed.

**What This Means & Why**
When exceptions appear — a school in rural Madhya Pradesh with girls winning university ranks, a Jharkhand university VC navigating digital exclusion with structural clarity, a chess ecosystem in Tamil Nadu producing twenty-six grandmasters from one inspiration — they are treated as flukes, as the product of exceptional individuals who transcended a broken system. This framing is wrong and dangerous, because it lets the system off the hook. The exceptions are not flukes. They are proof of concept. They demonstrate that when institutional conditions are right, talent that the system was designed to overlook surfaces anyway.

**The India Reality**
VidyaGyan, as documented in 'VidyaGyan: Allowing Indian Talent to Flower and Bloom' (2015) by Rishikesha T Krishnan, finds the highest-potential students from families earning under Rs 1 lakh per year — talent that India's standard educational pathway would never surface. The programme's existence is not heartwarming. It is indicting. It proves that the talent was always there.

**The Evidence**
In 'FF Insights #706: Chennai's chess pioneers' (2022), Founding Fuel traces the arc from Manuel Aaron to Viswanathan Anand to twenty-six Tamil Nadu grandmasters. The mechanism is explicit: "When Anand became the first Indian Grandmaster, it inspired a whole generation of Chennai players. Each World Championship triggered a new cohort." This is not talent discovery — it is talent activation through inspiration cascades, institutional support, and mentorship. The same mechanism that produced chess grandmasters can produce scientists, engineers, and leaders, if the institutional conditions are built deliberately.

**The Implication**
Identify one talent pipeline in your organisation or community that is systematically overlooking a specific demographic and ask what one structural change — not a programme, a structural change — would activate the talent that is already there.

### Principle 15

> The entrepreneurial mindset in learning is not about starting companies — it is about treating knowledge as a means to action, not an end in itself.

**What This Means & Why**
Effectuation — Saras Sarasvathy's framework for how entrepreneurs actually think — begins with available means and works toward emergent goals, rather than setting fixed goals and acquiring the knowledge to reach them. This is the opposite of how most education trains people to think. The entrepreneurial learner does not wait until they know enough to act. They act with what they have, learn from what happens, and adjust. Most corporate learners are trained for the opposite: gather information, analyse, decide, execute. In stable environments, this works. In the environment India is now in, it does not.

**The India Reality**
India's startup culture has, paradoxically, created a new orthodoxy that may be as limiting as the old one. As S. Ramakrishna Velamuri argues in 'From employee to entrepreneur' (2023), the pendulum has swung too far: Shark Tank India has made regular employment "decidedly uncool," pushing young people into entrepreneurship before they have built the domain knowledge and practical experience that actually predicts startup survival. Entrepreneurial learning is not about skipping the experience phase — it is about bringing a different orientation to it.

**The Evidence**
In 'How entrepreneurs learn' (2019), Indrajit Gupta applies Sarasvathy's effectuation framework to the learning context: entrepreneurial learning follows the logic of working with available means, remaining open to surprise, building from what is present rather than what is planned. The contrast with causal reasoning — set the goal, acquire what you need, execute — is the contrast between learning for a stable world and learning for an uncertain one.

**The Implication**
Take one area of your work where you have been waiting until you know enough to act, and commit to a small, reversible action this week that will generate real information about what you actually need to learn.

### Principle 16

> The systems thinking gap — treating every problem as isolable — is producing educated people who cannot understand the world they are operating in.

**What This Means & Why**
Children are taught in subjects. Organisations are managed in functions. Governments are structured in departments. Each domain has its own vocabulary, its own metrics, its own definition of success. The world does not respect these divisions. Complex problems — education quality, public health, economic development, technology adoption — are the product of systems, and a mind trained to think in isolated subjects will consistently misdiagnose what is actually happening and prescribe interventions that make things worse in adjacent domains.

**The India Reality**
India's education crisis is not an education problem. It is a health problem (poor nutrition reduces cognitive capacity), an infrastructure problem (no roads means no schools), an economic problem (families pull children out of school when money is short), a gender problem (girls are pulled first), and a political economy problem (private capital flows into credential-producing institutions, not learning-producing ones). Solutions that address any one of these in isolation will not work. The evidence from Pratham's work — teaching at the right level, rebuilding from where children actually are rather than where the curriculum assumes they are — works precisely because it starts from a systems view of where children are, not a syllabus view of where they should be.

**The Evidence**
In 'Study the system, not its fragments' (2020), Founding Fuel draws on Arun Maira's argument: "A new Enlightenment founded on 'systems thinking' is required to understand complexity — not by breaking complexity into simpler components, but by understanding patterns and relationships among the components of complex systems. Children are taught to think of the world in compartmentalized subjects… knowledge of systems as a whole is falling apart." Maira's IVI framing — Innovation-VUCA-Innovation — argues that management education faces a "double bind": the very conditions that demand systems thinking are also the conditions that make piecemeal, subject-by-subject responses feel most natural.

**The Implication**
Before proposing any solution to a persistent problem in your organisation or community, map three systems that the problem is connected to and ask how your proposed solution affects each of them.

### Principle 17

> Mentorship requires honest, difficult feedback — not encouragement, not validation, not career sponsorship.

**What This Means & Why**
The mentor relationship has been rebranded in corporate India as a form of sponsorship or career navigation: the senior person who opens doors and endorses the junior. This is not mentorship — it is networking with an emotional dimension. Real mentorship, as Robert Greene frames it, requires the mentor to see the mentee clearly — including their weaknesses — and to give them "the sharpest dose of reality that is possible." This is uncomfortable for both parties. It is also the only version that actually accelerates development.

**The India Reality**
India's hierarchical culture makes honest downward feedback rare and honest upward feedback almost nonexistent. The mentor who tells a promising junior that their presentation was unclear, their thinking was shallow, or their instinct was wrong is performing an act of courage in a culture where authority is supposed to validate, not challenge. The alternative — encouragement, soft feedback, the preservation of the relationship over the development of the person — is common, comfortable, and a developmental dead end.

**The Evidence**
In 'To achieve mastery you need tough love' (2020), Founding Fuel cites Robert Greene's 'Mastery': "Masters are those who by nature have suffered to get to where they are… As mentors, they alone can gauge the extent of our progress, the weaknesses in our character, the ordeals we must go through to advance. In this day and age, you must get the sharpest dose of reality that is possible from your mentor." Greene's prescription is not cruelty — it is the recognition that without accurate feedback, practice cannot be deliberate and mastery cannot accumulate.

**The Implication**
In your next mentoring conversation, give one piece of feedback that you have been withholding because you were worried about how it would land, and do it with enough specificity that the person knows exactly what to change.

### Principle 18

> Speed in learning is the enemy of depth, and India's information culture is systematically producing the former at the expense of the latter.

**What This Means & Why**
The velocity at which information now flows creates an illusion of learning. You have read about something. You have heard a podcast. You have seen a thread. You feel informed. You are not. Genuine understanding requires slowness — re-reading, sleeping on it, returning with new questions, testing comprehension against reality. The brain needs the diffuse mode as much as the focused mode. The culture that celebrates consuming more, faster, is the culture that produces people who know the shape of ideas without knowing their substance.

**The India Reality**
The TED Talk culture — articulate people giving audiences compressed, polished versions of complex ideas — has a particular grip on India's aspirational professional class. As Charles Assisi argues in 'Performance monkeys on the TED stage' (2017), these performances give audiences false permission to learn superficially: the feeling of having understood Tai Lopez's book-a-day habit is precisely the obstacle to actually reading carefully.

**The Evidence**
In 'Why we should re-examine the clichés in our heads' (2020), Founding Fuel cites the story of a Polish dancer: the city producing bad dancers offered "delicious" lessons "with tons of new information." The city producing great dancers focused on one core thing per lesson, drilled to perfection. "The takeaway from her narrative is depth matters over breadth." Barbara Oakley's research, cited in 'FF Recommends: Note Taking Apps' (2020), adds the neuroscience: both focused and diffuse (open-monitoring) modes are necessary, and the culture that promotes only focused mode — relentless consumption, no breaks — actually impairs learning.

**The Implication**
Pick one idea you think you understand from something you read in the last month and spend thirty minutes writing out your understanding of it — without looking at the source — then check where you were wrong.

---

### Principle 19

> The academic rigour that equates coverage with depth is not rigour — it is cognitive overload disguised as standards.

**What This Means & Why**
There is a persistent confusion in education between the breadth of material covered and the depth of understanding produced. Covering more material in less time does not produce more learning — it produces more exposure. Students under the pressure of dense syllabuses resort to the only strategy that produces exam success: memorisation. The irony is that the institutions most committed to appearing rigorous — through exhaustive curricula, comprehensive exams, heavy reading lists — are often the ones producing the shallowest learners.

**The India Reality**
As Uma Narain documents in 'Relearning the Craft of Teaching' (2026), Indian academic culture explicitly "equates rigour with range — with cramming every possible topic into a course. The unintended consequence is cognitive overload: students under pressure, resorting to memorisation rather than building the habits of critical thinking." This is not a failure of individual teachers. It is a system-level design choice, sustained by the belief that more is more.

**The Evidence**
In 'Relearning the Craft of Teaching' (2026), Uma Narain draws the contrast with Harvard's Bok Center approach: "At Bok, rigour did not come from covering everything. It came from slowing down, choosing a few foundational ideas, and returning to them until understanding deepened." The article also names the PowerPoint problem: "Glued to the screen, it became easy to teach through the deck rather than to the students. Eye contact thinned. Rapport weakened." The medium of teaching became the obstacle to the purpose of teaching.

**The Implication**
If you design or deliver any learning experience, cut one third of the content and use the recovered time for one idea you return to repeatedly, from different angles, until the group can explain it in their own words.

---

### Principle 20

> AI will not replace human learning — but humans who use AI to avoid thinking will be replaced by humans who use AI to think better.

**What This Means & Why**
The arrival of generative AI in education has produced two panic responses: ban it, or surrender to it. Both miss the point. AI is extraordinarily capable at the tasks that Indian education has historically most valued — recalling information, generating plausible text, passing standardised tests. This means AI has effectively rendered the credential-as-memory-test obsolete. What it cannot do is what education was always supposed to produce: genuine judgment, contextual wisdom, the ability to sit with uncertainty and think clearly. The question is not what AI should do — it is what humans must now do that AI cannot.

**The India Reality**
India's students are already ahead of the institutional response. As documented in 'AI in Schools: Not a Generational Divide, but a Dialogue Waiting to Happen' (2025) by Ipsita Bandyopadhyay and Ishani Ray, students are using AI not to avoid learning but to bypass pedagogical gatekeeping — getting answers to questions their teachers dismiss as outside the syllabus, moving at their own pace, pursuing curiosity that the institution does not have space for. The institution's response — restriction, detection, policing — addresses the symptom while leaving the disease untouched.

**The Evidence**
In 'AI in Academia: The Grind, the Gain, and the Great Recalibration' (2025), Kavi Arasu refuses both the ban and the surrender: "The real challenge is not what AI should do — it's what humans should still do." The California Faculty Association's blanket restriction is described as "quite the contrary" to what the evidence supports. The article simultaneously pushes back against naive AI adoption: the grind — rewriting a paper, wrestling with ideas, receiving tough feedback — has value precisely because the struggle is the learning. The implication is not that AI removes the struggle. It is that the struggle must now be at a higher level.

**The Implication**
In one domain of your work this month, use AI to handle the retrieval and synthesis tasks, then spend the recovered time on the judgment task that AI cannot do — and notice what that judgment task actually requires of you.

## FF's View

**The Core Conviction**

FF's single most important argument on learning and education is this: the system is not broken because it lacks resources. It is broken because it has the wrong theory of what learning is.

This is the claim that most practitioners resist, because it removes a convenient excuse. If the problem were money, the solution would be money. If the problem were infrastructure, the solution would be infrastructure. But FF's reporting, across a decade and hundreds of conversations with educators, entrepreneurs, business leaders, and policymakers, points to something harder: the dominant model of education — in schools, in business schools, in corporate training rooms — is built on a fundamentally mistaken premise. It treats learning as content delivery. And it has been doing so for so long that we have confused the measure with the goal.

In 'AI in Academia: The Grind, the Gain, and the Great Recalibration' (2025), Kavi Arasu puts it plainly: "The goal of education was always to teach thinking — knowledge was simply a measure of that thinking. Somewhere along the way, we confused the measure with the goal. Instead of focusing on fostering deep thought, we turned education into a test of memory." This is not a critique of any single institution. It is a critique of a civilizational choice, made gradually, over decades, in classrooms and boardrooms across the country.

The consequences run everywhere. Students who have "done" an experiment by reading its description in a textbook. Business school graduates who can write a strategy deck but cannot execute in a complex environment. Corporate training programmes that consume budget and produce nothing measurable. An engineering college boom built, as M. Rajshekhar reported in 'An engineering college boom built on a foundation of dysfunction' (2021), on land conversions, money laundering, and regulatory arbitrage — not on any theory of human development whatsoever.

The conviction that FF holds, and holds consistently, is that learning is not a product to be delivered or a credential to be conferred. It is a practice to be cultivated — daily, deliberately, uncomfortably, for life. Every principle FF has reported on, every thinker it has engaged with, every practitioner it has interviewed, points back to this. The organisations and individuals who get this right build capability that compounds. The ones who get it wrong perform rituals that consume time and money and leave people exactly where they started.

The practitioner reading this page needs to sit with that claim, because it has implications that go all the way down. If learning is a practice, not a product, then the entire architecture of how Indian institutions think about education — from the JEE coaching system to the corporate L&D calendar to the MBA programme — needs to be examined from first principles, not incremented.

**What India Means for This Theme**

India's relationship with learning and education is unlike any other country's, and not in the ways that are usually celebrated.

Start with the scale of waste. In 'VidyaGyan: Allowing Indian Talent to Flower and Bloom' (2015), Rishikesha T Krishnan described one of the foundational tragedies of Indian public life: the systematic suppression of talent in families earning under Rs 1 lakh a year. VidyaGyan exists precisely because the talent exists — it just never surfaces. The child who would have been a world-class scientist, a transformative entrepreneur, a brilliant doctor, is sitting in a government school somewhere in Uttar Pradesh learning by rote, being taught by an absent or undertrained teacher, preparing for an examination that tests nothing useful, and will enter adulthood with a certificate that means almost nothing and a set of capabilities that were never developed. This is not a marginal problem. This is India's central problem. Everything else — the innovation deficit, the productivity gap, the quality of governance, the fragility of institutions — flows partly from this.

Then there is the structural distortion of the higher education system. India accounts for a quarter of all global business schools, as Krishnan noted in 'How to create an Indian MBA' (2015), but only three had AACSB accreditation at the time of writing. This is not a quantity problem. It is a purpose problem. Business schools in India were built, largely, to produce graduates for multinational corporations that wanted people who could operate in a global context while understanding Indian conditions. That was a reasonable brief for 1995. It is the wrong brief for 2025. The India that needs management talent today needs something different: people who can execute in conditions of radical uncertainty, manage without the organizational clarity that Western frameworks assume, navigate the complexity of family businesses, government relationships, and a workforce that is both aspirational and first-generation professional.

The Bharat dimension is where the analysis gets most uncomfortable for urban, English-speaking practitioners. India's learning crisis is not primarily a crisis of the IITs and IIMs. It is a crisis of the 200 million children in government schools. In 'Change is possible for public education in India. Azim Premji shows how' (2017), Founding Fuel reported on what it actually takes to move the needle in this system — not a technology solution, not a curriculum reform announced from Delhi, but patient, granular, school-by-school work that respects the complexity of what teachers face and builds from what already works. The Pratham approach, reported in 'FF Daily #487: The risk-taking funnel' (2021), makes the same argument from a different angle: Karnataka's "Odu Karnataka" programme moved over half a million children in Standards IV-V by twenty to thirty percentage points in basic reading and math in sixty days, not by importing a new curriculum, but by teaching at the right level — meeting children where they actually were, not where the syllabus said they should be.

The COVID-19 pandemic exposed what was always true but easy to ignore. In 'Working From Home Daily #1-70' (2020), Founding Fuel quoted Sonajharia Minz, Vice-Chancellor of Sido Kanhu Murmu University in Jharkhand: "Till date, some districts in Odisha have no roads and electricity. If digital education becomes the final medium of teaching in the post-lockdown phase, we will be consciously depriving and eliminating a section which does not have access to this platform." The digital learning pivot of 2020 was, in much of India, not a learning opportunity but a learning loss — and the loss was not distributed equally. As 'Unplanned online education is short-changing our children' (2021), by Piyul Mukherjee, documented, even the families with access were let down, because the system transplanted the worst habits of physical schooling onto online platforms without rethinking anything. Sixty thousand children dropped out of school in Andhra Pradesh alone. Girls in Classes 9 and 10 were being married off by distressed families. Boys took up work in brick kilns. A learning crisis became a human development catastrophe.

India also carries a specific cultural distortion that operates below the surface. In 'An edtech entrepreneur's learning journey' (2021), Parul Gupta of Springboard described it with unusual clarity: "In India, how we grow up, an almost 'fixed mindset' is beaten down in our heads." The system is calibrated to identify and sort, not to develop. The examination is the point, not the preparation. Marks are the currency, not capability. And so the bright child who does well in this system arrives at adulthood with a credential, a fixed theory of their own intelligence, and very little experience of genuine struggle, failure, recovery, and growth. The system produces people who are excellent at performing competence and fragile under conditions that actually require it.

What works elsewhere and won't work here, at least not without translation: the American model of liberal arts education, the European model of vocational training, the Chinese model of state-directed mass examination. None of these can be lifted and placed. India's learning challenge is not simply one of access or quality in the conventional sense. It is a challenge of building, from scratch in many cases, a culture of learning — in families, in organisations, in institutions — that can operate at the scale of 1.4 billion people, across 22 scheduled languages and 780 indigenous tongues, across a digital divide that is not narrowing fast enough, with a teaching workforce that is itself under-trained and under-supported. That is the actual brief. It is harder than any policy document acknowledges.

**Where Global Best Practices Break Down**

The dominant global management orthodoxy on learning, as transmitted through HBR, McKinsey, and the better American business schools, has three foundational assumptions. First, that the primary constraint on adult learning is motivation — create the right incentives, and people will learn. Second, that the primary vehicle for learning is the programme — design a good curriculum, deliver it well, and capability will follow. Third, that the measure of learning is completion — did the person attend, did they pass, did they get the credential.

All three assumptions are wrong, and India's evidence shows why with unusual sharpness.

On motivation: The problem in Indian organisations is not primarily that people lack motivation to learn. The problem is that the context in which learning is supposed to happen actively suppresses it. The fixed mindset is not a personal failing; it is a rational adaptation to a system that has consistently punished failure and rewarded the performance of knowing. The child who never asked a question in class because the teacher would say it wasn't in the syllabus becomes the junior manager who never admits uncertainty in front of a senior because the cultural cue is clear. Motivation is not the lever.

On the programme: In 'The end of training' (2015), Founding Fuel asked whether corporate training is an anachronism that the business world continues to cling to. The critique was precise: executives are dragged into programmes that have no link to actual business outcomes, treated as entitlement (the leadership programme as a reward for tenure, not a developmental intervention), and unable to demonstrate any measurable impact. Asian Paints' response — disbanding the annual training calendar entirely, replacing it with six focused programmes explicitly linked to business outcomes — is the counter-model. Work is the learning. Learning embedded in work sticks. Learning separated from work is entertainment at best, ritual at worst.

On completion: The B-school campus placement number is the paradigm case of measurement corruption. In 'The b-school campus placement BS—and how to clean it up' (2018), Indrajit Gupta reported that nearly 70% of students quit their first job after graduating within nine months to a year. The "100% placement" headline, which is how business schools market themselves and how the media covers them, is not a measure of learning outcomes. It is a measure of the institution's ability to persuade a recruiter to issue an offer letter. These are not the same thing. The credential certifies completion; it says nothing about capability.

Where HBR fails most specifically in the Indian context is in its assumption that organisations are the relevant unit of analysis. The imported frameworks — talent management, succession planning, 70-20-10 learning models — are built for organisations with stable structures, clear hierarchies, and the HR sophistication to implement them. A significant fraction of the Indian economy consists of family businesses, informal enterprises, and public institutions that look nothing like the organisations these frameworks were designed for. The management challenge Krishnan identified in 'How to create an Indian MBA' (2015) — executing in a complex environment without Western-style organizational clarity, managing government stakeholders, managing a culturally diverse and first-generation professional workforce — is simply not addressed by the imported toolkit.

The Gigerenzer critique is relevant here in a specific way. In 'Gigerenzer's simple rules' (2017), NS Ramnath reported on the argument that complex models perform worse than simple heuristics under genuine uncertainty. The imported frameworks are complex models designed for environments of risk — where probabilities can be estimated and outcomes are broadly predictable. India is often an environment of genuine uncertainty. The practitioner who applies a McKinsey talent matrix in this context is not being rigorous; they are performing rigour while actually abandoning it.

The 10,000-hour rule is another import that has done damage in proportion to its popularity. As Founding Fuel reported in 'The Beatles, and mastery through deliberate practice' (2018), Gladwell's formulation was a distortion of Ericsson's research. The original finding was not about hours — it was about the quality of practice. Deliberate practice requires feedback, attention to errors, and consistent effort to push beyond current competence. Mere repetition, however extended, does not produce expertise. It produces automation — which, as Kavi Arasu argued in 'Work Smart. Not Hard' (2015), is the enemy of expertise, not its goal.

**How FF Diverges from Mainstream Media**

Indian mainstream business media covers education primarily through three lenses: rankings, funding rounds, and policy announcements. The ranking story tells you which institution moved up or down in some global index. The funding story tells you which edtech startup raised how much at what valuation. The policy story tells you what the government announced. None of these lenses, individually or together, tells you anything useful about whether learning is actually happening.

FF's divergence begins with the unit of analysis. Where mainstream media focuses on institutions, FF focuses on learners. Where mainstream media covers what is being offered, FF asks what is being absorbed. Where mainstream media treats the credential as the outcome, FF treats capability as the outcome, and asks what the gap between them reveals.

This divergence has been consistent since the earliest articles. In 'The Learned Life' (2015), Charles Assisi grounded the entire question of learning in KV Kamath's personal practice: "To stay relevant, I have to learn all the time." This is not a statement about policy or curriculum or technology. It is a statement about a personal discipline that a senior practitioner maintains because survival demands it. FF's learning coverage has always been more interested in the practitioner's inner life than in the institution's outer architecture.

The edtech boom of 2019-2022 is the clearest case where mainstream media and FF diverged in real time. The dominant coverage of edtech was about disruption, scale, and the transformation of education through technology. Founding Fuel was asking a different set of questions. In 'Will India's tryst with online courses pay off?' (2016), Indrajit Gupta raised the completion rate problem with MOOCs years before it became a mainstream concern. The infrastructure prerequisites for digital education in rural India — reported through Sonajharia Minz's testimony in 2020 — were not part of the edtech boom narrative at all. The pedagogical failures of online schooling — documented by Piyul Mukherjee in 'Unplanned online education is short-changing our children' (2021) — were visible to FF before the mainstream media acknowledged them.

FF was also early on the B-school research failure. Harsh Vardhan's essay 'How business schools in India have killed off research' (2018) made an argument that was not popular in the business education establishment: that Indian B-schools had systematically chosen prestige over rigour, rankings over relevance, and had produced an academic culture in which research was written by academics for academics, disconnected from both practice and from any India-specific knowledge base. The mainstream media's B-school coverage is largely promotional. FF's is consistently diagnostic.

On the TED Talk culture, FF was unusually direct. In 'Performance monkeys on the TED stage' (2017), Charles Assisi identified something that the mainstream learning and development conversation was actively celebrating: the articulate speaker who gives audiences permission to believe they have learned something in eighteen minutes. FF named this as a form of intellectual fraud — not in the speaker, necessarily, but in the structure of the format. "How do you read a book a day? Sometimes I take a week. But sometimes, books only have one or two things that are worth reading." The mainstream learning industry was, at that moment, producing Tai Lopez and calling it democratization of knowledge. FF called it shallow performance.

The consensus has caught up on some of these points. The edtech valuation crash of 2022-23, the growing mainstream acknowledgment of learning loss in government schools, the increasing skepticism about B-school rankings as meaningful signals — these are all positions FF had staked out earlier. The mainstream media moved when the evidence became impossible to ignore. FF moved when the analysis pointed there.

**Against Management Orthodoxy**

The received wisdom of business schools and consulting firms on learning and education can be summarized in a few propositions that FF's evidence consistently contradicts.

**The orthodoxy says: Training is the organizational response to capability gaps.**

The evidence shows: Training is usually the organizational response to the need to be seen doing something about capability gaps. In 'The end of training' (2015), Founding Fuel asked whether corporate training had become an anachronism. The evidence was that the standard corporate learning intervention — the offsite programme, the external facilitator, the competency framework and associated workshop series — produces almost no measurable improvement in actual performance. The organisations that do learning well have stopped treating it as a programme and started treating it as a property of work itself. Asian Paints disbanded the training calendar. The credit-learning apprenticeship that K Ramkumar described — learning embedded in real transactions, with a mentor present, feedback immediate — is the model that works. The sausage factory does not.

**The orthodoxy says: You need the right talent; find it through psychometric screening.**

The evidence shows: Psychometric screening, as it is actually practised, has almost no predictive validity for the qualities that matter. In 'Putting psychometrics in perspective' (2016), K Ramkumar reported on KV Kamath's experiment: fifty validated leaders, only one of whom would have been selected by standard psychometric tools. The tools measure things that can be measured with paper and pencil. Leadership capacity, which develops in context, under pressure, across time, is not among them. The high-IQ whiz kids in Kennedy's administration, as Ramkumar noted, crafted brilliant policies that defied common sense on Cuba and Vietnam. IQ is not leadership capacity. The HR function that treats psychometric screening as a reliable filter for talent is not practising talent management. It is performing it.

**The orthodoxy says: The MBA is the foundational credential for business leadership.**

The evidence shows: The MBA, as it exists in India, was designed to serve a brief that has expired. In 'How to create an Indian MBA' (2015), Krishnan argued that India's management challenges lie in execution and implementation in complex environments — family businesses, government relationships, a culturally diverse aspirational workforce, informal sector dynamics. The Indian MBA, overwhelmingly influenced by American models, prepares people for organizational contexts that look like McKinsey or Unilever. Most of India does not look like McKinsey or Unilever. The gap between what the MBA produces and what India actually needs is structural, not incidental. Srikant Datar's diagnosis — quoted in 'Masterclass: The Future of the MBA' (2020) — is that MBA programmes focus on "knowing," not on "doing" or "being." That is not a curriculum design failure. It is a theory-of-learning failure.

**The orthodoxy says: Expertise comes from experience. Accumulate enough experience and competence follows.**

The evidence shows: Expertise comes from deliberate practice — a specific form of experience that requires attention to errors, feedback loops, and consistent effort to extend beyond current competence. In 'Only perfect practice makes perfect' (2016), Indranil Chakraborty deployed Ericsson's research to make the point that unconscious competence — the automation of skill through repetition — is not expertise. It is the capacity to perform a known task without thinking. Under novel conditions, it fails. The senior manager who has been "doing this for twenty years" may have one year of real learning repeated twenty times. The deliberate practitioner who has been doing it for five years with consistent attention to feedback, regular reflection on errors, and disciplined effort to expand their range will outperform them. Experience is not the variable. Quality of attention to experience is.

**The orthodoxy says: Learning is for early career. Experienced leaders develop through doing, not through formal study.**

The evidence shows: The learning that most matters for senior leaders is the learning they are most reluctant to do — the kind that requires admitting ignorance, engaging with unfamiliar disciplines, revising long-held mental models. In 'The Learned Life' (2015), KV Kamath framed this not as aspiration but as survival: "Else, I fear rapid change will make me obsolete." John Gardner's 1990 McKinsey lecture, cited in 'WFH Daily #124: Personal renewal' (2020), made the same point: "There's a myth that learning is for young people. It's what you learn after you know it all that counts." The barnacle metaphor — people who run out of steam in midcareer because they stopped engaging with what challenges them — is not a description of lazy people. It is a description of what happens to competent people who believe that their accumulated expertise exempts them from the discipline of continuing to learn.

**The Entrepreneurial Mindset Beyond Startups**

The entrepreneurial approach to learning is not primarily about startups. It is about a posture toward uncertainty, a willingness to act without complete information, and an orientation toward learning from experience that differs fundamentally from the institutional approach.

Saras Sarasvathy's effectuation framework, cited in 'How entrepreneurs learn' (2019), captures the distinction precisely. Causal reasoning — the dominant model in management education — starts with a predetermined goal and works backward to the means required. Effectuation starts with available means and asks what goals are achievable from here. The entrepreneur's learning is embedded in action: you try something, observe what happens, update your understanding, try something different. This is not a method for startups only. It is a description of how learning actually works under uncertainty, which is where most of the interesting problems in Indian business and public life actually live.

The implications for large organisations are significant and underexplored in Indian management practice. The organisation that has committed to a five-year strategy and a competency framework built to execute it has, in effect, decided in advance what it needs to learn. That decision will be wrong in proportion to how uncertain the environment is. The entrepreneurial approach — building learning capacity at the level of the team, treating unexpected results as data rather than failure, creating feedback loops tight enough to update strategy in real time — is available to any organisation that chooses it. Most don't, because it requires tolerating a level of ambiguity that institutional processes are designed to eliminate.

For policymakers, the entrepreneurial mindset applied to education means something specific: Atul Gawande's "positive deviance" model, described in 'FF Daily #393' (2021). Instead of importing a solution and implementing it top-down, positive deviance asks: where is the problem already being solved, by people inside the system, using resources the system already has? The hospital hand-washing case study — where the change that worked was not a new protocol imposed from outside but a practice that some nurses were already doing, identified, described, and spread — is a model for education reform that Indian policymakers almost never use. Pratham's "teaching at the right level" approach is a version of this: it works because it builds on what teachers can actually do in contexts that are actually present, rather than on a vision of what education should look like in an ideal system.

The Shankar Mahadevan Academy example, cited in 'FF Life: The Best of #Beacon2022' (2022), extends the entrepreneurial learning model into the arts. Thirty-five thousand students, ten thousand in eighty-four countries, one-third of total signups arriving post-pandemic. The constraint that Shankar Mahadevan Academy identified was not technology — the platform was buildable. It was teachers. Finding good teachers is harder than building the platform. This is the entrepreneurial insight: the resource that actually constrains scaling is not the one that looks scarce from the outside.

Haresh Chawla's account of his IIM Bangalore session, reported in 'Working From Home Daily #99: First principles thinking' (2020), frames the meta-skill in terms that apply to any practitioner: "They have to learn to be curious. They have to learn to create mental models of the world." An MBA student who finishes the programme knowing what to think about a set of case studies is not ready for what follows. An MBA student who finishes knowing how to learn — how to encounter an unfamiliar problem, form a hypothesis, test it, update — is prepared for something the programme could not anticipate. The entrepreneurial mindset is not about risk appetite or innovation frameworks. It is about maintaining, through formal education and organisational life, the posture of the learner rather than the expert.

**On Transformation: Process, Not Just Destination**

Change in learning — whether in an individual, an organisation, or a system — does not happen in the way that most change narratives describe it. It does not happen through epiphanies, programme launches, or policy announcements. FF's reporting, across a decade, describes a different mechanism.

The first element is time. Daniel Coyle's research on habit formation, cited in 'FF Insights #575: Don't give up yet' (2022), identified eight weeks as an important threshold — before which most people abandon new practices because they don't feel immediate improvement. Neural circuitry takes time to rewire. The person who tries a new reading practice for two weeks, doesn't feel smarter, and stops, has not given the intervention a chance to work. This is not a motivational failure. It is a cognitive misunderstanding about how the brain learns. George Leonard's formulation, from 'FF Insights #593: Plateauing out matters much' (2022), is the clearest description of what the process actually feels like: most of a learning journey is spent on the plateau, where nothing seems to be changing, where feedback is not dramatic, where the practitioner cannot feel the accumulation of competence. Our institutions — schools, business schools, corporate L&D programmes — are almost universally calibrated to produce visible, rapid, measurable progress. They are thus almost universally calibrated against the actual mechanism of mastery.

The second element is feedback. In 'FF Daily #394: How to become top-class' (2021), Founding Fuel stated the point precisely: "Hours and hours of practice are necessary for great performance, but not sufficient. Smart practice always includes a feedback loop that lets you recognize errors and correct them." The feedback loop is not an optional addition to a learning programme. It is the mechanism of learning. Practice without feedback is repetition. Repetition produces automation, not expertise. The Indian educational system — at every level, from the village government school to the corporate leadership programme — is designed around one-directional information transfer, not feedback loops. The teacher or the facilitator delivers content. The learner is expected to absorb it. The question of whether absorption has occurred, and what errors in understanding need to be corrected, is addressed (if at all) by an examination weeks or months later, when it is too late to course-correct.

The third element is psychological safety at the level of the individual learner. In 'FF Daily #297: Why mistakes matter' (2021), Founding Fuel quoted Daniel Dennett: "Many of the students who arrive at very competitive universities pride themselves in not making mistakes. I often find that I have to encourage them to cultivate the habit of making mistakes, the best learning opportunities of all." The transformation of learning culture in an organisation or an institution begins here: not with a new curriculum, not with a new platform, not with a new policy, but with a change in the social meaning of error. In India, as Parul Gupta noted, the fixed mindset is socially ingrained from childhood. Transforming this is not a training intervention. It is a cultural project, and it requires consistent, deliberate, years-long effort from leaders who model the behaviour themselves.

IIM-A's founding principles illustrate what systemic transformation looks like when it works. The evidence from 'Untold stories about how India built its higher ed institutions' (2021) is that the institution was built around an inviolable principle — that faculty must research, consult, and teach, without exception — and that this principle was enforced at the cost of convenience, including for senior people. The Nirma/HUL consulting case feeding back into the classroom is not an anecdote about one professor. It is a description of how the system was designed: practice and theory in continuous dialogue, neither upstream of the other. That design was deliberate. It was also fragile — it required leadership willing to enforce the principle against institutional inertia, every time. The transformation of India's higher education institutions in the decades since has often involved the erosion of exactly this kind of principled design, replaced by institutional comfort and ranking optimization.

The SSISM case from Sandalpur, reported by Krishnan in 'Idealistic, innovative entrepreneurship has the potential to change Indian higher education' (2016), is a different kind of transformation story. A privately-built, idealistic college in rural Madhya Pradesh, fourteen girl students winning university ranks, graduates employed at Cognizant, Infosys, and SAP. The mechanism here is not scale, not technology, not policy. It is the sustained commitment of people who believed that the problem was solvable at the grassroots and were willing to work on it without institutional support. These transformations are real. They are also underreported, underfunded, and difficult to replicate without the specific human commitment that made them possible.

**The Human and Cultural Dimension**

The factors that most influence whether learning happens — in an individual or in an organisation — are almost entirely invisible to the frameworks that dominate education policy and management practice.

Begin with the teacher. In 'To the teachers who made us' (2021), Founding Fuel put the point that most education reform misses: "It's often less about how much content and concepts, and more about how they make you feel about yourself." The teacher who communicates, implicitly or explicitly, that the student is capable — that the difficulty of the material is a challenge to be met, not evidence of a fixed limit — changes the trajectory of a learner's development in ways that no curriculum reform can replicate. Jaime Escalante's story, cited in 'WFH Daily #122: Practise physical, not social, distancing' (2020), is the canonical case: inner-city Hispanic students learning college-level calculus because their teacher asked "How can I teach them?" not "Can I teach them?" The question reveals the theory, and the theory determines the outcome.

The mentor relationship operates by the same logic, but with higher stakes for adult learners. In 'WFH Daily #170: To achieve mastery you need tough love' (2020), Founding Fuel quoted Robert Greene on what genuine mentorship requires: "In this day and age, you must get the sharpest dose of reality that is possible from your mentor." The mentor who protects the mentee from difficult feedback is not mentoring. They are managing a relationship. The feedback that changes development is the feedback that is accurate about failure, specific about what needs to change, and delivered in a context of genuine belief in the learner's capacity to change it. This kind of feedback is rare in Indian organisational culture, where hierarchy and deference consistently filter out the signals that leaders most need. The practitioner who wants to develop needs to actively seek conditions in which honest feedback is possible — which often means going outside the hierarchy.

The emotional dimension of learning is where Indian management practice is most systematically blind. Carol Dweck's entity versus incremental research, reported in 'FF Daily #506: Incremental learning' (2021), shows that the children most at risk of becoming helpless under pressure are the ones labeled as "smart" — precisely because they have invested their identity in the performance of intelligence rather than in the practice of learning. The senior executive who has built their reputation on expertise is in an analogous position. Engaging genuinely with a domain in which they are a beginner — which is what the pace of change now regularly requires — threatens the identity that their career has been built around. This is not a motivational problem. It is a deeper problem of self-concept. Adam Grant's work on impostor syndrome, cited in 'Succeeding in the workplace: What I learnt from Adam Grant' (2021), reframes this: the discomfort of not knowing can be fuel for growth rather than evidence of inadequacy, but only if the person has been taught to read it that way.

The flow state research, introduced through Csikszentmihalyi in 'FF Daily #265: How often do you experience a state of flow?' (2021), describes the human experience of learning at its best: complete absorption, loss of self-consciousness, a sense of operating at the edge of competence. Surgeons describe it. Musicians describe it. Athletes describe it. It is, as Csikszentmihalyi argued, available to almost anyone engaged in a task that is calibrated to the right level of difficulty — not so easy as to be boring, not so hard as to be paralyzing. Most Indian educational and organisational contexts are calibrated in exactly the wrong way: too easy for the talented, too hard for the unprepared, with almost nothing in between. The factory model of education, which processes everyone at the same pace through the same material, is structurally incapable of producing flow.

The cultural dimension specific to India is the weight of social expectation on the learning choices of individuals. The coaching class ecosystem that Charles Assisi navigated for his daughter, described in 'How to think about coaching classes' (2021), is not a failure of the market or the state. It is a rational response to a system of scarcity — brutal competition for a small number of IIT and NEET seats — in which the social cost of failure is very high and the social reward for the credential is very real. Understanding this does not require celebrating it. But it does require acknowledging that the human choices being made inside this system are not irrational. They are adaptive responses to the actual incentive structure. Changing the learning culture requires changing the incentive structure. Moralizing about coaching classes while leaving the examination system intact is not education reform.

**The Systems View**

Learning and education, as FF covers them, are never standalone phenomena. They are embedded in — and constitutive of — every other system that matters for Indian development.

The most fundamental systems connection is between education and economic growth, but not in the linear way that policy documents usually describe it. The standard narrative is: invest in education, produce skilled workers, grow the economy. FF's reporting suggests the relationship is more complex and more fragile. In 'An engineering college boom built on a foundation of dysfunction' (2021), M. Rajshekhar described an educational system that had been colonized by the logic of land conversion and black money laundering — not the logic of human development. The engineering college that exists to generate regulatory arbitrage and not to educate engineers is not a failure of education policy in isolation. It is a symptom of the interaction between weak regulatory enforcement, poorly designed incentives, and the specific economics of a mineral boom period. You cannot fix the education system without fixing the political economy that surrounds it.

The systems connection between education and language runs deeper than is commonly acknowledged. In 'The Love of One's Tongue in the Age of AI' (2025), Kavi Arasu documented the risk that AI development creates for India's linguistic diversity: 22 scheduled languages, over 780 indigenous tongues, dozens at risk of losing the data infrastructure that would allow them to persist in a world where AI mediates an increasing share of communication and knowledge work. The Inglehart-Welzel Cultural Map analysis is pointed: every major large language model clusters in the Protestant Europe/English-speaking quadrant. They can speak 35 languages but they think in one. For India, this is not an abstract cultural concern. It is a concrete learning access problem: if the AI systems that will increasingly mediate education, employment, and civic life are calibrated to one cultural-linguistic paradigm, the billion Indians who think, feel, and dream in other languages are not just culturally underserved. They are cognitively excluded.

The connection between education and public health is another systems relationship that appears frequently in FF's reporting. In 'FF Daily #287: What's your learning style?' (2021), Founding Fuel noted plainly that poor levels of health hurt India's growth prospects severely. This is not metaphor. Stunting, anemia, and the cognitive effects of chronic childhood malnutrition directly impair learning capacity. The school reform that proceeds without addressing the nutritional status of the children in the schools is operating on a system in which a crucial variable has been left out of the model. Similarly, the mental health crisis among students — visible in the coaching class phenomenon, in the pressure around examinations, in the dropout rates — is a public health problem that sits upstream of educational outcomes.

The chess ecosystem in Chennai, documented in 'FF Insights #706: Chennai's chess pioneers' (2022), is a microcosm of what the systems view reveals about learning infrastructure. Manuel Aaron to Viswanathan Anand to twenty-six Tamil Nadu grandmasters is not a story about individual talent. It is a story about inspiration cascades — how one person's achievement changes the belief system of an entire generation, which changes the willingness to invest, which changes the quantity of training, which changes the probability of the next achievement. Every World Championship by Anand triggered a new cohort of young players who now believed that world-class performance was achievable by someone from Tamil Nadu. The system was self-reinforcing. The lesson is not that chess is special. It is that every learning ecosystem depends on visible proof points that achievement is possible. India has produced those proof points in scattered domains — chess, mathematics through Ramanujan and then Chandrasekhar's observation that his work inspired a generation, software engineering through the IIT diaspora — but has not systematically built the infrastructure to convert them into cascades.

The policy system connects to the learning system through the New Education Policy, which Founding Fuel assessed carefully rather than celebratorily. Meeta Sengupta's analysis in 'WFH Daily #130: What the New Education Policy gets right' (2020) identified the credit flexibility provision — allowing students to step away from higher education and return without losing progress — as genuinely radical. Life disrupts learning. A system that treats any disruption as a catastrophic penalty is a system that is working against the interests of the people it is supposed to serve. The NEP's acknowledgment that the pipeline model has damaged enormous human potential is, at the level of diagnosis, correct. Whether the implementation machinery exists to convert that diagnosis into changed practice is a different question, and FF's coverage has been appropriately skeptical about institutional capacity to translate policy intent into ground reality.

The AI-education system interaction is the most rapidly evolving of these connections, and FF has tracked it carefully. In 'AI in Schools: Not a Generational Divide, but a Dialogue Waiting to Happen' (2025), Ipsita Bandyopadhyay and Ishani Ray reported the student who asked why they should wait a year for a teacher to cover material when ChatGPT gives the answer in seconds. This is not a question about technology. It is a question about the theory of the teacher's role. If the teacher is primarily an information delivery mechanism, they are already outcompeted. If the teacher is a guide for thinking, a mirror for self-understanding, a model of the intellectual life, they are not replaceable by any AI that currently exists. The systems question is whether Indian educational institutions have the pedagogical culture to make that transition — from information delivery to thinking cultivation — before the information delivery function is automated away.

**The Evolution of FF's Thinking**

FF's earliest articles on learning, from 2015, are marked by a kind of programmatic optimism about what deliberate attention to learning could achieve. 'The Learned Life' (2015) is organised around KV Kamath as exemplar — the senior practitioner who has maintained a learning discipline across decades and attributes his continued relevance to it. The frameworks invoked — deliberate practice, curiosity, question-asking — are presented as tools available to anyone willing to use them. The tone is enabling.

By 2017-2018, the reporting had acquired a sharper critical edge. 'Performance monkeys on the TED stage' (2017) was not a gentle critique. It was an angry one. The TED Talk culture was being celebrated in the Indian management conversation as democratization of knowledge. FF called it shallow performance. 'How business schools in India have killed off research' (2018) was similarly pointed — not a nuanced assessment of a complex problem, but a direct accusation of institutional failure. The gap between academic research and management practice, the brain drain of management scholars to Western institutions, the vanity metrics of placement records — all of these appeared in the 2017-2018 period as subjects of direct criticism, not polite concern.

The COVID-19 period, 2020-2021, shifted the register again. The pandemic forced a confrontation with questions that had been theoretical — what is the minimum infrastructure required for learning to happen? What is lost when the physical encounter between teacher and student is removed? What does the digital divide actually mean in practice, not in policy documents? FF's response was to go very close to the ground: the Jharkhand Vice-Chancellor on districts without roads and electricity, the alternative school parents whose pedagogical burden was transferred onto them overnight, the sixty thousand children who dropped out in Andhra Pradesh. The tone in this period is more sorrowful than angry, more diagnostic than prescriptive. The optimism of the early articles about what deliberate learning practice could achieve sits uncomfortably alongside the scale of what the pandemic revealed about systemic neglect.

The 2021-2022 period introduced a deeper engagement with the inner life of the learner — flow states, vocations, the plateau, multiple identities, the paradox of potential. This is FF's most philosophically rich period on this theme. The George Leonard material on loving the plateau, the Csikszentmihalyi material on passive consumption as parasites of the mind, the Deresiewicz material on elite students as stem cells whose possibility paradoxically becomes limitation — these are arguments about what learning is for, not just how to do it better. FF had been primarily interested in the how. By 2021-2022, it was increasingly interested in the why.

The most recent period, 2022-2026, is characterised by two new concerns that were not present in the early articles. The first is AI — not primarily as a learning tool (though that appears), but as a force that is changing the question of what human learning is for. In 'AI in Academia: The Grind, the Gain, and the Great Recalibration' (2025), Kavi Arasu articulated the central challenge: if AI can do the knowing, what is left for humans to do? The answer FF has moved toward is consistent with its earliest articles — thinking, not knowing — but the urgency is greater and the implications more structural. The second new concern is linguistic and cultural diversity. The risk that AI monoculture poses to India's linguistic ecosystem, documented in 'The Love of One's Tongue in the Age of AI' (2025), was not on the agenda at all in 2015. It is now one of the most important systemic risks in the learning and education domain.

What FF believed in 2015 that it no longer holds: the implicit faith that the right individual practices — deliberate practice, reading, curiosity, question-asking — are sufficient responses to the learning challenge. By 2025, the coverage is more insistent that individual practice occurs within systems, and that the systems are powerful enough to defeat individual intention if they are not also addressed. The practitioner who reads and reflects and practices deliberately can develop themselves. But they operate within organisations, institutions, and cultures that may be actively working against the kind of learning that matters. The individual response is necessary. It is not sufficient.

**What FF Is Skeptical Of**

FF is skeptical of the credential as a proxy for capability. The B-school placement rate, the AICTE-approved degree, the ISO-certified training programme — all of these are measures of process compliance, not of whether anything valuable has been learned. In 'The b-school campus placement BS—and how to clean it up' (2018), Indrajit Gupta documented the 70% first-year attrition rate that follows India's celebrated "100% placement" headlines. The credential is a social signal. It is not a learning outcome. The organisations and individuals who confuse the signal for the substance end up optimizing for the wrong thing, and paying the price later.

FF is skeptical of technology as a learning solution rather than a learning enabler. The reflexive turn to digital platforms as answers to educational problems — which was already visible in the MOOC enthusiasm of 2016 and became explicit policy during the pandemic — ignores the fact that the pedagogical problem is not solved by changing the delivery mechanism. As 'Unplanned online education is short-changing our children' (2021) documented, the problem with the pandemic's online schooling was not that it used screens. It was that it used screens to replicate bad pedagogy at scale. Technology that delivers rote learning faster is not an improvement on rote learning.

FF is skeptical of the wisdom conveyed in easily digestible formats. 'Performance monkeys on the TED stage' (2017) made this argument forcefully about TED Talks. The same skepticism extends to summary apps, business book digests, and the entire productivity-content industrial complex that has grown up around the idea that learning can be made fast and painless. In 'WFH Daily #167: FF Recommends science movies to binge watch' (2020), the Polish dancer story made the point through contrast: the city that produced great dancers focused on one thing per lesson, drilled to depth. The city that produced bad dancers offered "delicious lessons with tons of new information." Breadth without depth is not learning. It is the performance of curiosity.

FF is skeptical of purpose-washing in the corporate learning context. In 'Do leadership books matter?' (2022), D. Shivakumar's observation that "90% of CEOs don't know what they're talking about" on purpose was reported with evident approval. The learning and development industry has enthusiastically adopted purpose as a framework — purpose-led organisations, purpose-driven leadership, purpose as the engine of employee engagement. FF's view is that this is often the language of engagement for people in boring jobs, not a genuine organisational commitment to meaningful work. The distinction matters because the learning design is different: purpose-washing produces programmes about articulating purpose; genuine purpose produces conditions in which meaningful work can happen and learning is embedded in it.

FF is skeptical of psychometric tools as reliable filters for talent or leadership potential. The Ramkumar-Kamath experiment (fifty validated leaders, one selected by psychometrics) is the empirical core of this skepticism. The deeper critique is that the HR function, in India as elsewhere, has invested heavily in tools that produce the appearance of rigour without the substance. The appearance of rigour is valuable to the HR function as a defence against criticism. It does not select for leadership capacity.

**What FF Celebrates**

FF celebrates learning that is embedded in real work, with real stakes, and real feedback. The Asian Paints model — six focused programmes linked to business outcomes, the annual training calendar disbanded — is celebrated precisely because it treats the connection between learning and performance as non-negotiable. The IIM-A founding model — research, consulting, and teaching as inseparable, enforced without exception — is celebrated for the same reason. Learning in these models is not a retreat from work. It is a more rigorous engagement with it.

FF celebrates curiosity as a disposition and a discipline, not a personality trait. Ian Leslie's distinction between diversive curiosity (the itch that begins the hunt) and epistemic curiosity (the deepening into directed understanding) captures what FF admires: not the person who is "curious about everything" in a shallow way, but the person who has learned to follow a question past the first answer, to resist the satisfaction of a plausible explanation, to stay with difficulty until something genuinely new emerges. The Titanic example from 'FF Daily #386: The power of curiosity' (2021) — planners who had questions about safety and didn't raise them for fear of appearing foolish — is the cautionary countercase. Curiosity suppressed is not neutral. It has consequences.

FF celebrates the deliberate practitioner. Benjamin Franklin's writing practice — cited twice across the batch, in 'Only perfect practice makes perfect' (2016) and 'The Beatles, and mastery through deliberate practice' (2018) — is the canonical example. The Beatles in Hamburg. Anand in Chennai. Ramanujan in Kumbakonam. What these cases share is not prodigious talent acting without effort. It is sustained, intentional practice with attention to the gap between current performance and the standard being aimed at. FF celebrates this across domains: in music, in chess, in mathematics, in writing, in management practice. The common thread is not the domain. It is the quality of attention brought to the practice.

FF celebrates institution-builders who hold principles inviolate against convenience. The IITs, IIMs, IISc, IRMA, TIFR — documented in 'Untold stories about how India built its higher ed institutions' (2021) as cases where "we did some things right back in the 50s, 60s, 70s and 80s" — are celebrated for the courage of their founders in refusing to compromise the founding principles when institutional pressure demanded it. The SSISM case is celebrated for the same reason, at a different scale: the privately-built college in rural Madhya Pradesh that produced graduates in Cognizant and Infosys not because of resources but because of commitment.

FF celebrates the thinker who destroys their own best ideas. Charlie Munger's maxim — "Any year that passes in which you don't destroy one of your best loved ideas is a wasted year" — appears in 'FF Daily #481: Creative destruction for the self' (2021) as the standard against which intellectual honesty is measured. The practitioner who has been saying the same things for fifteen years is not accumulating wisdom. They are accumulating barnacles. FF admires the person willing to do the uncomfortable work of revising.

**The Historical Lens**

FF uses history on the learning and education theme in two distinct ways. The first is as a source of models — individuals and institutions that demonstrate what is possible when the right conditions are created. The second is as a diagnostic lens — to understand why current Indian institutions are the way they are, by tracing the choices and compromises that produced them.

Ramanujan is the recurring individual archetype. In 'FF Daily #261: S Ramanujan, the man who continues to inspire' (2020), Chandrasekhar's observation was cited: almost all the mathematicians who reached distinction in the decades following Ramanujan were directly or indirectly inspired by his example. The historical function Ramanujan serves in FF's narrative is as proof that world-class original intellectual achievement is possible from within India, from a "scientifically sterile atmosphere," without the institutional support that Western models assume. He is also a cautionary case: the conditions that produced him were not reproducible by design, and the waste of talent that his near-miss with obscurity represents — he very nearly died unknown — is as important as the inspiration of his recognition.

The IIT/IIM founding generation serves a different historical function. In 'Untold stories about how India built its higher ed institutions' (2021), FF asked an uncomfortable question: if India built these institutions well in the 1950s-1980s, what happened? The historical answer is about the erosion of founding principles under institutional pressure — the drift from research-consulting-teaching integration toward ranking optimization and placement performance, the political pressures that compromised academic autonomy, the brain drain that removed the most intellectually ambitious faculty to Western institutions. The history is not nostalgic. It is diagnostic: these institutions worked when certain conditions held, and understanding what those conditions were is the first step toward recreating them.

The chess ecosystem history — Manuel Aaron teaching Viswanathan Anand, Anand inspiring a generation of Tamil Nadu players, twenty-six grandmasters following — is the clearest historical demonstration of the inspiration cascade mechanism. Each achievement changed the belief system of the next cohort. The mechanism is not mysterious. But it requires, as a precondition, that the initial achievement is visible and legible to the people who need to be inspired by it. India has produced extraordinary achievements in mathematics, science, literature, and sport that have not generated cascades, partly because they were not made visible in ways that the next generation could receive. The historical lesson is about the infrastructure of inspiration, not just the fact of achievement.

The global historical lens appears in FF's deployment of Benjamin Franklin as the canonical deliberate practice case. Franklin did not have access to great teachers. He taught himself to write by reading good essays, putting them aside, reconstructing them from memory, comparing his version to the original, identifying the gaps, and correcting them. This is the self-directed learning model that Susan Wise Bauer's work, cited in 'FF Insights #678: Teaching yourself' (2022), frames as an ancient tradition — "for centuries, women and men undertook this sort of learning without needing institutional certification." Thomas Jefferson's advice to his nephew is the same tradition. The historical point is that the association of learning with institutions is recent, and its dominance is a historical contingency, not a necessity.

**Unresolved Tensions**

The deepest unresolved tension in FF's coverage is between the individual agency thesis and the systems constraint thesis. The individual agency thesis — that deliberate learning practice, curiosity, reading, reflection, and self-direction can produce meaningful development regardless of institutional context — runs through almost every article on personal learning practice. The systems constraint thesis — that the institutional, cultural, and structural environment can defeat individual intention — runs through almost every article on Indian education at the systemic level. Both are true. FF holds them both. But they point in different directions for the practitioner, and FF has not resolved this.

The practitioner who takes the individual agency thesis seriously will invest in their own reading practice, seek out mentors who give honest feedback, cultivate curiosity as a discipline, and practice deliberately at skills they want to build. This is good advice. It is also advice that works better if you went to a good school, have access to quality reading material, work in an organization that tolerates intellectual risk-taking, and were not subjected to a decade of examination culture that beat the growth mindset out of you. The structural conditions are not irrelevant. But acknowledging their weight risks sliding into fatalism. FF navigates this tension by holding both, but never quite resolving which should have priority when they conflict.

The second unresolved tension is between depth and breadth in learning. FF is consistently and firmly on the side of depth: the Polish dancer story, the deliberate practice framework, the critique of the TED Talk format, the Shivakumar criterion for a good book (it must change how you think about something). But FF also celebrates multidisciplinary thinking — Peter Kaufman's three buckets, Munger's mental models, Leslie's "thinkering" as a cognitive style that mixes concrete and abstract. Depth in one domain and breadth across domains are not obviously compatible as learning strategies for a practitioner with finite time. FF gestures toward the synthesis but does not specify it.

The third unresolved tension is around the relationship between formal credentials and actual capability. FF is clearly skeptical of the credential as a proxy for learning. But it also celebrated the founding of Ashoka University and ISB as legitimate alternatives to foreign education, the IIT diploma as a signal of intellectual capacity, and the SSISM graduates' placements at Cognizant and Infosys as evidence that the educational intervention worked. The credential matters and doesn't matter simultaneously, and FF's coverage reflects genuine ambiguity about when to treat it as meaningful and when to treat it as vanity.

The fourth unresolved tension is around AI. FF's recent coverage has moved toward the position that AI changes the question of what human learning is for — if machines can do the knowing, humans need to focus on thinking, judgment, and wisdom. But the same coverage acknowledges that the Indian educational system is not currently producing thinking, judgment, and wisdom. It is producing memorization. The transition from memorization to thinking requires a wholesale change in pedagogical culture, which requires teacher development at massive scale, which requires the very institutional capacity that FF's earlier reporting showed was absent or degraded. The AI opportunity and the institutional incapacity exist simultaneously. Which one dominates the next decade is genuinely uncertain, and FF has not resolved it.

**The Missing Voices**

The most significant absence from FF's learning and education coverage is the voice of the student, particularly the student at the lower end of the economic and geographic spectrum. FF has reported extensively about students in crisis — the children who dropped out in Andhra Pradesh, the rural students without internet access, the children of alternative-school parents who were let down by the online schooling pivot. These are important reports. But they are reports about students, not by them. What students themselves think about what they are learning, why they are learning it, what they find valuable and what they find useless, is almost entirely absent from FF's coverage. This absence is significant because the most powerful evidence for what works in learning often comes from the learner's own account of their experience.

The primary school teacher is another missing voice. FF covers education at the level of policy, institutional design, and individual leadership practice. The government schoolteacher in a Jharkhand village or a Bihar district — the person whose daily choices determine what actually happens to the largest number of learners in the country — is present only as a structural problem (too many, too untrained, too absent) or occasionally as an anecdote. The pedagogical wisdom that experienced teachers have developed, often entirely through their own reflection and self-directed learning, in conditions of near-total institutional abandonment, would be a significant addition to the analysis.

Vocational education is substantially absent. The ITI system, the apprenticeship programmes, the informal skill development that happens in workshops and on construction sites and in agricultural communities — this is where the majority of Indian learning happens, in the sense of learning that produces economically productive capability. FF's coverage is concentrated on formal education (schools, colleges, B-schools) and individual professional development (reading, deliberate practice). The learning that happens in the informal economy, which is where most Indians work, is almost entirely outside the frame.

Women's experience of education and learning in India, beyond the specific angles of STEM representation (the Reshma Saujani material) and school dropout rates, is underrepresented. The specific ways in which the learning and credentialing system interacts with the labour market for women, the ways in which the fixed mindset is gendered in India, the specific barriers that women face in accessing the kind of deliberate practice environments that produce expertise — these are present in fragments but not as a sustained analytical thread.

The Dalit and Adivasi experience of the educational system is another gap. The constitutional literacy workshops facilitated by We The People Abhiyan, cited in 'We, the People of India' (2025), appear as a single data point. The experience of first-generation learners navigating institutions designed for and by a different social class, the ways in which caste operates as a hidden variable in educational outcomes, the specific forms of institutional violence that the formal educational system visits on students from marginalized communities — these are not absent from Indian public discourse on education, but they are largely absent from FF's coverage of this theme.

**What This Means For You**

The belief you are most likely carrying that this evidence says is wrong: that improving learning in your organisation or your own practice is primarily about finding better content. Better courses, better speakers, better reading lists, better apps. FF's entire body of reporting on this theme points to the same conclusion: content is not the constraint. The constraint is the quality of attention brought to practice, the presence of honest feedback loops, the willingness to sit with difficulty long enough for genuine learning to happen, and the cultural conditions that make it safe to not know. You are probably investing in content when you should be investing in conditions.

The thing to stop: treating training programmes as a learning response. Whether you are designing them as an L&D leader, commissioning them as a business leader, or attending them as a practitioner — the standard corporate training programme, delivered offsite, disconnected from actual work, evaluated by attendance and satisfaction survey, is not producing what you think it is producing. The evidence from 'The end of training' (2015) is clear: the intervention that works is learning embedded in real work, with a feedback loop tight enough to catch errors and correct them before they become habits. The programme that works is the one designed around a specific business problem, where the learning and the doing are happening simultaneously. Stop spending money on rituals that produce the appearance of development. Start building the conditions for learning in the actual work.

The thing to start: building a feedback system for yourself that gives you accurate information about where your current understanding is wrong. This does not require a coach, a programme, or a budget. It requires choosing one domain in which your performance matters, identifying the gap between where you are and where you want to be, and creating a regular practice of testing your understanding against reality — through writing (which, as Paul Graham observed in 'FF Insights 710: Why writing matters' (2022), reveals the gaps in thinking that the speaker's fluency conceals), through deliberate practice with attention to errors, or through seeking out the person in your environment most likely to tell you what you don't want to hear and asking them for honest assessment. You do not need more information. You need better feedback on the information you already have.

The conviction to carry out of this page: in a world where the pace of change makes accumulated expertise fragile, the only durable competitive advantage — for a practitioner, an organisation, or a nation — is the capacity to learn faster than the environment changes. That capacity is not a personality trait, a talent, or a gift. It is a practice. And every day you don't practise it is a day you are falling behind.
