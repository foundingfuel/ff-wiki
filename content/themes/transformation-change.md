# Transformation & Change

---

## Part 1 — The Opening

Here is what is actually happening. Across India's boardrooms, government offices, and founder war rooms, the word "transformation" has become the most expensive ritual in business. Committees form. Consultants arrive with decks. A vision document gets laminated. And then, mostly, nothing changes — or worse, the wrong things change fast while the right things stay stuck.

The conventional understanding of transformation in India is borrowed wholesale from Western management orthodoxy: hire the right firm, install the right technology, cascade the right communication. The problem is that this model was designed for organisations with different institutional histories, different labour realities, different relationships between hierarchy and trust. It assumes that the main constraint is information or process. In India, the main constraint is almost always something else: the gap between what leaders say they want and what the organisation's culture will actually permit. The gap between the speed at which the top floor moves and the pace at which the middle absorbs. The gap between the transformation that gets announced and the transformation that gets done.

What makes this moment particularly dangerous is that the pace of external change has outrun the pace of internal adaptation — and most leaders know it but are reaching for the wrong tools. They are planning more, when they should be learning faster. They are bringing in outsiders, when the people who understand what is broken are already inside. They are optimising for speed, when the actual problem is depth.

This page is not about managing change. It is about understanding why transformation fails so predictably in India, and what the evidence says actually works — from Aadhaar to Bishan Bedi's Delhi cricket team, from Best Buy's turnaround to Persistent Systems' S-curve dilemma. What you cannot get elsewhere: a clear-eyed account of what Indian practitioners specifically get wrong, grounded in two decades of evidence, with no imported framework left unchallenged.

## Part 2 — Principles

---

### Principle 1

> Transformation that begins with a plan will fail; transformation that begins with a loop will not.

**What This Means & Why**
The instinct in any large change effort is to plan thoroughly before acting — to reduce uncertainty before committing. But planning in conditions of genuine uncertainty does not reduce it; it merely defers the encounter with reality. What actually works is a tight loop: do something small, learn from what happens, adjust, repeat. This is not agile-as-buzzword. It is an epistemological claim: in complex systems, knowledge is only acquired through action. The people who plan most are often the ones most surprised by outcomes.

**The India Reality**
India's institutional environment — where regulations shift, infrastructure is uneven, and stakeholder coalitions are fragile — makes detailed upfront planning not just inefficient but actively misleading. A plan that makes sense in February may be structurally obsolete by June. The organisations that have executed the most consequential transformations in India have done so by treating plans as hypotheses, not commitments.

**The Evidence**
In 'Detailed planning is passé. A do-learn-do approach works better' (2019), Founding Fuel examines Nandan Nilekani's construction of Aadhaar, noting that Nilekani "was an outsider in the government with all its pulls and pushes" and that "the predominant mood during much of Nilekani's time at the UIDAI was one of scepticism." His success is attributed not to a grand plan but to constant feedback from multiple sources, willingness to learn publicly, and flexibility to adapt at each stage — a do-learn-do cycle operating under relentless political and operational pressure.

**The Implication**
Before launching the next transformation initiative, identify the three smallest experiments that would tell you whether your core assumptions are correct, and run them in the next thirty days before writing any further documentation.

### Principle 2

> The people who already work here know more about what is broken than any consultant you can hire.

**What This Means & Why**
Organisations in transformation almost reflexively reach for outside expertise — it signals seriousness, imports credibility, and diffuses internal political risk. But outside consultants, however talented, arrive with the knowledge base of their previous clients, not of this organisation. They cannot see the informal networks, the hidden workarounds, the places where the official process and the real process have been quietly diverging for years. The people who work inside the organisation carry exactly that knowledge. The transformation question is not whether to use it — it is whether leaders have built the conditions for insiders to use it honestly.

**The India Reality**
In India, hierarchy suppresses upward honesty with particular efficiency. The culture of deference — especially to founders, to seniors, to external "experts" — means that the diagnosis that arrives at the top of the organisation has already been filtered, softened, and politically adjusted multiple times. The consultant's deck often simply confirms what leadership already wanted to believe, presented in a format that makes it unchallengeable.

**The Evidence**
In 'Play to your strengths' (2019), Founding Fuel cites Behnam Tabrizi's research in HBR, which found that organisations seeking transformations "frequently bring in an army of outside consultants who tend to apply one-size-fits-all solutions in the name of 'best practices'," and argues explicitly that the alternative — relying on insiders with intimate knowledge of what works and what does not in daily operations — produces better outcomes. The piece frames this as a structural argument, not a cost argument.

**The Implication**
Convene a structured session with frontline managers and individual contributors — without their direct supervisors present — and ask them to name the three things that would need to change for this transformation to actually work.

### Principle 3

> The crisis arrives suddenly. The signals were ordinary and continuous.

**What This Means & Why**
Leaders scan for dramatic warnings — the shocking quarter, the competitor's breakthrough product, the analyst downgrade. What they miss is the slow drift: the gradual erosion of culture, the steady decline in a metric that nobody is watching, the small workarounds that have quietly become the real process. By the time the disruption is visible and dramatic, the structural conditions that made it inevitable have been in place for years. The Hemingway formulation — "gradually, then suddenly" — is not a poetic description. It is an operational warning.

**The India Reality**
India's business culture rewards responsiveness to acute crises and systematically underinvests in monitoring slow drift. Quarterly cycles, public market pressures, and the speed of startup competition all bias attention toward the sharp and the immediate. The slow signals — a subtle shift in customer behaviour in tier-2 cities, a creeping change in how middle management is communicating with the front line — are rarely anyone's formal responsibility to track.

**The Evidence**
In 'Spot change as it happens gradually. Or get swept away suddenly' (2019), Kavi Arasu writes: "When we miss seeing the small shifts that happen by the passing minute, we get presented with the big disruption one fine day! That's the thing about change. It happens in ordinary trickles, while most of us are busy looking for the spectacular waves." The piece argues that the skill of seeing change as it accumulates — not after it has arrived — is the foundational competence of leadership, and that it is systematically underdeveloped.

**The Implication**
Assign someone — not a committee, one person — the explicit responsibility to monitor three leading indicators of drift in your organisation, with a monthly report directly to the leadership team that is never filtered or summarised before it arrives.

### Principle 4

> Speed is a strategy. Hurry is a pathology. Confusing them is India's most expensive management mistake.

**What This Means & Why**
Urgency is real. The external environment genuinely demands faster response. But urgency applied to problems that require depth produces solutions that fail on contact with reality — and then require expensive remediation. The distinction between speed (acting decisively on what is known) and hurry (acting before adequate understanding is built) is not a philosophical nicety. It is the difference between transformation that holds and transformation that has to be done twice.

**The India Reality**
Vijay Kelkar and Ajay Shah have named this directly: "a cult of jald baazi is taking root in India" — the notion that a problem can be understood in a few days and solved in a few weeks. This is not merely a bureaucratic tendency. It pervades private sector transformation efforts, policy design, and startup execution with equal force. India's tolerance for the thoughtful, sustained effort that hard problems require has been compressed by competitive pressure into something that often produces neither speed nor quality.

**The Evidence**
In 'Working From Home Daily #77: Virtual teams and digital transformation' (2020), Founding Fuel quotes Kelkar and Shah on the "jald baazi" pathology, and separately cites Carl Honore's slow fix argument — that "we have to vaccinate ourselves against a pernicious virus called 'hurry', which constantly tempts us to reach for a 'quick fix'" — with Toyota's discipline of asking why five times as the counter-model. Both sources converge on the same claim: the problems that matter most are precisely the ones that resist the quick fix most stubbornly.

**The Implication**
For your single most important current transformation initiative, write down the minimum time genuinely required to test whether the core approach is working — and then defend that timeline explicitly in the next leadership conversation where someone asks you to compress it.

### Principle 5

> Your value statement is not a transformation tool. It is a cliché generator.

**What This Means & Why**
Corporate values processes produce language that is simultaneously true of every organisation and useful to none of them. "Integrity," "customer-centricity," "innovation" — these words do not differentiate because every organisation can claim them and most do. The problem is not cynicism or bad intentions. The problem is abstraction: when the language is abstract, every person in the organisation assigns their own meaning to it, and those meanings diverge precisely at the moments when alignment matters most — during a crisis, a difficult trade-off, a transformation decision.

**The India Reality**
India's large conglomerates and family-owned businesses have historically governed through relationship and founder authority rather than codified values. As these organisations professionalise and scale, they reach for values statements as a substitute for that informal governance — but import the global template without the cultural translation. The result is a document that signals professionalisation without delivering the alignment it promises.

**The Evidence**
In 'Get out of the clichés trap' (2015), Founding Fuel argues that "the words we use in describing our values are often abstract" and that "when faced with abstraction we assign our own meaning." The piece proposes a specific solution: move "table stake" terms like "integrity" out of the values statement entirely and into a Code of Conduct, then invest the values statement only in what is genuinely distinctive about how this organisation makes decisions. The point is not to write better values statements — it is to make the values statement do a different job than it currently does.

**The Implication**
Take your current values statement and, for each word, ask whether a direct competitor could use the exact same word in the exact same sentence — if the answer is yes, the word is doing no organisational work and should be removed or replaced.

### Principle 6

> Leadership transitions are the moment that reveals whether the transformation was real or theatrical.

**What This Means & Why**
Transformations are easy to sustain while the person who initiated them is still in the room with authority. The true test of whether the transformation has taken root in the organisation — in its culture, its processes, its decision logic — is whether it continues, adapts, and deepens when leadership changes. Organisations that have not genuinely transformed substitute succession planning for culture work and then are surprised when the new leader immediately starts dismantling what was built.

**The India Reality**
India's most consequential organisations — the Tata Group, large IT services firms, family-owned conglomerates — have repeatedly faced leadership transitions that became existential tests. The conflation of the founder's identity with the company's identity is particularly acute in Indian business culture, where the founder is often not just the leader but the living embodiment of the brand, the values, and the strategy. Disentangling those is not a governance exercise — it is a cultural surgery.

**The Evidence**
In 'The challenges before N Chandra' (2017), Indrajit Gupta examines N Chandrasekaran's appointment at Tata Sons, framing the challenge not primarily as strategic but cultural: whether the organisation's transformation under Mistry would hold, deepen, or reverse. The piece uses BCG's corporate centre parenting models to argue that Chandrasekaran's first decision — about how to govern the portfolio, not what to add or subtract — would signal more than any public statement about the direction of the transformation. The transition, not the transformation, was the test.

**The Implication**
Identify one specific decision that your organisation would make differently if you were not in the room, and treat that as the first item on your culture and governance agenda.

### Principle 7

> Agility is not a process you install. It is a culture you permit — and most cultures in India actively prohibit it.

**What This Means & Why**
The agile methodology has been adopted with enormous enthusiasm and implemented with almost universally disappointing results, because the methodology is the symptom of an underlying cultural condition that cannot be installed. Agility requires that people at every level of the organisation are genuinely permitted — not just theoretically allowed but actually safe — to surface bad news, contradict seniors, and abandon approaches that are not working. Command-and-control cultures that adopt scrum ceremonies remain command-and-control cultures. They just have daily standups.

**The India Reality**
India's organisational cultures are overwhelmingly high-power-distance. Juniors who surface bad news risk being seen as disloyal or incompetent. Middle managers who contradict strategy in public are rarely celebrated for intellectual honesty. The Mill condition — that "our identity as a person must be kept separable from ideas we endorse at a given time," so that changing your mind is not a loss of face — is precisely what Indian corporate culture most consistently violates.

**The Evidence**
In 'An inquiry into Agility' (2019), Founding Fuel argues, drawing on John Stuart Mill's On Liberty, that genuine agility requires tolerance for dissent as a structural condition: "Someone's controversial idea today may turn out to be right tomorrow." The piece uses ING and the Danish government's arms as live cases, but its deeper argument is that the cultural prerequisites for agility — the willingness to be publicly wrong, to reverse direction visibly, to separate identity from position — are precisely what most large organisations, and most Indian organisations in particular, have not built.

**The Implication**
In your next leadership team meeting, identify one decision made in the last quarter that turned out to be wrong, name what the correct decision would have been, and publicly credit whoever in the organisation was closest to seeing it first.

### Principle 8

> Founders asking "will the company be better off without me?" is not weakness. It is the most important strategic question they will ever answer.

**What This Means & Why**
The founder's dilemma in transformation is not primarily about equity or control — it is about whether the organisation is stuck at the top of one S-curve because the founder who built the capabilities for that curve cannot personally make the transition to the next one. The skills that build a company to a certain scale are genuinely different from the skills required to transform it to the next level, and founders who cannot acknowledge this become the primary constraint on the transformation they are nominally leading.

**The India Reality**
India's founder-driven corporate culture — where the founder is simultaneously the chief strategist, chief culture officer, chief relationship manager, and often the only person with real authority — creates a structural dependency that is hard to see from inside and almost impossible to challenge from outside. The organisations that navigate this well are rare precisely because the incentives all push in the wrong direction: the board defers to the founder, the team defers to the founder, and the founder's identity is so deeply fused with the company's identity that any challenge to the transition question feels like a personal attack.

**The Evidence**
In 'Anand Deshpande's founder's dilemma at Persistent' (2021), Founding Fuel reports that Deshpande genuinely grappled with the question: "'Will the company be better off with me or without me?'" — framing this not as a succession planning question but as a strategic clarity question. The piece uses the S-curve framework to argue that "companies go through these S-curves. You are at the top of the curve and the team is not ready to transition to the next S." FF frames Deshpande's willingness to hold this question openly, rather than resolving it with ego, as the quality that made his organisation's transformation possible.

**The Implication**
Write down — privately, before the next board meeting — the three capabilities your organisation's next phase genuinely requires, and honestly assess which of them you personally cannot provide.

### Principle 9

> Short-termism does not just damage strategy. It destroys the institutional infrastructure of transformation.

**What This Means & Why**
Transformation requires deep investment in leadership development, culture, and learning capability — investments whose returns are measured in years and whose costs are felt in the current quarter. Short-termism, driven by shareholder value obsession and quarterly reporting cycles, systematically disinvests from exactly the things that make sustained transformation possible. The result is organisations that are perpetually capable of announcing transformations and structurally incapable of completing them.

**The India Reality**
GE's Crotonville directly inspired a generation of Indian corporate universities — Unilever's Gulita, Tata's TMTC, Aditya Birla's Gyanodaya, SBI, Infosys, L&T, Bharat Petroleum. These institutions represented India's serious attempt to build the internal capability infrastructure that transformation requires. Their progressive defunding under short-term pressure is not a side note — it is a leading indicator of how India's large organisations are quietly destroying their own capacity for deep change while continuing to announce transformation programmes.

**The Evidence**
In 'Behind Crotonville's demise' (2024), Indrajit Gupta traces how the closure of GE's legendary leadership development centre was driven by "the short-termism that has gripped corporate America and its obsession with shareholder value creation," a virus that "has, in turn, percolated down to many markets around the world, including India." The piece frames the demise of Crotonville not as a cost-cutting decision but as a symptom of a deeper failure to understand what transformation actually requires to be sustained across generations of leadership.

**The Implication**
Calculate the total investment your organisation made in leadership development and internal capability-building in the last three years as a percentage of revenue, and compare it explicitly to your stated transformation ambition — the gap between those two numbers is your real transformation commitment.

### Principle 10

> Branding during transformation is not communication. It is organisational sense-making.

**What This Means & Why**
When organisations are in genuine transition, the brand is not primarily an external signal — it is an internal navigation instrument. It answers the question that every person in the organisation is actually asking: who are we now, and how do we make decisions in this new form? The organisations that treat branding during transformation as a cosmetic exercise — a new logo, a refreshed tagline, a campaign launch — miss the deeper function that branding serves in moments of genuine change: it makes the transition legible from the inside.

**The India Reality**
India's corporate transitions — from family to professional management, from domestic to global, from product to platform — are structurally more complex than the Western corporate transitions that most brand management frameworks were built around. The gap between what the organisation intends and what its employees and customers actually perceive is not a communications problem. It is an identity problem, and identity problems require different tools than communications problems.

**The Evidence**
In 'The Quiet Work of Branding' (2026), Indranil Gupta argues that "branding can no longer be cosmetic. It has to help organisations orient themselves before they can persuade others." The piece identifies the gap between intent and perception as "where much of today's branding work is unfolding" — a reframing that positions the brand as a diagnostic instrument for organisational clarity, not a broadcast instrument for external persuasion.

**The Implication**
Before the next external brand communication in the context of your transformation, ask whether your own employees — the people who have to live the new identity — can articulate what is genuinely different about who the organisation is now, and treat the answer as the primary brief.

### Principle 11

> The real constraints on transformation in India are not technological. They are institutional — and they sit inside your organisation as much as outside it.

**What This Means & Why**
India's transformation narrative is overwhelmingly framed as a technology adoption story — digital transformation, AI-readiness, platform plays. The actual constraint is almost never technology. The constraint is the institutional capacity to absorb, implement, and sustain change: the governance structures, the decision-making authority, the accountability systems, and the trust networks that determine whether a technology investment produces a real outcome or a sophisticated pilot that never scales.

**The India Reality**
Raghuram Rajan's diagnosis applies with equal force inside organisations and outside them: "where arguably we may have a long way to go is in the capacity of the government to deliver governance and public services." Replace "government" with any large Indian organisation and the sentence remains accurate. The pandemic made visible what was always there: the gap between stated capacity and actual delivery capacity, at every level of the system.

**The Evidence**
In 'Working From Home Daily #77: Virtual teams and digital transformation' (2020), Founding Fuel cites Rajan's argument about institutional capacity deficits, applied in the context of the pandemic's exposure of state and organisational fragility. The piece draws the direct connection between public institutional capacity constraints and private sector ones — arguing that both face the same underlying problem: the systems designed for stable conditions have very limited capacity to absorb rapid change because they were never designed with adaptability as a requirement.

**The Implication**
Map the actual decision-making path for one critical transformation initiative — not the formal org chart, the real path — and identify every point where the chain depends on a single person's informal authority rather than a replicable process.

### Principle 12

> What you learn must be immediately usable. Learning that is not doing-ready is an expensive ritual.

**What This Means & Why**
India's organisations invest substantially in training and development, and most of it produces very little sustained change in behaviour. The reason is structural: formal learning programmes are designed for comprehension, not application. Participants leave with new concepts and return to environments that have not changed and where the new concepts find no purchase. The learning that actually transforms capability is learning that is immediately applied to real problems in real conditions, with feedback loops tight enough to correct in real time.

**The India Reality**
India's IT services sector — which employs hundreds of thousands of people who need to continuously upgrade their capabilities as technology transitions accelerate — has a particularly acute version of this problem. The training infrastructure is large, well-funded, and largely disconnected from the actual work. The gap between what a training programme covers and what a client engagement requires has historically been bridged by on-the-job acceleration, which means the formal training was a credential, not a capability.

**The Evidence**
In 'Learning to stay relevant' (2019), Founding Fuel reports on K Ramkumar's argument that "'learning which is not doing-ready is wasted effort'" — and describes a concrete alternative: a 24-week, 100-case apprenticeship model for learning credit risk, where learning and application happen simultaneously, with each case providing the feedback loop for the next. The model is structurally incompatible with classroom delivery but consistently produces capability that classroom delivery does not.

**The Implication**
For the next learning initiative you approve, require that every module has a corresponding real work application completed within two weeks, with output reviewed by a senior practitioner — and treat any module that cannot meet this requirement as not yet ready to be delivered.

### Principle 13

> Standards transform organisations. Talent, alone, does not.

**What This Means & Why**
The dominant narrative of organisational transformation focuses on acquiring talent — hiring better people, building better teams, creating conditions for exceptional individuals. This gets the mechanism backwards. The organisations that produce sustained high performance do so by raising the standard that everyone is held to, not by concentrating exceptional individuals. When standards are low, exceptional individuals either leave or regress to the mean. When standards are genuinely high, ordinary people become capable of extraordinary things because the environment demands and supports it.

**The India Reality**
India's competitive obsession with IIT entrance ranks, MBA pedigree, and individual star performers creates organisations that are talent-dense and standard-light. The implicit assumption — that assembling enough exceptional individuals will produce exceptional collective performance — is contradicted by the observable reality of many Indian organisations that have all the talent and none of the execution.

**The Evidence**
In 'How Bishan Bedi took Delhi from Club Class to World Class' (2021), Founding Fuel describes Bedi's transformation of Delhi cricket from a culture of low standards — "a few warm-up stretches, a fifteen-minute net session and a few catches" — to a world-class programme, through a single intervention: raising the standards that everyone was held to and demanding a free hand from the administration to enforce them. The transformation is attributed not to finding better players but to creating an environment in which existing players had to meet a standard they had never previously been held to.

**The Implication**
Identify the one standard in your organisation that everyone knows is not being met but that no one has been held accountable for in the last year, and make the decision to enforce it — publicly, visibly, starting next week.

### Principle 14

> Work from home in India is not a policy question. It is a household infrastructure question — and the infrastructure is not there.

**What This Means & Why**
The global conversation about remote and hybrid work has been conducted almost entirely from the perspective of urban, educated, upper-income workers in large apartments with reliable internet, a dedicated workspace, and household support. In India, this describes a small fraction of the workforce. The rest are navigating a radically different physical reality — multi-generational households, shared spaces, unreliable connectivity, and gendered household labour expectations — that makes the imported playbook not just suboptimal but actively harmful if applied without modification.

**The India Reality**
The specific texture of Indian WFH — the interruptions on video calls from family members, the husbands being shouted at in the background, the wives being asked to bring chai while simultaneously on a work call, the complete collapse of the separation between work and domestic life — is not a minor implementation detail. It is a structural feature of how work from home lands in a society where the household has not been redesigned for it.

**The Evidence**
In 'WFH: Drinking from a fire hose' (2020), Kavi Arasu describes the lived reality of Indian WFH in specific, unvarnished detail: "all of the immediate family is at home. And at many Indian homes that is immediate family, aged parents, in-laws, relatives, all living in a small space. I have been on calls where kids have interrupted, husbands have been shouted at and wives have been asked to bring chai — while their work selves are trying hard to focus on the call." The piece argues that this reality requires India-specific frameworks, not adaptations of Western remote work thinking.

**The Implication**
Before setting any policy on remote or hybrid work, survey your employees — not their managers, the employees themselves — on the specific physical and household conditions in which they are actually working, and use that as the design input rather than any global benchmark.

### Principle 15

> The return-to-office debate is a generational negotiation dressed up as a productivity argument.

**What This Means & Why**
Most organisations framing their return-to-office position as an evidence-based productivity decision are actually navigating a values conflict between different generations with genuinely different relationships to work, place, and technology. Resolving it with a blanket policy — in either direction — is not a productivity optimisation. It is a choice about which generation's preferences the organisation will centre, with predictable consequences for which generation stays.

**The India Reality**
In India, this generational divide is playing out with particular sharpness because the generation that built many of India's largest organisations — and that holds most of the authority in them — genuinely experienced the office as a social and professional community, not just a place of work. The generation that now constitutes the majority of the workforce has had that experience interrupted, replaced, and in many cases simply never formed. These are not reconcilable positions. They require negotiation, not policy.

**The Evidence**
In 'Rushing back to office' (2022), Indrajit Gupta documents a conversation between an HR stalwart and his son, also senior HR at a global tech firm, who tells his father: "Dad, you simply aren't aware of how technology is changing the way work is done." The son has relocated to Goa. Gupta frames this not as an anecdote but as a structural observation: "This generational divide is playing out inside families" — and therefore is playing out inside every organisation that contains both generations, regardless of what the policy document says.

**The Implication**
Before issuing or enforcing any return-to-office policy, convene a structured conversation between your most senior leaders and a representative group of employees under thirty-five, without an HR intermediary, and listen to what the disagreement is actually about.

### Principle 16

> The McKinsey Three Horizons model was true when it was built. The conditions that made it true no longer exist.

**What This Means & Why**
The Three Horizons framework assumes that Horizon 3 innovations — genuinely disruptive, transformative ideas — require years of development before they can threaten Horizon 1 businesses. This assumption was accurate in a pre-cloud, pre-platform, pre-open-source world where building new infrastructure took time and capital that only incumbents had. In a world where a startup can deploy at enterprise scale in months, the assumed lag between Horizon 3 disruption and Horizon 1 threat has collapsed to nearly zero. Using Three Horizons to sequence a transformation strategy is now a way of giving yourself permission to be slow.

**The India Reality**
India's IT services sector used Three Horizons extensively to manage the transition from legacy outsourcing to digital services. The framework provided intellectual comfort for a transition that was genuinely moving faster than the framework's assumptions allowed for. The result was a systematic underestimation of how quickly the market was shifting underneath organisations that believed they had years to prepare.

**The Evidence**
In 'What Yes Bank can learn from Cognizant and Francisco D'Souza' (2019), Founding Fuel reports that D'Souza at Cognizant used the Three Horizons model and then examines Steve Blank's explicit critique: "Today, disruption — Horizon 3 ideas — can be delivered as fast as Horizon 1 ideas." FF endorses Blank's counter-argument directly, framing Three Horizons not as a useful planning tool but as a model that creates false confidence in the time available for transformation.

**The Implication**
Review your current transformation roadmap and identify every milestone that is predicated on a competitor or market being slower to move than your assumptions — treat each one as a risk, not a given.

### Principle 17

> Leaders who cannot move between task focus and human focus — within seconds, not quarters — will lose their organisations during transformation.

**What This Means & Why**
Transformation creates simultaneous demands on leadership that are neurologically in tension with each other: the analytical focus required to solve hard operational problems, and the social scanning required to maintain the human trust and engagement that makes transformation possible. Leaders who are good at one and weak at the other predictably succeed at the technical elements of transformation and fail at the human ones, or vice versa. The capacity to cycle rapidly between these two modes — not to choose one, but to oscillate between them in real time — is the distinctive leadership competence that transformation requires.

**The India Reality**
India's dominant model of business leadership rewards analytical and hierarchical authority. The leader who is perceived as emotionally attuned — who invests visibly in the human dimension of transformation — is frequently coded as soft, insufficiently decisive, or easily manipulated. The bias is structural, not personal, and it produces leadership cultures that are technically sophisticated and humanly brittle at precisely the moments when human resilience matters most.

**The Evidence**
In 'FF Insights #628: Taking people along' (2022), Founding Fuel cites Daniel Goleman and Richard Boyatzis on the neural network dynamics of leadership focus: "The neural network that engages when we focus on a goal differs from the circuitry for social scanning. 'They inhibit each other,' says Boyatzis. 'The most successful leaders cycle back and forth between these within seconds.'" The piece uses this research to argue that the leaders who are most effective at taking people through transformation are not those who are warmest or most visionary, but those who can move between the two modes with speed and precision.

**The Implication**
In your next team meeting during a transformation initiative, deliberately set aside the agenda for fifteen minutes and ask each person in the room to name the one thing that is making their part of the transformation harder than it needs to be — and listen without problem-solving.

### Principle 18

> Writing is a transformation tool, not a documentation tool.

**What This Means & Why**
The leaders who navigate transformation most effectively are almost always the ones who think in writing — not because writing produces better memos, but because writing forces the kind of slow, sequential, left-hemisphere analytical processing that surfaces the contradictions, ambiguities, and unexamined assumptions that fast thinking glosses over. The organisations that make the best transformation decisions are disproportionately the ones where thinking in writing is a cultural norm, not a bureaucratic obligation.

**The India Reality**
India's business culture is overwhelmingly oral. Decisions are made in rooms, transmitted in calls, confirmed in brief WhatsApp messages. The discipline of writing down the reasoning — the assumptions, the alternatives considered, the criteria for success — is rare in Indian business, which means that transformation decisions are frequently made without the kind of slow thinking that reveals whether the reasoning is actually sound.

**The Evidence**
In 'FF Life: The rewards of writing' (2024), Krishna Jha draws on Iain McGilchrist's neuroscience of hemispheric processing to argue that writing activates the right hemisphere's capacity for holistic, contextual, integrative thinking in ways that speaking does not — and that this is what makes writing valuable not as communication but as cognition. The piece frames writing as a tool for building the kind of genuine understanding that transformation decisions require, distinct from the kind of performance of understanding that most presentations produce.

**The Implication**
Require that every major transformation decision be preceded by a one-page written argument — not a slide deck, a prose argument — that states the problem, the alternatives, and the reasoning for the chosen path, and treat the quality of that argument as evidence of the quality of the decision.

## FF's View

**The Core Conviction**

The deepest argument FF makes about transformation — the one most practitioners resist, and the one that every article in this body of evidence points back to — is this: transformation is not a project. It is not a programme with a start date, a consulting engagement with deliverables, a digital overhaul with a go-live milestone. It is a continuous, slow, often invisible reorientation of how an organisation thinks, learns, and lets go. The moment you treat it as a project, you have already failed in the most important sense — even if every KPI eventually turns green.

This conviction sits in uncomfortable tension with how Indian business actually operates. The planning cycle demands milestones. The board wants quarterly evidence of progress. The CEO wants a transformation narrative for the annual report. The consultants — the ones brought in from outside with their frameworks and their benchmarks and their international case studies — want a roadmap they can invoice against. All of this creates enormous institutional pressure to treat transformation as a bounded thing, a before-and-after, a problem that can be understood in a few days and solved in a few weeks.

In 'Working From Home Daily #77' (2020), Founding Fuel cites Vijay Kelkar and Ajay Shah's warning about what they call the "cult of jald baazi" — the deeply Indian assumption that speed signals competence, that a fast answer is a confident leader's answer, that moving quickly is moving well. This is not a new pathology. It predates startups and digital transformation. But it has found new expression in the current moment, where transformation is both genuinely urgent and genuinely misunderstood.

What FF holds against this is something almost unfashionable: that real change requires slowness in its thinking even when it moves fast in its execution. That the do-learn-do cycle — not the detailed plan, not the waterfall roadmap — is the only honest account of how transformation actually works. That the organisations which have changed most durably are the ones that treated transformation as a way of operating rather than a destination to reach.

Nandan Nilekani building UIDAI is FF's foundational case. He had no detailed plan. He had vision, tolerance for feedback, and the willingness to adapt. He was an outsider in the government with all its pulls and pushes, operating in an institution that the planning cycle had never produced anything like Aadhaar from. What he had was a do-learn-do orientation, and the institutional backing to sustain it long enough for it to work. As 'Detailed planning is passé. A do-learn-do approach works better' (2019) makes clear, the lesson from Aadhaar is not "have a visionary leader." The lesson is: the process matters as much as the person, and the process must be built for learning, not for proving a plan right.

FF believes this because the evidence insists on it. Across industries, across decades, across the specific conditions of India — the organisations that transform are the ones that have developed genuine learning capacity. Not the ones with the shiniest transformation programmes.

**What India Means for This Theme**

Transformation theory is mostly written in American and Western European conditions. It assumes institutional stability. It assumes that the regulatory environment, while imperfect, is at least predictable. It assumes that talent markets are deep, that infrastructure is reliable, that the distance between a decision and its execution is measured in weeks rather than years. None of these assumptions hold cleanly in India, and several of them fail spectacularly.

The first India-specific reality is the institutional capacity deficit. In 'Working From Home Daily #77' (2020), Founding Fuel quotes Raghuram Rajan on where India genuinely lags: "the capacity of the government — and by this I mean regulators like the RBI also — to deliver governance and public services." This is not a temporary gap. It is structural. It means that transformation in India almost always involves navigating around institutional weaknesses rather than building on institutional strengths. The organisation that waits for the policy environment to be right before beginning to transform will wait indefinitely. The organisation that builds its own capacity for change — that treats institutional uncertainty as the permanent condition rather than the temporary exception — has already made the most important adaptation.

The second reality is the household constraint that Western frameworks of work and change simply do not see. When COVID forced the question of remote work and organisational transformation onto Indian businesses at speed, the discussion imported from Harvard and McKinsey assumed a certain kind of household — relatively spacious, relatively private, a working adult and perhaps a partner, with domestic infrastructure sufficient to support knowledge work from home. In 'WFH: Drinking from a fire hose' (2020), Kavi Arasu names what that framework missed: "All of the immediate family is at home. And at many Indian homes that is immediate family, aged parents, in-laws, relatives, all living in a small space. I have been on calls where kids have interrupted, husbands have been shouted at and wives have been asked to bring chai — while their work selves are trying hard to focus on the call." This is not a detail. It changes the entire calculus of what transformation of work means in India. The question of where and how people work cannot be separated from the question of what their lives actually contain.

The third reality is generational, and it is sharper in India than the mainstream business conversation acknowledges. In 'Rushing back to office' (2022), Indrajit Gupta captures a conversation between an HR stalwart and his son — also senior HR at a global tech firm — in which the son tells his father plainly: "Dad, you simply aren't aware of how technology is changing the way work is done." The son has relocated to Goa. The father represents a generation for which physical presence at work is not merely a preference but a moral commitment. The son represents a generation for which the office is one option among several. This is not a debate about productivity. It is a debate about identity and about what a career means. And it is happening inside Indian families, not just inside Indian organisations, which means it cannot be resolved by policy alone.

The fourth reality is the Bharat dimension — the vast, non-urban, non-English-speaking India that most transformation discourse ignores. Aadhaar is the only transformation story at scale that genuinely confronted this: the need to build something that worked for a farmer in Bihar who had never had a document proving his existence, not just for a software engineer in Bengaluru. Almost everything else in India's transformation canon — the startup ecosystem, the digital economy, the shift in management practices — has happened in and for urban India. The questions about what transformation means for the rest of the country remain substantially unanswered.

The fifth reality is what might be called the conglomerate complexity. India's largest organisations are not clean, single-business entities. They are conglomerates — Tata, Aditya Birla, Mahindra — with multiple businesses operating across multiple industries, each at a different point in its competitive trajectory. The question of how to transform these entities is categorically different from the question of how to transform a focused technology company. In 'The challenges before N Chandra' (2017), Indrajit Gupta uses BCG's Corporate Center parenting models to frame the governance choices facing Chandrasekaran at Tata Sons — but the deeper point is that India's dominant organisational form creates transformation challenges that Western theory, built mostly around focused single-business competitors, simply has not developed adequate language for.

**Where Global Best Practices Break Down**

The McKinsey Three Horizons model is the canonical framework for thinking about how organisations manage transformation across time. Horizon 1 is the core business. Horizon 2 is emerging businesses. Horizon 3 is genuinely new, transformative bets. The model says: work all three simultaneously, in sequence, protect the cash engine while building the future. Francisco D'Souza used it at Cognizant. It is used across India's IT sector. It is taught in business schools everywhere.

In 'What Yes Bank can learn from Cognizant and Francisco D'Souza' (2019), Founding Fuel cites Steve Blank's direct challenge to this framework: "Today, disruption — Horizon 3 ideas — can be delivered as fast as Horizon 1 ideas." Blank's point is that the Three Horizons model was built for a world where disruption moved on a different timescale than incumbency. That world no longer exists. When a competitor can take a Horizon 3 idea from conception to market in eighteen months, the comfortable assumption that you have time to protect Horizon 1 while building Horizon 3 in parallel is a dangerous fiction. The framework becomes a rationale for not moving, rather than a structure for moving strategically.

The agile orthodoxy — Scrum, sprints, ceremonies, velocity points — is another case where the framework arrives in Indian organisations stripped of the conditions that made it work. In 'An inquiry into Agility' (2019), Founding Fuel is careful about this: real agility requires something close to what John Stuart Mill argued for in On Liberty — an organisational culture in which dissent is treated as potentially correct, in which "someone's controversial idea today may turn out to be right tomorrow." The ceremonies of agile without the culture of agile produce the worst of both worlds: the administrative overhead of agile methods combined with the hierarchical decision-making of traditional management. Indian organisations, where hierarchy is not just a structural feature but often a deeply personal one — where deference to seniority is a social norm that runs well below the organisational chart — are especially vulnerable to this failure mode.

The corporate university model — inspired by GE's Crotonville, replicated at Unilever's Four Acres and Singapore, at HLL's Gulita, at Tata's TMTC in Pune, at Aditya Birla's Gyanodaya, at SBI, Infosys, L&T, Bharat Petroleum — is a third case. The model rested on a conviction that leadership development was a long-term investment, that the organisation's future depended on building people over years, that culture was transmitted through immersive experience rather than e-learning modules. In 'Behind Crotonville's demise' (2024), Indrajit Gupta traces how short-termism — what he calls a "virus" of shareholder value obsession — destroyed GE's own Crotonville and now threatens every institution it inspired. The global best practice is being abandoned at its source. The Indian organisations that built on it need to decide whether the best practice was ever right, or whether it was simply well-resourced.

The outsider-consultant transformation model is perhaps the most pervasive failure mode. In 'Play to your strengths' (2019), Founding Fuel cites Behnam Tabrizi's HBR argument directly: "organisations that seek transformations frequently bring in an army of outside consultants who tend to apply one-size-fits-all solutions in the name of 'best practices'." The counter-evidence is consistent: transformations led by insiders — people with "intimate knowledge about what works and what doesn't in their daily operations" — consistently outperform those led by outside consultants applying imported frameworks. The consultants are not wrong about the frameworks. They are wrong about the application. And in India, where the distance between a benchmark and a context is especially large, this failure is especially expensive.

**How FF Diverges from Mainstream Media**

Mainstream Indian business media covers transformation as announcement. The coverage follows a predictable rhythm: the CEO announces a transformation programme, the press release names the technology platform or the consulting partner or the new organisational structure, the magazine runs a profile of the leader's vision. Eighteen months later, there is sometimes a follow-up on whether it worked. More often, there is a new announcement, and the cycle begins again.

FF's approach is structurally different. FF covers transformation as process — slow, contested, often invisible in the moment it is happening. The 'gradually, then suddenly' metaphor that runs through Kavi Arasu's 2019 piece 'Spot change as it happens gradually. Or get swept away suddenly' is also a description of how FF tries to report: paying attention to the ordinary trickles while mainstream media waits for the spectacular waves.

This produces different coverage in concrete ways. Where mainstream media covered Cognizant's leadership transition primarily as a succession story, FF used it to examine the underlying competitive transformation challenge — the gap between the business D'Souza had built and the business the market was moving toward, and what that meant for anyone leading a similar organisation. In 'What Yes Bank can learn from Cognizant and Francisco D'Souza' (2019), Founding Fuel explicitly frames the Cognizant case as generalisable — a lesson not for Cognizant alone but for every Indian IT services firm facing the same structural challenge of moving from services to solutions before the market makes the choice for them.

Where mainstream media covered the return-to-office debate as a productivity question or a management preference, FF covered it as a generational, cultural, and structural question. In 'Rushing back to office' (2022), Indrajit Gupta names the generational divide not as a management problem to be resolved by policy but as a genuine tension between two legitimately different understandings of what work is for — and suggests that the leaders most confident about full return are the ones least equipped to see what is actually changing.

FF has also been earlier than most to the question of what corporate culture actually does during transformation — and what happens when culture is treated as a communications exercise rather than an operational one. The 2015 piece 'Get out of the clichés trap' on company values was ahead of the mainstream conversation about the gap between stated values and lived reality. The 2026 piece by Indranil Gupta, 'The Quiet Work of Branding,' extends this into the present: "Branding can no longer be cosmetic. It has to help organisations orient themselves before they can persuade others." The gap between what organisations say they are becoming and what they are actually becoming is not a communications gap. It is a leadership gap. FF has been making this argument longer than the mainstream has been comfortable with it.

**Against Management Orthodoxy**

The orthodoxy says: set the vision, build the plan, execute the plan, measure against the plan. The SMART goals, the balanced scorecard, the OKRs — these are all variations on the same underlying belief that transformation can be planned in advance with sufficient fidelity that deviation from the plan represents failure rather than learning.

What the evidence actually shows — from Aadhaar to Persistent Systems to Bishan Bedi's transformation of Delhi cricket — is that transformation is iterative by necessity, not by preference. In 'Detailed planning is passé. A do-learn-do approach works better' (2019), Founding Fuel quotes directly: "While long-term vision is crucial, you can't get things done by detailed planning. What you need is the willingness to constantly learn, be open to feedback from multiple sources, and flexibility to adapt to changes." The vision is stable. The plan is provisional. The organisations that confuse these two things — that treat the plan as the commitment and the vision as the aspiration — get the priorities exactly backwards.

The orthodoxy says: bring in external expertise to accelerate transformation. The outsider sees what the insider cannot. The consultant brings best practice from elsewhere. The new leader from a different industry brings a fresh perspective unclouded by history.

What the evidence shows is more complicated. In 'Play to your strengths' (2019), the argument for insiders as change agents is not that outsiders are always wrong but that outsiders systematically underestimate the importance of operational intimacy — knowing not just what the best practice is but which version of it will work in this specific organisation, with these specific people, in this specific market. Nilekani at UIDAI was technically an outsider to government, but his approach was to develop operational intimacy rapidly through the do-learn-do cycle, not to apply a framework developed elsewhere and imported in full.

The orthodoxy says: define your values, communicate them clearly, hold people accountable to them. This is the standard culture-change playbook, replicated in thousands of transformation programmes across India every year.

What the evidence shows is that the words organisations use to describe their values are almost universally useless. In 'Get out of the clichés trap' (2015), Founding Fuel names the pathology: abstract value statements that every employee interprets differently because abstraction permits any interpretation. "Integrity." "Customer-centricity." "Innovation." "Sustainability." These words appear in virtually every Indian corporate value statement. They produce no shared understanding because they make no commitment. The antidote is not better communication of the same abstractions. It is specificity — values expressed as concrete choices, as the things you will do and the things you will not do, as decisions rather than adjectives.

The orthodoxy says: the genius leader can transform an organisation through sheer force of vision, will, and personality. The Steve Jobs story — told and retold — has made this the dominant narrative of transformation in the technology sector.

What the evidence shows, as FF traces through the Housing.com case in 'What Steve Jobs 2.0 can teach Housing.com's Rahul Yadav 1.0' (2015), is that the Jobs narrative is selectively remembered. "The grand narrative of Steve Jobs is that of a flawed genius, whose genius was in combining the big picture and minute details." The implication — that the genius justifies the flaws — is the dangerous part of the story. Most people who exhibit the flaws do not have the genius to compensate. And even Jobs's genius was institutional as well as individual: he built organisational capability, not just inspired it.

The orthodoxy says: move fast, break things. Speed is competitive advantage. The organisation that decides faster will win.

What the evidence shows — consistently, from multiple angles — is that speed in execution is only valuable when the thinking has been slow enough. In 'FF Daily #434: Living slow' (2021), Founding Fuel cites Carl Honore's argument directly: "We have to vaccinate ourselves against a pernicious virus called 'hurry', which constantly tempts us to reach for a 'quick fix'. But most of the important problems we face need the opposite — slow fix." Toyota's practice of asking why five times before solving a problem is not inefficiency. It is the difference between solving the symptom and solving the cause.

**The Entrepreneurial Mindset Beyond Startups**

The do-learn-do orientation is not a startup methodology. It is a thinking posture that anyone — in a large bank, in a government institution, in a family business — can adopt or refuse to adopt. What makes it entrepreneurial is not the organisational form. It is the willingness to treat a decision as a hypothesis rather than a commitment, to regard failure as data rather than verdict, and to maintain the vision while holding the plan loosely.

Nilekani at UIDAI is the clearest illustration of this. He was not running a startup. He was operating inside the government of India, surrounded by institutional inertia, political uncertainty, and the full weight of a bureaucracy that had never produced anything like what he was attempting. What he brought was not a startup's speed or a startup's funding or a startup's freedom from legacy. What he brought was the startup's orientation toward learning. As 'Detailed planning is passé' (2019) makes clear, this is transferable. It is a choice, not a condition.

Bishan Bedi at Delhi cricket is another case. In 'How Bishan Bedi took Delhi from Club Class to World Class' (2021), Founding Fuel traces how Bedi transformed Delhi's cricketing culture — not by importing talent but by raising standards. He demanded a free hand from administration. He replaced the casual preparation culture ("a few warm-up stretches, a fifteen-minute net session and a few catches") with genuine professional expectations. This is the entrepreneurial mindset applied to institutional transformation: identifying the precise lever that, if moved, changes everything else, and moving it with enough conviction to hold it in place against the resistance that inevitably follows.

In large organisations, this mindset tends to appear at the edges before it appears at the centre. The middle manager who redesigns a credit approval process using apprenticeship rather than classroom training — as K Ramkumar describes in 'Learning to stay relevant' (2019), where a 24-week, 100-case apprenticeship model produces genuinely doing-ready credit officers rather than certificate-holders — is exercising an entrepreneurial orientation within a large institution. The question is whether the institution rewards or suppresses it.

Hubert Joly's turnaround of Best Buy, cited in 'FF Daily #458' (2021), is the international case that FF uses to make this point at the leadership level. Best Buy was written off. The consensus view was that it could not survive Amazon. Joly's approach was not to build a new business model in a garage. It was to find the specific sources of genuine value that Best Buy possessed — the store footprint, the technical expertise, the vendor relationships — and to reconstruct the strategy around those assets. This is the entrepreneurial mindset inside an incumbent: not denial of disruption, not imitation of disruptors, but honest assessment of what you have that others cannot easily replicate.

For policymakers, the implication is parallel. The policy equivalent of the do-learn-do cycle is the pilot — the bounded experiment that generates evidence before the full commitment. India has used this well in some contexts (the Direct Benefit Transfer system was effectively piloted before it was scaled) and not at all in others (demonetisation, implemented at full scale without the feedback loop that a pilot would have provided). The difference between these two is not just political. It is an orientation toward learning — or away from it.

**On Transformation: Process, Not Just Destination**

The mechanism of transformation — how it actually happens, not how it is announced — is the thing most management frameworks systematically obscure. Because the destination (the transformed state) is legible and the process (the messy, non-linear journey) is not, organisations communicate the destination and hope the process follows. It rarely does.

What FF's reporting reveals, consistently, is that transformation happens in layers and at different speeds for different parts of the organisation. The technology layer changes fastest. The process layer changes more slowly. The culture layer changes most slowly of all, and most organisations dramatically underestimate how slowly.

In 'Play to your strengths' (2019), the framing of the challenge is explicit: "The challenges aren't as much about innovation or technology, but unlearning and learning afresh." The verb is important. Unlearning is active, not passive. You do not unlearn by deciding to unlearn. You unlearn through experience that contradicts what you previously knew and through institutional structures that protect the space for that contradiction to be felt rather than suppressed.

The S-curve model — cited in 'Anand Deshpande's founder's dilemma at Persistent' (2021) — maps this temporal reality: "companies go through these S-curves. You are at the top of the curve and the team is not ready to transition to the next S." The gap between being at the top of one curve and successfully launching onto the next is the most dangerous moment in a company's transformation. It looks, from the outside, like confidence. It feels, from the inside, like momentum. What it actually is, is inertia — the continuation of what worked yesterday into conditions where it no longer will.

The leadership transition is the most acute version of this problem. In 'The challenges before N Chandra' (2017), Indrajit Gupta examines what Chandrasekaran inherited at Tata Sons and how the governance architecture he needed to build was qualitatively different from what Cyrus Mistry had left behind. The point is not about individuals. It is about what kind of change is possible in what kind of time. Governance transformation — changing how a conglomerate makes decisions — is slower than product transformation, slower than market transformation, and far slower than the announcement of a new governance philosophy.

The Crotonville case, traced in 'Behind Crotonville's demise' (2024) by Indrajit Gupta, shows what happens when the process of transformation is interrupted before it completes. GE built Crotonville over decades — a genuine, immersive system for developing leaders who could manage at the scale and complexity GE required. When short-termism dismantled it, the effects were not immediately visible in financial results. They are visible now, in the quality of leadership GE has been able to sustain. Process interrupted does not produce a partial result. It often produces a worse result than if the process had never begun — because the organisation has lost the old capability without building the new one.

This is the genuine risk of half-finished transformation: the organisation that has committed enough to abandon its old ways but not enough to establish new ones. The "capability gap" that opens in this middle state is where most transformations fail — not because the vision was wrong but because the process was starved of time, patience, or conviction before it could complete.

**The Human and Cultural Dimension**

Strategy frameworks treat people as the medium through which transformation happens. Culture frameworks treat culture as an asset to be managed. FF's approach is different: it treats human and cultural factors not as implementation considerations but as the primary substance of transformation. Everything else — the technology, the strategy, the process redesign — is secondary to whether the people in the organisation have genuinely shifted how they think and what they value.

This is not soft. It is the hardest part. Because the things that need to shift — identity, habit, the story a person tells about their own expertise — are the most resistant to management intervention. In 'FF Insights #628: Taking people along' (2022), Founding Fuel cites Daniel Goleman and Richard Boyatzis on what this looks like in neurological terms: "The neural network that engages when we focus on a goal differs from the circuitry for social scanning. 'They inhibit each other,' says Boyatzis. 'The most successful leaders cycle back and forth between these within seconds.'" The leader who is most focused on the transformation objective is, at the same moment, least equipped to read the room — to sense whether the people they need to carry are actually coming along. Holding both simultaneously is the core leadership challenge of transformation.

The cultural dimension is compounded in India by hierarchical norms that are not merely organisational but personal and social. In 'An inquiry into Agility' (2019), Founding Fuel uses the Mill argument — that genuine agility requires the capacity to hold your identity separate from the ideas you currently endorse — to diagnose why agile methods so often fail in India's corporate culture. The manager who has built their identity around a set of expertise, a set of answers, a particular way of running a team cannot easily become the manager who says "I don't know — let's find out." The culture does not make space for that kind of public epistemic humility. The consequence is that the forms of agility are adopted — the Scrum ceremonies, the sprint reviews — without the substance: the genuine willingness to be wrong, to update, to discard what was previously true.

Branding — the way organisations represent themselves to themselves as much as to the outside world — is where FF's coverage of the cultural dimension has become most precise in recent years. In 'The Quiet Work of Branding' (2026), Indranil Gupta argues that branding during transformation is not about managing external perception. It is about helping the organisation understand what it is actually becoming — "orient themselves before they can persuade others." The gap between the brand (what the organisation says it is) and the culture (what the organisation actually does) is not a communications failure. It is a cultural failure. And it is especially pronounced during transformation, when the organisation is in-between — no longer what it was, not yet what it intends to become.

The emotional texture of this in-between state is almost never discussed in transformation programmes. The people in transforming organisations experience real loss — of status, of expertise that was once valued, of relationships that were structured around the old way of working. This is not resistance to be overcome. It is grief to be acknowledged. The organisations that move through transformation most successfully are the ones that create enough space for this grief to exist without suppressing it into compliance or amplifying it into sabotage.

The Deven Pabaru piece, 'How my family health crisis forced me to rethink the value of insurance' (2025), is a different kind of evidence for the same point. The observation that "middle class India was one major hospitalisation away from slipping into poverty" is a statement about financial fragility, but it is also a statement about psychological fragility — about how precarious the platforms are from which people are expected to manage change. Transformation programmes that assume a workforce of stable, secure individuals with sufficient resources to absorb uncertainty are not reading the human reality of most Indian professionals.

**The Systems View**

No transformation happens in isolation. The organisation that transforms exists within an industry, within a regulatory environment, within a talent market, within a broader social and infrastructure context. The second and third-order effects of transformation — on suppliers, competitors, employees' families, communities, institutions — are almost always underweighted in transformation planning, and the feedback effects from those systems into the transformation itself are almost always ignored.

The Aadhaar story is the clearest illustration of this. UIDAI's transformation was not just a government project. It was a platform transformation of India's identity infrastructure — a change that, when it worked, created entirely new possibilities for financial inclusion, for digital payments, for targeted welfare delivery. The do-learn-do cycle that Nilekani used was not just a management methodology. It was a systems design approach: build enough to learn, learn enough to build further, repeat. As 'Detailed planning is passé' (2019) makes clear, the detailed plan would have been obsolete before it was executed, because the system being changed was itself changing in response to early interventions.

The interdependence between corporate transformation and policy environment is a persistent structural feature of Indian business that transformation frameworks — built for relatively deregulated Western markets — systematically underweight. When the RBI changes capital requirements, when the GST structure is modified, when labour regulations shift, the transformation roadmap that was built on last quarter's regulatory assumptions becomes partially or wholly obsolete. The organisations that handle this best are not the ones with the most sophisticated scenario planning. They are the ones with the most adaptive operating capacity — the ones that have made do-learn-do the default mode rather than a special programme.

The talent system creates its own systemic effects. The decision of Persistent Systems to invest in technology transition — the S-curve move described in 'Anand Deshpande's founder's dilemma at Persistent' (2021) — changed what kind of talent the organisation needed, which changed how it competed in the talent market, which changed the expectations of other organisations in the same market. Transformation at scale within an industry changes the industry's talent ecosystem. This is rarely modelled in advance.

The office and work ecosystem, examined across multiple FF pieces on the return-to-office question, is a system with more actors than just employers and employees. The commercial real estate sector, the urban commuting infrastructure, the domestic household economy, the local services businesses that depend on office footfall — all of these are implicated in the question of where knowledge workers work. In 'FF Insights #614: The nature of buildings' (2022), Stewart Brand's insight that "where a plan is based on prediction, a strategy is designed to encompass unforeseeably changing conditions" is applied to the office reopening question. The organisations that treated the return-to-office as a planning question — when do we return, what percentage, what days — missed the systems-level question: what is the office actually for, and how should we redesign it for what we now know?

The corporate university as system — described in 'Behind Crotonville's demise' (2024) — is another case. Crotonville was not just a training facility. It was a node in GE's leadership development system, connected to career pathways, to promotion criteria, to the organisation's conception of what a leader needed to know and experience. When GE dismantled it, they did not just close a training centre. They severed connections throughout that system — connections whose effects are now visible in leadership quality but whose cause is traced by few.

**The Evolution of FF's Thinking**

FF's earliest coverage of transformation — the 2015 pieces on values clichés and on Rahul Yadav and Steve Jobs — is primarily diagnostic. The work of this period is to name what is broken: the gap between stated values and lived culture, the danger of the genius-justifies-all narrative, the specific failure modes of leadership. The tone is sharp, the critique is precise, but the frame is relatively limited in ambition: here is what is going wrong, here is a sharper way to see it.

By 2017 and 2019, FF's thinking has widened. The Tata Sons piece on N Chandra brings in the governance dimension — the question of how large, complex conglomerates actually change at the structural level, not just the cultural or strategic one. The Cognizant and Yes Bank piece brings in the competitive transformation question — how industries shift, how the organisations inside them need to change in response, what frameworks help and which mislead. The Aadhaar and Agility pieces bring in a genuine methodology: not just a critique of what fails but an argument about what works. The do-learn-do cycle, the insider advantage, the conditions for agility — these are FF building a positive account of transformation, not just a negative one.

By 2021 and 2022, the pandemic has sharpened FF's attention to the human and organisational dimensions of change at speed. The WFH coverage, the return-to-office pieces, the Bishan Bedi case — these show FF working to understand what transformation feels like from the inside, not just what it looks like from the outside. The generational divide piece is probably the most sociologically sophisticated thing in this body of evidence: it does not take a side, it describes a genuine tension between two legitimately different worldviews, and it resists the temptation to resolve it by siding with either.

The 2024 Crotonville piece represents a maturation of FF's thinking on institutional change. The argument is no longer just "short-termism is bad." It is "short-termism destroys specific institutional capabilities that took decades to build, and we will not know the full cost until much later." This is FF at its most patient and its most systemic — willing to trace a slow-moving consequence that most coverage, anchored to the quarterly cycle, cannot see.

The 2025 and 2026 pieces — on health insurance and on branding — show FF moving into territory that transformation discourse rarely enters: the personal and the existential. Transformation is not just an organisational question. It is a question about what individuals believe, what they fear, what they are willing to let go of. The branding piece's argument that organisations must "orient themselves before they can persuade others" is FF's most recent position on what transformation is fundamentally about: not change management, but sense-making.

**Kavi Arasu** brings the practitioner-from-within perspective. His 2019 piece on gradual versus sudden change is the source of the "gradually, then suddenly" metaphor that runs through FF's entire transformation coverage — and it reads like a practitioner who has watched organisations miss the slow signals enough times to have developed a specific kind of frustration with it. His 2020 WFH piece is the most viscerally accurate account of what remote work actually meant for Indian professionals, as opposed to what it was theorised to mean. Arasu's value is his refusal to abstract — he writes from inside the experience, and this keeps FF's transformation coverage tethered to what is actually happening.

**NS Ramnath** brings the strategic and startup-adjacent perspective. His 2015 piece on Rahul Yadav and Steve Jobs is early and still accurate — a careful parsing of what the genius narrative actually claims and what the evidence actually shows. His coverage of the IT sector's transformation challenge places India's most globally competitive industry inside a set of structural forces that the industry itself has been slow to name.

**Indranil Gupta** (note: different person from Indrajit Gupta) brings the brand and culture dimension. His 2026 piece 'The Quiet Work of Branding' is the most recent evidence that FF's transformation coverage has evolved toward the sense-making dimension — the question of how organisations understand themselves during change, not just how they execute it.

**Deven Pabaru** represents a voice type that is relatively rare in FF's transformation coverage: the individual practitioner experiencing a personal transformation triggered by a life event, rather than an organisational one. His 2025 piece on health insurance and financial vulnerability is evidence that transformation discourse in India needs to account for the personal and financial insecurity of the people it asks to change.

**Krishna Jha** contributes the intellectual dimension — the argument, in 'FF Life: The rewards of writing' (2024), that writing is itself a transformation tool, a way of using language to force clarity about what one actually believes as opposed to what one thinks one believes. The Iain McGilchrist framing of right-hemisphere versus left-hemisphere processing is the most philosophically serious contribution to FF's coverage of how individual transformation actually works at the cognitive level.

**What FF Is Skeptical Of**

FF is skeptical of any transformation that announces itself loudly at the beginning and goes quiet in the middle. The announcement — the new CEO's vision, the transformation programme launch, the rebranding exercise — is the least important moment in a transformation. It is the middle, when the initial energy has dissipated and the resistance has consolidated and the quick wins have been banked but the structural change has not yet taken hold, that determines everything. Mainstream business media covers the announcement. FF tries to cover the middle.

FF is skeptical of outsider-led transformation consulting. Not because outsiders are incompetent — many are very competent — but because competence applied without operational intimacy is insufficient for the kind of transformation that actually changes how an organisation works, not just how it is structured. The arguments in 'Play to your strengths' (2019) are specific and evidenced, and they point consistently toward insider knowledge as the critical variable that outsider frameworks cannot replicate.

FF is skeptical of speed as virtue. The jald baazi critique, running from the Kelkar-Shah observation in 2020 through Carl Honore's slow fix argument in 2021, is one of FF's most consistent positions. It is also one that puts FF in direct tension with a dominant strain of Indian business culture — the startup culture that celebrates velocity, the consulting culture that sells acceleration, the investor culture that rewards quarterly momentum. FF does not argue for slowness as such. It argues for slowness in thinking even when speed is necessary in execution, and for the discipline to distinguish between decisions that can be made quickly and problems that cannot be solved quickly.

FF is skeptical of corporate value statements as currently practised. The 2015 critique of clichéd values — "integrity," "customer-centricity," "innovation" — remains current because the practice has not changed. Indian corporations continue to produce value statements that are abstract enough to mean anything and therefore mean nothing. The antidote FF has consistently offered — concrete, specific, differentiated values that function as actual decision rules — has been heard but not widely adopted.

FF is skeptical of the return-to-office mandate as a transformation strategy. In 'Rushing back to office' (2022), Indrajit Gupta is careful not to argue that offices have no value. His argument is more precise: that mandating full return on the assumption that the pre-2020 model was correct is not a transformation position, it is a regression position. And the leaders most confident in this position are the ones whose careers were built on the assumption that physical co-presence is intrinsically valuable — an assumption that the evidence from two years of remote work has not confirmed as universally as they believe.

FF is skeptical of the Three Horizons model as currently applied. The Steve Blank critique cited in 'What Yes Bank can learn from Cognizant and Francisco D'Souza' (2019) is FF's most pointed departure from a specific management framework — and it is specifically relevant to India's IT services sector, which has used the Three Horizons to justify a slower pace of transition than the competitive environment actually permits.

---

**What FF Celebrates**

FF celebrates the do-learn-do practitioner — the leader who holds the vision tightly and the plan loosely, who creates feedback loops instead of defending forecasts, who treats every implementation as an experiment to be learned from rather than a commitment to be justified. Nilekani at UIDAI is the archetype. What FF celebrates in him is not the achievement (Aadhaar) but the orientation (constant feedback, constant adaptation, refusal to let the plan become the goal).

FF celebrates insiders who lead transformation. Not because insiders are always right — the Deshpande case at Persistent is partly a story of an insider who had to decide whether his own presence was helping or preventing the change the company needed — but because insider knowledge, when combined with genuine openness to change, produces the most durable transformation. The insider who decides to change is more powerful than the outsider who decides to impose change.

FF celebrates leaders who raise standards rather than manage expectations. Bishan Bedi's transformation of Delhi cricket, described in the 2021 piece, is celebrated not because Bedi was charismatic or visionary in the conventional sense, but because he identified the specific thing that was producing mediocrity — the casual preparation culture — and refused to accept it. The willingness to name what is insufficient and hold the standard against resistance is what FF consistently recognises as leadership in transformation.

FF celebrates organisations that invest in people over time periods longer than the incentive structures reward. The corporate university model, even as FF traces its current vulnerability to short-termism, is held up as an example of what genuine commitment to development looks like. The organisations that built Gulita, TMTC, Gyanodaya were making a bet on a time horizon that most management systems cannot see. FF respects the bet even while it documents the pressures against it.

FF celebrates the practitioner who writes — who uses writing, as Krishna Jha argues in 'FF Life: The rewards of writing' (2024), to force genuine clarity rather than the performance of clarity. The capacity to put something on paper and discover, in the writing, that you believed something different from what you thought you believed is a transformation tool that costs nothing and requires only the discipline to sit with uncertainty long enough to resolve it into words.

---

**The Historical Lens**

FF uses history to make the present legible — not as nostalgia but as diagnostic. The Crotonville case is the clearest example: tracing the arc from GE's creation of what was essentially the world's first corporate university, through its decades of influence on leadership development practice globally and in India specifically, to its dismantling under short-termist pressure, gives FF a precise vocabulary for what is currently at risk in India's own corporate universities. Without the historical frame, the risk is invisible — each individual decision to cut the training budget or reduce the Crotonville equivalent looks like a reasonable cost management choice. With the historical frame, the cumulative cost becomes legible.

The Steve Jobs narrative in the 2015 Rahul Yadav piece is historical in a different way — it uses the Jobs story as a lens for reading a contemporary situation, and in doing so both illuminates the contemporary situation and complicates the historical one. FF does not simply apply the Jobs story. It examines the Jobs story closely enough to distinguish what was genuinely true about Jobs from what has been mythologised in the retelling. This is the historical lens at its most careful: using precedent not to confirm a pre-existing argument but to test it.

The Mill citation in 'An inquiry into Agility' (2019) is the most philosophically ambitious historical reference in this body of evidence. Using On Liberty — a nineteenth-century argument about individual freedom in political society — to diagnose why twenty-first-century organisations fail at agility is FF at its most intellectually ambitious. The argument is that the conditions Mill identified for the free development of human potential are also the conditions required for organisational agility: tolerance for dissent, the separation of identity from position, the willingness to be wrong in public. History here is not illustration — it is foundation.

The Hemingway quote — "gradually, then suddenly" — used by Kavi Arasu in 'Spot change as it happens gradually' (2019) is the briefest historical reference but possibly the most resonant. It captures, in four words, what decades of disruption research has struggled to name precisely: that the slow accumulation of small changes is the actual mechanism of large transformation, and that the sudden disruption most people experience as the event is merely the moment when the accumulated weight of the gradual becomes impossible to ignore. Hemingway was writing about bankruptcy. Arasu is writing about markets. The mechanism is identical.

---

**Unresolved Tensions**

The deepest unresolved tension in FF's transformation coverage is between the argument for slowness and the reality of urgency. FF consistently argues for the slow fix, for the do-learn-do cycle that takes time, for the patience that transformation actually requires. FF also documents the genuine competitive urgency that Indian organisations face — the speed at which markets are moving, the pace of technological change, the narrowing window for the IT sector's transformation from services to solutions. These two things are both true, and they point in different directions. FF has not resolved this tension, and it should not pretend to. The practitioner who reads FF on transformation will find rigorous arguments for patience and rigorous arguments for urgency in the same body of work, and will need to develop their own judgment about which applies to their specific situation.

The second unresolved tension is between insider advantage and insider capture. FF celebrates insiders who lead transformation — their operational intimacy, their knowledge of what actually works, their ability to distinguish between what the organisation says it values and what it actually rewards. But FF also covers cases where the insider is the obstacle — where the founder who knows the business most intimately is also the person most invested in the strategy that is no longer working. Anand Deshpande's honest self-questioning — "Will the company be better off with me or without me?" — is held up as admirable in 'Anand Deshpande's founder's dilemma at Persistent' (2021). But the fact that it required a genuine grappling with suggests how rare this kind of honest insider appraisal is. The tension between insider knowledge and insider capture is not resolved by the evidence — it is sharpened by it.

The third unresolved tension is between the individual and the institutional. FF's transformation coverage contains brilliant accounts of individual leaders who created change — Nilekani, Bedi, Joly — and equally compelling accounts of institutional forces that destroy what individuals build: the short-termism that dismantled Crotonville, the organisational culture that resists agility, the governance structures that preserve the old way against the new leader's best efforts. FF has not fully resolved the question of how much transformation depends on individuals and how much depends on institutions — and the honest answer is probably that it depends on the moment in the transformation, with different answers at different points in the arc.

The fourth tension is the most personal: between the value of slowness as thinking and the experience of working in organisations that reward speed as performance. FF argues, consistently and with evidence, that the slow fix is more effective than the quick fix. But the career incentives of almost every Indian professional environment reward the quick fix — the visible win, the fast turnaround, the confident answer. The practitioner who absorbs FF's argument for slowness in thinking will need to find ways to protect that slowness within structures that penalise it. FF names this tension but does not resolve it.

---

**The Missing Voices**

The most significant gap in FF's transformation coverage is the non-urban, non-English-speaking practitioner. The transformations covered — Aadhaar, Persistent, Tata Sons, Cognizant, the WFH debate — are all urban, largely English-speaking, largely centred on the knowledge economy. The transformation challenges faced by a family business in Rajasthan, a public sector bank branch in Odisha, a cooperative in Maharashtra, a government school system in Bihar — these are almost entirely absent. And because they are absent, the principles FF develops from its evidence base may not generalise to contexts where the institutional environment, the talent availability, the infrastructure, and the cultural norms are substantially different from the ones the evidence is drawn from.

The frontline employee voice is also largely missing. FF's transformation coverage is primarily the view from the top and the middle — CEOs, HR leaders, founders, strategists. The experience of the person whose job is being transformed — whose skills are being declared obsolete, whose daily work is being reorganised, who is being asked to unlearn things that took years to learn — appears mainly as data (resistance to change, engagement scores, attrition rates) rather than as first-person account. The Kavi Arasu WFH pieces are a partial exception — they try to capture the texture of lived experience — but the frontline is largely unheard.

Women's experience of transformation is another significant gap. The WFH coverage occasionally notes that the domestic burden of working from home falls disproportionately on women — the chai being asked for while the professional call is in progress is a specifically gendered observation — but this is not developed into a sustained analysis. The question of how transformation programmes, which typically assume a certain kind of career continuity, interact with the reality of interrupted careers, caregiving responsibilities, and gender-based organisational dynamics is substantially unexplored in FF's transformation coverage.

The regulatory and policy perspective from inside — not Rajan's external assessment of state capacity, but the account of the bureaucrat or regulator who is trying to manage transformation within a government institution — is almost entirely absent. The Nilekani story is told from the outside, as a management success story. The story of what UIDAI looked like from inside the bureaucratic system it had to work within — the resistance, the accommodation, the deals made — is not told.

---

**What This Means For You**

**The belief to update:** You probably believe that your transformation is primarily a technology or strategy problem — that if you get the platform right, or the org structure right, or the roadmap right, the rest will follow. The evidence says this is wrong. In 'Play to your strengths' (2019), Founding Fuel cites the consistent finding that transformation fails not because the technology fails, not because the strategy is wrong, but because the unlearning does not happen. The people who need to think differently do not think differently. The culture that needs to shift does not shift. Technology and strategy are the visible architecture. Culture and unlearning are the foundation. If you are spending more time on your platform selection than on your learning architecture, you have the priorities inverted.

**The thing to stop:** Stop bringing in consultants to lead your transformation and then managing them from a distance. This is not a criticism of consultants — some are excellent. It is a criticism of the pattern in which the outsider's framework becomes the substitute for insider judgment rather than the sharpener of it. As 'Play to your strengths' (2019) makes clear, the consultants who apply "one-size-fits-all solutions in the name of best practices" are not failing because they lack knowledge. They are failing because they lack operational intimacy — and you cannot compensate for that by adding more slides to the deck. If your organisation's transformation is primarily being designed and driven by people who do not work there, you have already made the most important mistake.

**The thing to start:** Build one feedback loop — one explicit mechanism for learning from your implementation — before the end of this month. Not an annual review. Not a post-mortem after the programme ends. A regular, structured, low-stakes conversation between the people designing the transformation and the people experiencing it, with the explicit purpose of updating the plan based on what is being learned. This is the do-learn-do cycle in its most minimal form. It costs almost nothing. It changes everything about whether the transformation you are attempting is building toward something real or building toward a slide deck. Nilekani did not succeed at UIDAI because he had a better plan than anyone else. He succeeded because he had a better feedback loop.

The conviction to carry out of this page: transformation is not what you do to your organisation — it is what your organisation becomes capable of doing to itself, continuously, without a programme to justify it.
