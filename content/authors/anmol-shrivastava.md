# Anmol Shrivastava: Intellectual Profile

## Who Is This Person

Anmol Shrivastava occupies an unusual position in Founding Fuel's contributor ecosystem. He is neither a professional journalist nor a pure practitioner, but something closer to an informed generalist with an investigative disposition — a systems analyst who writes. His work spans electoral politics, pandemic preparedness, agricultural supply chains, AI productivity, and aviation disaster investigation, a range that would seem scattered if not for the consistent analytical frame he applies across all of it. What he brings that other FF contributors typically do not is a willingness to descend from the conceptual to the granular at the exact moment the argument demands it: from WHO transmission policy to a specific 3M mask SKU, from AI adoption principles to the precise Zoom feature you should enable before your next panel. He is also, across this body of work, increasingly willing to make himself the subject of the inquiry — not as confession for its own sake, but because his own errors and discoveries are frequently the most persuasive data points he has.

## Dominant Thesis

The single idea Shrivastava returns to across eight articles spanning six years can be stated simply: what we think we know is usually less than what is actually true, and the cost of that gap is paid in real systems. In "The Virus Has Upgraded Itself," the discovery is that WHO and CDC quietly reversed their position on aerosol transmission without announcement. In the AI 171 series ("The Last 19 Minutes," "The Many Threads We Must Untangle," "The Search for What Really Went Wrong," all 2025), the recurrent observation is that "establishing the 'what' is often far easier than the 'why.'" In "AI's Best-Kept Secret" (2025), he distinguishes between surface-level AI output — "standard boilerplate stuff, their names, designation and a one-line bio" — and genuinely inferential, contextualised intelligence. The pattern is invariant: received knowledge is incomplete; deeper inquiry reveals hidden architecture; and the people and institutions that resist deeper inquiry impose costs on everyone downstream.

## India Lens

Shrivastava's India is primarily an institutional India — he writes about what the Indian state can and cannot do, rather than about poverty, caste, linguistic diversity, or rural-urban migration as ends in themselves. His India lens is most precisely described as *state capacity under stress*, with a particular interest in the tension between central and local institutions. In "The Satara Uprising" (2020), the local administration under Satara's municipal commissioner innovates directly and concretely while "the state's political establishment tried to draw the attention of the Centre" and received "no proper response." In the AI 171 series, Indian aviation investigative institutions — AAIB, DGCA — are put under the lens of international scrutiny, with the NTSB chair's public call for transparency rendered as a kind of civilisational test: can India's investigative infrastructure meet global standards when the stakes are catastrophic and the watching world is impatient? He notices how India's PPE market was flooded with counterfeits during the pandemic, how Air India's transformation under Tata placed "national institutional credibility" in the global spotlight, and how local agricultural entrepreneurs in Maharashtra adapted faster than the systems theoretically designed to support them. His India, consistently, is one where local improvisation compensates for central opacity.

## Themes

Shrivastava contributes most substantially to three interlocking theme clusters. In **Leadership and Organisational Culture**, his BJP analysis ("The BJP's Real Brahmastra," 2019) is not political commentary but organisational anatomy — how a movement builds narrative coherence, aligns workers around a singular figure, and converts emotional investment into electoral machinery. In **Technology and Digital Disruption**, his AI work is distinguished by a focus not on AI's capabilities but on the quality of human preparation required to make those capabilities useful. In **India-Specific Challenges and Opportunities**, the Satara piece and the AI 171 series together constitute his most original contribution — a portrait of Indian institutions under exceptional pressure, sometimes rising (Satara's local administration, the farmers who became entrepreneurs) and sometimes struggling against inherited opacity (AAIB's communication failures, the Centre's non-responsiveness to Maharashtra). His engagement with **Policy, Governance and Regulation** is real but largely implicit — he observes institutional failures without constructing normative frameworks for reform. **Science and First Principles** appears in the Covid and AI 171 work, specifically in his interest in investigative methodology and epistemic honesty.

## Mental Models

Several frameworks appear with enough regularity to constitute a toolkit. The **cascade or chain-of-failure model** is his most-used analytical structure: in Part Three of the AI 171 series, he explicitly states that "no single clue explains AI 171 in isolation — the truth lies in connecting these events in the right sequence." In Satara, the lockdown disrupted one node in a supply chain, which cascaded into transport failure, which paradoxically opened a direct-to-consumer channel for farmers. The **preparation-as-leverage frame** organises the AI masterclass piece: the value of a tool is determined entirely by the quality of thinking you bring to it, not by the tool itself. The **hero narrative framework**, drawn from Steve Jobs and applied to Narendra Modi in the 2019 BJP piece, is his most portable single construct — great organisational movements require a singular face through which followers project meaning. He also reaches, once explicitly and frequently implicitly, for the **availability heuristic** from behavioural science, invoking it to explain why dramatic visuals distort public understanding of aviation safety. And running beneath all of this is what might be called a **blame-free investigation principle**, derived from ICAO's Annex 13 framework: systems that separate investigation from liability generate more honest information, and that design insight applies far beyond aviation.

## Evolution

From 2019 to 2025, Shrivastava's writing moves through three legible phases. In 2019, he works at distance, using corporate analogies — Apple, Steve Jobs — as scaffolding for analysis of political and technological phenomena. The register is cool and somewhat removed. In 2020 and 2021, he closes the distance dramatically: Satara features named individuals, embedded audio, and a farmer's line in Hindi that does more analytical work than any abstraction could. The Covid piece begins "I was wearing my mask incorrectly" — personal failure as the entry point to useful insight. By February 2025, in "AI's Best-Kept Secret," the confessional mode is fully established: "my attention often wanders during these video/audio formats," "I am more of a reader than a listener." The analytical frame is subordinated to the experiential narrative. Then, in June 2025, the AI 171 series pulls him back toward reported journalism — detached, technical, co-authored with Indrajit Gupta, appropriately grave. The personal essayist voice nearly disappears. The overall trajectory is from macro-analytical to personal-experiential, with a reversion to macro-institutional when the subject demands it. What has not changed is the core scepticism about received knowledge and the practitioner's insistence on actionable conclusions.

## Distinctive Voice

Shrivastava writes differently from most FF contributors in one fundamental way: he positions himself as a user or observer who has just discovered something, and then invites the reader into that discovery rather than presenting himself as an established authority. The February 2025 piece opens with an almost anxious appeal — "hang on! This one's different" — that is his most unguarded editorial moment and arguably his most effective opening. This is not a rhetorical trick. It reflects a genuine epistemic stance: he is usually writing about something he has recently worked out, not something he has long believed. The emotional register across most of the work is curious and practical — not alarmed, not celebratory. The Satara piece is his warmest writing, genuinely moved by Nikam's forced entrepreneurialism. The AI 171 series is his most sober, matching the subject. The Covid piece is urgent but controlled: institutional outrage compressed into a single sentence before pivoting to specific guidance. He is capable of lightness, particularly in the February 2025 AI piece, but it never tips into the breezy.

## What They Are Skeptical Of

Shrivastava is persistently sceptical of institutional communication — not aggressively, but consistently. WHO, CDC, AAIB, the Indian central government: all are noted for choosing opacity over transparency at moments when transparency would have served the public interest. He is equally sceptical of mono-causal explanations for complex outcomes, resisting the attribution of BJP's 2019 victory to any single factor and insisting throughout the AI 171 series that no single failure explains the crash. He is sceptical of boilerplate AI use — the whole argument of "AI's Best-Kept Secret" is that surface-level adoption produces surface-level results. And he is quietly but unmistakably sceptical of electoral methods that may not, as he carefully puts it, "pass muster on ethical grounds" — a hedge that signals discomfort without committing to critique.

## Where They Fit in FF's Worldview

Shrivastava aligns most directly with Founding Fuel's interest in entrepreneurship as compelled adaptation rather than startup formation — his Satara piece is perhaps the purest expression of this ethos in FF's recent output. He also reflects FF's commitment to systems thinking and its interest in how institutions navigate transformation. Where he adds something distinctive is in his investigative journalism mode, most fully developed in the AI 171 series: FF rarely publishes multi-part reported investigations of this technical depth, and Shrivastava and Gupta together demonstrate that the publication can sustain that form. His work on AI tools similarly extends FF's conversation in a practical direction that complements the more conceptual AI writing the platform typically features. Where he diverges slightly is in his relative disinterest in the entrepreneurial ecosystem as such — he writes about organisations and institutions more than about founders and markets.

## Best Work

Five articles represent Shrivastava at his sharpest. "The Satara Uprising" (2020) is his most complete piece — reported, humanly specific, analytically clear, and genuinely surprising in how it reframes agricultural crisis as entrepreneurial emergence. "The Virus Has Upgraded Itself" (2021) demonstrates his most effective persuasive structure: institutional failure identified, personal failure admitted, practical guidance provided, all in tight sequence. "AI's Best-Kept Secret" (February 2025) is his most original methodological contribution — the specific reframing of AI as preparation tool rather than retrieval tool is a genuinely useful idea, and the personal voice makes it stick. Of the AI 171 series, Part Three ("The Search for What Really Went Wrong") is the strongest individual instalment — the chain-of-failure logic is most fully developed here, and the explicit articulation of blame-free investigation culture as a design principle elevates the piece beyond crisis journalism. Together, these represent the range of his capabilities: the field reporter, the practical adviser, the investigative analyst.

## Memes and Jokes

A Shrivastava meme would be the iceberg image, captioned: "What WHO told you in March 2020 / What WHO quietly changed their mind about in October 2020 without telling you." A second meme: the classic "task failed successfully" format, except it reads "Read the briefing doc / Didn't read the briefing doc / Used ChatGPT to summarise it / Used ChatGPT to *predict what they'll say* — task succeeded differently." A third: the spider-man pointing meme, where both figures are labelled "local administration" and "crisis forcing innovation," with the caption "one of you called the Centre, by the way — no response." A fourth: the scene from *Whiplash* where Fletcher asks "Was that in time?" recast as an aviation investigator asking AAIB "Did you confirm black box recovery?" — pause — "Did you confirm it *publicly*?" The fifth captures his BJP analysis best: the Lord of the Rings image of the Eye of Sauron, captioned "it's not about ideology, it's about narrative architecture and the relentless gaze of a single organizing face." Each of these reveals something real: his institutional scepticism, his tool-as-leverage thinking, his affection for local improvisation, his interest in whether institutions perform their accountability functions, and his preference for structural explanation over ideological attribution.

## Intellectual Allies and Adversaries

Shrivastava would find natural intellectual companionship with Charles Perrow, whose *Normal Accidents* theory holds that complex systems coupled tightly enough will inevitably fail in cascades — a structural argument for the chain-of-failure model Shrivastava reaches for throughout the AI 171 series. He would agree with Atul Gawande on the gap between what practitioners know and what systems communicate — Gawande's work on surgical checklists and institutional knowledge transfer echoes Shrivastava's insistence that better preparation rather than better talent is usually the lever. He would align with Nassim Taleb's critique of institutional forecasting and overconfidence, though Shrivastava lacks Taleb's belligerence. He would find common cause with Diane Vaughan, whose Challenger launch decision analysis pioneered the "normalisation of deviance" concept that underpins the AI 171 investigation's logic. His intellectual adversaries would include Malcolm Gladwell — not because Gladwell is wrong about the power of narrative, but because Gladwell's mono-causal storytelling ("the tipping point," "the outlier") runs directly against Shrivastava's insistence that complex outcomes require multi-factor chain analysis. He would resist any analyst who attributes electoral outcomes to a single variable, any public health official who updates guidance silently, and any technologist who claims that better tools automatically produce better outcomes.

## Writing and Argument Style

Shrivastava builds arguments by beginning with a discovery — something he has recently learned that contradicts a prior assumption — and then using that discovery as the key to unlock a larger analytical claim. He almost never leads with the conclusion; he leads with the moment of surprise. In "The Virus Has Upgraded Itself," the surprise is the silent WHO reversal. In "AI's Best-Kept Secret," it is the realization that a mundane ChatGPT prompt produced a qualitatively different kind of output when the question was changed. This discovery-first structure is his most consistent rhetorical habit, and it serves a specific function: it positions the reader as co-discoverer rather than passive recipient, which is exactly suited to his subject matter — the gap between what we think we know and what is actually true.

He handles counterarguments implicitly rather than explicitly — he rarely constructs a straw man and then demolishes it. Instead, he represents the conventional position fairly ("no doubt, all of these would have contributed in some measure"), then layers additional evidence until the conventional account appears insufficient. At the sentence level, his writing is clean and compressed. He favours the short declarative sentence after a longer analytical one, using the rhythm to create emphasis. He is drawn to the precise phrase that does double analytical duty: "Corona humko bechna sikha raha hai" is quoted not for colour but because it contains his entire argument about forced entrepreneurship in six words. His most characteristic rhetorical move is the calibrated hedge — "not all of the methods it employed may pass muster on ethical grounds" — which signals awareness without committing to polemic. He is a writer who distrusts both abstraction and excess, and whose best work lives in the space between the structural and the specific.
