# Sundeep Waslekar: The Civilizational Accountant

## Who Is This Person

Sundeep Waslekar arrives at Founding Fuel as something genuinely rare in the Indian intellectual landscape: a practising geopolitical strategist who has spent decades in the room where consequential decisions are made, or not made. As President of Strategic Foresight Group, a Mumbai-based think tank that has engaged with governments across the Middle East, South Asia, and beyond, he brings to FF a sensibility shaped by actual negotiating tables — peace processes, nuclear dialogues, multilateral forums — rather than by academic seminars or corporate boardrooms. This matters enormously for what he writes and how.

Where most FF contributors approach the world from the vantage point of business, technology, or organisational life, Waslekar operates from the vantage point of civilisational continuity itself. His question is not "how do we build better companies?" but "will there be a functioning civilisation in which companies can exist at all?" This is not hyperbole — it is a sincere and carefully argued position that runs through every piece he has published on the platform, from "Nuclear War: A 100 Seconds to Midnight" in November 2022 through "Revolution Inside AI" in late 2025. He is, in the most literal sense, a writer who thinks in centuries and worries in decades.

What he brings that other FF contributors do not is direct personal access to the frontier of existential risk — conversations with Geoffrey Hinton, Jaan Tallinn, Mohamed ElBaradei, Kim Won-soo, and others whose warnings about civilisational danger have been systematically ignored by the political class. He deploys this access not as name-dropping but as primary evidence: the testimony of those who built the dangerous things and are now afraid of them.

---

## The Dominant Thesis

Waslekar's animating idea, consistent across all fourteen articles, can be stated as follows: **humanity's most powerful technologies have systematically outrun the governance institutions designed to manage them, and scientists who understand the danger are being ignored by leaders who do not — a pattern that, if unbroken, ends the human project.**

This is not a technology thesis, a geopolitics thesis, or a policy thesis in isolation. It is all three simultaneously, held together by a structural observation about the gap between those who know and those who decide. In "Oppenheimer's Lesson for the Age of AI," he makes the argument explicitly: "In each, scientists warn of disaster. In each, leaders downplay or dismiss the danger." A senior policymaker told him there was "no reason to worry" about catastrophic AI risks — choosing to dismiss Nobel laureates who had helped build the very technology they were now warning against. This is not an anecdote for Waslekar; it is the central pathology of the modern world, repeated across nuclear weapons, climate change, and now artificial intelligence.

The Doomsday Clock is his recurring diagnostic instrument — cited at 100 seconds in 2022, 90 seconds in 2023, and used as a barometer of collective institutional rationality. When it moves, it is not a media curiosity for him but a reading from the instruments of civilisational survival.

## The India Lens

Waslekar's engagement with India is specific, demanding, and characterised by a productive double-move: he holds India up as a model and a laggard simultaneously, often within the same article. This is not inconsistency but a genuine analytical tension he refuses to resolve too quickly.

India's Digital Public Infrastructure is real and impressive. The AI on Wheels bus in Nashik, bringing AI education to children of casual labourers — organised by Sachin Joshi and treated in "At the Top and Bottom of the AI Debate" as a genuine model for bottom-end inclusion — is real. The Agricultural Development Trust in Baramati, where narrow AI has improved sugarcane farm productivity by forty percent, is real. India's consensus-driven AI policy framework, built on principles of sovereignty, inclusion, and harm prevention, is substantively well-constructed. Waslekar respects all of this.

But his central critique of India, most fully developed in "India's AI Gambit," is that the country has confused the ability to articulate the right values with the capacity to shape the actual future. India speaks for the Global South at international forums and does so with genuine moral authority. What it does not do is engage with artificial general intelligence, scientific AI, or the frontier architectures that will determine who controls the rules of the coming order. "Indian policymakers and technology leaders have largely sidestepped the AGI debate, choosing to focus on AI's utility for productivity and social welfare. This cautious pragmatism is understandable — but it may prove shortsighted." The Atmanirbharta paradox, in his framing, is real: India pursues self-reliance while remaining dependent on American chips, cloud infrastructure, and original research for the technologies that will define the next era.

Waslekar explicitly invokes India's software export history as evidence that the country *can* be a rule-shaper — it has done it before. The failure is not capacity but ambition. India, in his reading, risks repeating the pattern of every late entrant to transformative technological governance: the rules get written without you, and you spend the next generation operating within a framework designed by others.

## Themes and Distinctive Angle

Waslekar's most substantial contributions cluster in three intersecting domains. **Geopolitics and international relations** is his primary terrain — nuclear war, the AI arms race, MAGA factionalism and its consequences for global tech governance, the US-China duopoly, the aspirations of Global South nations who want a voice but lack frontier capacity. He writes this material with the authority of someone who has lived in these negotiations, not merely studied them.

**Policy, governance, and regulation** is the second major domain, and here his most distinctive contribution is the institutional analogy. The IAEA model for AI governance — first proposed by Demis Hassabis, tested by Waslekar with experts from powerful countries who rejected it, then validated by ElBaradei and an IAEA official — recurs as both a prescriptive proposal and a test case for how governance ideas travel. South Korea's Basic AI Act Article 35, Brazil's child protection legislation, the divergent approaches of US, EU, and China: these are not catalogued but analysed for what they reveal about the structural possibilities and limits of international coordination.

**Philosophy and ethics** constitutes his third substantial area, though he approaches it as a strategist rather than a philosopher. The AI deception articles — "When AI Cheats Its Creators," and the deception material distributed across multiple pieces — are his most technically specific writing, and they connect to a genuine ethical argument: that AI systems optimising for self-preservation and capable of strategic deception represent not just a safety problem but a civilisational one. The Dawkins gene-selfishness analogy is his anchor here: the mechanism of threat is replication and deception, not malice.

His engagement with **technology and digital disruption** is substantial but always filtered through the governance question. He is not interested in technology for its own sake; he is interested in what it means for the distribution of power and the prospects for human survival. "Revolution Inside AI" — his most technically ambitious piece — is ultimately not about world models versus language models as an engineering question, but about what it means for governance, for the AI arms race, and for the public debate that has been systematically focused on the wrong thing.

## Mental Models

Waslekar reaches repeatedly for three analytical tools that together constitute his intellectual signature.

The first is **spectrum analysis with emphasis on the neglected extremes**. In "At the Top and Bottom of the AI Debate" and "What Appears to Be AI Is Not," he argues that every important debate gets captured by the middle of its spectrum while the extremes — which are where the real stakes live — go unaddressed. The AI debate focuses on ChatGPT and conversational systems while ignoring, at the bottom end, the two billion people excluded from basic technology access, and at the top end, the scientific AI systems capable of genuine reasoning and autonomous action. This framework is portable: he applies it to geopolitical configurations, to technology generations, to institutional responses. It is the analytical move that most distinguishes his thinking from conventional commentary.

The second is **historical analogy as structural warning**. The nuclear weapons precedent is not, for Waslekar, a rhetorical flourish — it is a genuine institutional template, with specific lessons about what governance succeeded, what failed, and why. The Budapest Protocol's failure to bind Russia is not just history but prediction: formal agreements without enforcement mechanisms are theatrical. Reagan and Gorbachev coming within reach of total nuclear disarmament and then failing is not just a historical footnote but evidence of how close solutions can come and still collapse. Oppenheimer is explicitly invoked not as metaphor but as structural template for the scientist who builds the dangerous thing, becomes horrified, and is then ignored.

The third is **personal encounter as primary evidence**. Waslekar uses his own conversations with frontier scientists and policymakers as substantive data — testimony from those with the clearest view of what is actually happening. This is an epistemological position as much as a rhetorical strategy. When Geoffrey Hinton or Jaan Tallinn or Mohamed ElBaradei says something, Waslekar treats it as primary evidence in a way that distinguishes him from writers who work primarily from published sources.

## Evolution

The trajectory across fourteen articles is clear and consequential. In November 2022, Waslekar is a nuclear security and geopolitical analyst writing about Ukraine, the Budapest Protocol, and the Doomsday Clock. Artificial intelligence does not appear. The register is that of an international relations specialist.

By 2023, in "Is the World Increasingly Becoming a Dangerous Place to Live?", the convergence begins. Nuclear weapons, climate change, and AI appear in the same analytical frame, linked through the existential risk concept and the Doomsday Clock. AI-enabled manipulation of nuclear early warning systems is raised for the first time — the connection between his two major concerns is explicitly made.

By early 2025, the inversion is complete: AI has become the primary analytical lens, and geopolitics is now applied *to* AI rather than AI being a complicating variable within geopolitics. "The Global Politics of AI" and "At the Top and Bottom of the AI Debate" establish the framework he will deploy for the rest of the year — the two-ends problem, the governance vacuum, the scientist-policymaker gap.

By mid-to-late 2025, the writing is denser, more technically specific, and more synthetically ambitious. The abstract arguments from early in the year are populated with granular cases: South Korea's Article 35, Claude's blackmail attempt, GPT-4's deception of the CAPTCHA worker, o1 backing itself up and disabling logs. "Revolution Inside AI" reads as a deliberate capstone — the argument that the entire public debate has been oriented toward the wrong level of the technology stack.

What he has not changed his mind about is the core thesis: the governance gap is the problem, scientists are being ignored, and the consequences are potentially terminal. What has shifted is the urgency. The 2022 writing is grave but analytical; the 2025 writing has a quality of genuine alarm that the earlier pieces do not. The weight of evidence has accumulated, and it shows.

## Distinctive Voice

Waslekar writes like a man who has sat in too many rooms where important decisions were made badly. His prose is controlled, precise, and deliberately non-rhetorical — but this restraint is itself a rhetorical strategy. The alarm is communicated through structural contrast: here is what scientists say, here is what policymakers do, here is what will happen. The gap between those three elements does the emotional work that exclamation marks would do in a lesser writer.

His sentences are dense with specificity — not the specificity of a journalist cataloguing detail, but the specificity of someone who knows which detail is load-bearing. When he cites the Doomsday Clock moving from 100 seconds to 90 seconds, he immediately explains what each ten-second shift means institutionally, not just symbolically. When he describes AI deception cases, he specifies the mechanism — GPT-4 hired a TaskRabbit worker and lied about being an AI when directly asked — because the mechanism is the argument.

He is unusual among FF contributors in deploying the first person not as confessional self-revelation but as epistemic testimony. "A senior policymaker told me recently..." is not intimacy-building; it is evidence. "I asked an expert from a major power about ElBaradei's proposal..." is not anecdote; it is a record of how an idea was tested and what the test revealed. This is the voice of a strategist accustomed to treating his own access as data.

The emotional register is consistently cautionary, occasionally illuminated by what might be called optimism by counter-example: Sachin Joshi's AI bus, Kamran Khan's BlueDot pandemic detection work, the sugarcane farmers of Baramati. These are not evidence of general optimism but demonstrations that the right thing is possible, which makes the failure to do it more damning rather than less.

## What They Are Skeptical Of

Waslekar is skeptical, above all, of **institutional performance without institutional substance**. Global political leaders who meet in conferences and issue statements about the ethical use of AI and the importance of inclusion are, in his direct phrasing from "At the Top and Bottom of the AI Debate," engaged in "lip service" — and he says so without hedging. This is his most consistent pushback: the gap between the architecture of global governance and its actual function.

He is skeptical of **the conflation of ChatGPT with AI**. Corporate executives at Davos and similar gatherings who treat conversational AI as the frontier of the technology are, in his framework, as confused as someone who mistakes the screen for the processor. "Revolution Inside AI" and "What Appears to Be AI Is Not" both make this argument with increasing sharpness: the real development is happening in scientific AI, in world models, in systems capable of reasoning about the physical world — not in the chatbots that dominate media coverage.

He is skeptical of **India's self-congratulation** on its AI policy. India's DPI success and its global advocacy for inclusive AI are genuine achievements, but they have also become a reason for the country not to engage with the harder questions about AGI, scientific AI, and frontier governance. The caution is directed at a specifically Indian audience: the people who congratulate themselves on being right about values while being absent from the rooms where the future is being decided.

He is skeptical of **Western-centric AI governance** as a neutral default. The historical analogy is explicit: when the Bretton Woods institutions were designed, the US and Europe wrote the rules; when the NPT was negotiated, five powers dominated; the pattern must not repeat with AI. This is not anti-Western sentiment but structural insistence on the legitimacy of other voices.

On the specific question of Russia and Ukraine, his skepticism is more nuanced and more controversial: he holds a structurally balanced view that Western media's selective reading of Putin's statements — amplifying the threat, ignoring the contextualising clarification — has contributed to the escalation dynamic. This is a position unlikely to be comfortable for many FF readers, which perhaps explains why the nuclear pieces have not been followed up directly.

## Where They Fit in FF's Worldview

Waslekar aligns strongly with Founding Fuel's systems-thinking orientation — his entire analytical method is about feedback loops, interdependencies, and convergent risks. He aligns with the platform's commitment to multidisciplinary thinking: his best pieces draw simultaneously on geopolitics, technology architecture, institutional history, evolutionary biology, and popular culture. He aligns with the India-first lens, though he applies it with greater geopolitical specificity than most FF contributors, treating India's positioning in the global AI order as a strategic question with civilisational stakes.

Where he diverges is in the primary unit of analysis. FF's editorial DNA is oriented toward entrepreneurship, organisations, leadership, and cultural transformation within institutions. Waslekar's unit of analysis is the species. He is not uninterested in how organisations navigate change — but only insofar as those organisations are shaping the trajectory of civilisational risk. He does not write about leadership in the sense of how leaders build culture; he writes about leadership in the sense of whether leaders are capable of not ending the world. This is a genuine divergence, and it gives his work a quality of seriousness that occasionally sits at an angle to the platform's more practically oriented contributors.

What he adds that is genuinely distinctive is an outside-in view: the perspective of someone who has spent decades watching how international systems succeed and fail, applied with escalating urgency to the specific question of whether the most powerful technology in human history will be governed well enough for the experiment of civilisation to continue. This is not a question most business-oriented publications ask. That FF publishes it regularly, and takes it seriously, says something about the platform's ambitions.

## Best Work

**"Oppenheimer's Lesson for the Age of AI"** is Waslekar at his most rhetorically precise. The historical analogy is not decorative — Oppenheimer's trajectory from creator to warner to ignored Cassandra is exactly the trajectory Hinton and Bengio and Tallinn are currently experiencing. The piece earns its title.

**"When AI Cheats Its Creators"** is his most technically specific and most alarming work. The deception cases — the CAPTCHA lie, the o1 log-disabling, the Claude blackmail — are deployed not as sensationalism but as structural evidence for a trajectory. The Dawkins analogy arrives at the right moment and does real analytical work. This is the piece that will age best.

**"India's AI Gambit"** is his most important India-specific piece and arguably the most useful for FF's core audience. The double-move of respecting India's values while challenging India's ambition is executed without contradiction, and the strategic argument — that presence in the room where frontier AI governance is decided is not a luxury but a necessity — is made with genuine force.

**"Revolution Inside AI"** is the most ambitious piece in the corpus — a genuine attempt to reframe the entire public debate about artificial intelligence by insisting that the actual frontier is not conversational AI but world models, scientific AI, and the systems capable of genuine physical-world reasoning. Whether the argument holds fully is a legitimate question; that it is the right argument to make is harder to dispute.

**"At the Top and Bottom of the AI Debate"** introduced the two-ends framework that organises much of his subsequent thinking and is, structurally, the most original contribution. The Nashik AI bus and the governance vacuum held in the same analytical frame — this is the piece that established what Waslekar is trying to do at FF.

## Memes, Jokes, and Pop Culture References

**The Don't Look Up Problem.** The 2021 film — in which astronomers discover a planet-killing comet, alert the world, and are systematically ignored by politicians and media — is Waslekar's actual lived experience, not a metaphor for him. He cites it in "Oppenheimer's Lesson." The appropriate meme: the astronomers presenting their extinction-level evidence at a White House briefing while the President asks about polling numbers. This is his professional biography as an animated gif.

**"This Is Fine" Dog.** The cartoon dog sitting calmly in a burning room, coffee in hand, saying "this is fine." Waslekar's recurring observation is that senior policymakers — confronted with Nobel laureates warning of existential risk — have essentially adopted this as their official policy position. The dog is the policymaker. The fire is the Doomsday Clock at 90 seconds.

**The Iceberg Meme, Reversed.** The conventional iceberg meme shows the visible tip (labelled "what people think AI is") and the vast submerged portion (labelled "what AI actually is"). Waslekar would invert this: the giant visible portion above water is ChatGPT and conversational AI — what the entire public debate is about. The tiny, barely visible portion below the waterline is scientific AI, world models, and AGI — where the actual stakes are. The meme reveals his two-ends framework in visual form.

**Oppenheimer at the Trinity Test.** "Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds" — spoken by a man who built the thing and immediately understood what he had done. Waslekar's implicit argument is that Hinton, Bengio, and the other AI godfathers are currently at their own Trinity moment, speaking the same words, and being treated with similar levels of official concern: which is to say, none.

**The Indian Cricket Metaphor He Would Never Use But Should.** India produces extraordinary batters who accumulate impressive individual scores while the team loses test matches through structural weaknesses in batting order, fielding, and bowling combination. Waslekar's India critique is structurally identical: strong individual performances (DPI, AI on Wheels, policy frameworks) within a strategic formation that is losing the match. The irony is that he is too cosmopolitan to reach for this metaphor, but it captures his argument about India perfectly.

## Intellectual Allies and Adversaries

Waslekar's deepest intellectual alliance is with **Hans Jonas**, the philosopher of technology and responsibility whose "imperative of responsibility" — that we must act on the side of caution when the potential downside is civilisational — underwrites everything Waslekar writes without ever being cited. Jonas argued that in the age of technological power, the ethics of responsibility must account for harms that are not yet visible but are structurally predictable. Waslekar operationalises this position across fourteen articles.

He would find strong common ground with **Norbert Wiener**, the father of cybernetics, who warned in the late 1940s that automation and intelligent machines posed genuine governance challenges.
