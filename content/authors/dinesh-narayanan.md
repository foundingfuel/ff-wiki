# Dinesh Narayanan: Intellectual Profile

## Who Is This Person

Dinesh Narayanan is a journalist and analyst who brings to Founding Fuel something rare in the platform's roster: the combination of a financial journalist's forensic patience with a foreign policy analyst's structural ambition. His background is evidently in business and economic journalism — the 2015 deep-dive into the National Spot Exchange Limited scandal, "Breaking the Stalemate," reads like someone who spent years covering markets, regulatory bodies, and the unglamorous machinery of Indian capitalism before anyone in the mainstream media was paying attention to commodity exchanges. By 2024 and 2025, his contributions have expanded dramatically in scope, encompassing India's neighbourhood policy, the unraveling of the American-led global order, and the geopolitical consequences of a second Trump presidency. What he brings that other Founding Fuel contributors do not is the specific gravity of a reporter who has spent time in mouldy institutional corners — literal and figurative — combined with an analyst's appetite for civilizational time-scales. He is not a management theorist or a startup ecosystem chronicler. He is a diagnostician of power: how it is organised, how it fails, and what institutional wreckage it leaves behind.

## Dominant Thesis

The one idea Narayanan returns to across a decade of writing, from a Mumbai commodity exchange to the Trump administration's tariff regime, is this: institutions decay when their designers confuse the prevention of crime with the encouragement of human possibility. His 2015 formulation, borrowed approvingly from a Chennai venture capitalist, says it best — Indian regulation was "based on a jail principle; intended to stop criminality, not encourage enterprise." This is not merely a critique of Indian bureaucracy. It is a master frame that he applies, with remarkable consistency, to the Forward Markets Commission, to India's neighbourhood policy, and ultimately to America's post-war multilateral architecture. In each case, the institution was designed for control and containment rather than for the cultivation of productive relationships, and in each case the gap between design intent and actual outcome becomes catastrophic. The jail principle is Narayanan's recurring diagnosis, even when he does not name it as such.

## India Lens

Narayanan's India is never exceptional and never hopeless. It is a state with a specific historical inheritance — socialist regulatory DNA from 1947, incomplete liberalisation from 1991, democratic accountability that simultaneously enables and constrains foreign policy — and he treats that inheritance as a live operating variable, not ancient history. In "Breaking the Stalemate," the oligopolistic linkages between industrialists, administrators, and lawmakers are not symptoms of Indian moral failure; they are rational adaptations to a system that rewarded proximity to state power over productive enterprise. "The closed nature of the economy bred oligopolistic tendencies" — this is structural, not judgmental.

By 2024, the India lens has widened but the logic is the same. In "Fire in the 'Hood," India's neighbourhood crises — Pakistan, Bangladesh, Myanmar, Sri Lanka, the delicate relationships with Nepal and Bhutan — are traced to inadequate investment in political relationships, not to some inherent neighbourhood hostility. India's problem is not that it is surrounded by difficult countries; it is that it pursued a "high global profile" while neglecting the harder, less glamorous work of regional integration. This is consistent with his 2015 argument: India mistakes ambition for strategy, and the gap between declared doctrine and actual institutional investment is where the real story lives.

Most distinctively, Narayanan refuses the triumphalist framing of India as a rising power confidently asserting itself. In "Briefing #4," he writes with deliberate bluntness that "India should be prepared to give a lot and be content with little in return" in its dealings with Trump. The vulnerability is structural — a 3,500 km disputed border with China, dependence on American financial and technological infrastructure, an NSA and home minister who cannot safely enter the United States — and he names it without diplomatic softening. This is India-first journalism in the truest sense: honest about India's actual position, not its aspirational self-image.

## Themes

Narayanan contributes most substantially to three intersecting domains. On **Policy, Governance and Regulation**, he is at his most original — the "jail principle" framework is a genuine analytical contribution, not a borrowed vocabulary, and his reading of how regulatory capture works in practice (the FMC as "a woefully incompetent and rather toothless body") is grounded in reportorial specificity. On **Geopolitics and International Relations**, his 2024–2025 work marks a significant expansion of scope, and his analytical tool — assembling a mosaic of expert voices to map genuine uncertainty rather than resolve it prematurely — is distinctive. On **Economics, Markets and Financial Systems**, the NSEL piece remains his most technically detailed work, a careful excavation of how a commodity exchange could collapse through the intersection of regulatory failure, incentive misalignment, and individual opportunism. His engagement with **Business and Entrepreneurship** is less celebratory than most Founding Fuel contributors; he is interested in the structural conditions under which enterprise is possible or impossible, not in the mythology of the founder. His contributions to **Leadership and Organizational Culture** are implicit rather than direct — he tends to situate leaders within the systems that produce their choices, which is a form of structural leadership analysis.

## Mental Models

Narayanan reaches for three analytical tools with sufficient regularity that they constitute a framework. First, **incentive architecture over individual agency**: Jignesh Shah's decision to hire ex-bureaucrats, India's decision to back Sheikh Hasina, Trump's tariff regime — all are explained as rational responses to incentive structures rather than as moral failures of individuals. Second, **historical periodization as rupture points**: he organises his arguments around inflection moments — 1947, 1969, 1991, 2001, 2025 — and uses them to explain why certain path dependencies are so durable. In "The Year the World Learned to Live Without America," he explicitly ranks geopolitical disruptions, placing Trump's Liberation Day tariffs as "the next most disruptive moment" after 9/11, which is a bold comparative-historical claim. Third, **the stalemate frame**: systems stuck in equilibria that require external shock to shift. This is visible in the title of his 2015 piece and recurs throughout his geopolitical work — India-neighbourhood stalemate, US-China relationship in slow-motion transition, Indian regulatory reform perpetually deferred.

## Evolution

The most significant shift in Narayanan's thinking across this decade is a movement from domestic institutional archaeology to real-time geopolitical intelligence. The 2015 piece required three months of research to reconstruct the 1947–1991 regulatory arc; the 2025 briefings are synthesising expert views on week-by-week developments in the Trump White House. The analytical ambition has scaled outward — from a commodity exchange to a collapsing world order — but the diagnostic logic remains remarkably stable. What has changed is the locus of disruption: in 2015, India's problems were self-generated, rooted in its own regulatory inheritance; by 2025, the disruptions are being imported from Washington and Beijing, and India appears less as an actor shaping its environment than as a state scrambling to manage external shocks. This is not pessimism — Narayanan consistently credits Indian diplomatic competence — but it reflects a genuine shift in how he models India's strategic situation.

## Distinctive Voice

Narayanan writes with what might be called understated urgency. He does not shout; the alarm is structural and cumulative. In "Fire in the 'Hood," the list of neighbourhood crises builds pressure through enumeration — Pakistan, Bangladesh, Myanmar, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan — rather than through rhetorical exclamation. The effect is more alarming than explicit alarm-raising would be. At the sentence level, he favours the plain declarative — "India has made inadequate investments in political relationships" — over hedged diplomatic language. He quotes experts extensively but deploys the quotes with editorial precision, selecting phrases that do his analytical work for him while maintaining the appearance of neutrality. The Kandivali neighbour's sympathy for Jignesh Shah — introduced without irony — reveals an emotional register that is empathetic but unsentimental. He takes seriously the human desire to build without allowing that sympathy to cloud structural analysis. This combination of reportorial granularity and civilizational scope is unusual in the Founding Fuel stable.

## What They Are Skeptical Of

Narayanan is skeptical of several narratives that circulate comfortably in Indian policy and business discourse. He pushes back against the idea that India's neighbourhood difficulties are primarily about hostile neighbours rather than India's own underinvestment in relationships. He resists the triumphalist framing of India as a confident rising power, insisting on the reality of leverage deficits. He is skeptical that personal chemistry between leaders — Modi and Trump's celebrated bonhomie — translates into durable policy outcomes; in "Briefing #4," he historicises that warmth as "distant past" with pointed economy. He is implicitly skeptical of the Jignesh Shah-as-villain narrative, preferring the more uncomfortable conclusion that Shah was a rational actor in a system that made his choices predictable. And he is deeply skeptical of institutions that confuse their founding mandate with their actual function — whether the FMC in 2015 or the American-led multilateral order in 2025.

## Where They Fit in FF's Worldview

Narayanan aligns most strongly with Founding Fuel's commitment to systems thinking and its India-first lens — he is consistently filtering events through the specific texture of Indian institutional history, never importing frameworks wholesale from Western business or policy discourse. His engagement with transitions and transformation is genuine rather than rhetorical; he is not interested in disruption as brand but in the structural conditions that make or break large-scale change. Where he adds something distinctive is in his sustained engagement with the state itself — not as backdrop to entrepreneurship but as the primary actor whose design choices determine whether enterprise is possible. Most Founding Fuel contributors are interested in what entrepreneurs and organisations can do within a given environment; Narayanan is interested in who built that environment and why it has the architecture it does. This gives his work a harder political-economy edge than the platform's typical register, and it is a genuine intellectual contribution.

## Best Work

"Breaking the Stalemate" (2015) is his most complete piece — three months of forensic research condensed into a structural argument about Indian regulatory history that uses the NSEL collapse as its occasion but has ambitions far beyond that specific failure. It is the piece where his voice is most fully formed and where the jail principle framework is most precisely articulated.

"Fire in the 'Hood" (2024) is his most courageous piece — an extended challenge to India's Neighbourhood First doctrine that refuses the comfortable conclusion that India's regional difficulties are someone else's fault. The observation that India backed Hasina "so unequivocally that it looked away as she brutally cracked down on the opposition" is the kind of sentence that requires analytical honesty.

"The Year the World Learned to Live Without America" (2025) is his most ambitious piece — an attempt to periodize a civilizational transition in real time. The claim that Trump's Liberation Day tariffs constitute the second most geopolitically significant moment of the 21st century is a bold historical judgment that will either look prescient or overblown depending on what the next decade brings. That he makes it at all is characteristic.

"Briefing #4" (2025) is his most unsentimental piece — a precise mapping of India's actual leverage position vis-à-vis Trump that functions as a corrective to every op-ed celebrating India's strategic autonomy without accounting for structural constraints.

## Memes and Jokes

If Narayanan were a Twitter account, he would be the one posting: "This is not a leadership failure. This is what a rational actor does when the incentive structure looks like this: [attached: diagram of Indian commodity exchange regulation, 1952-2015]."

His analytical relationship to institutional decay is best captured by the moment in *The Wire* when McNulty realises the entire bureaucratic apparatus exists to protect itself rather than serve its stated function — except Narayanan would note that this is not corruption, it is the logical outcome of a system designed for surveillance rather than service.

He is the person at the geopolitics conference who, when everyone is debating whether India should pivot to multipolarity, raises his hand to ask: "But what is India actually investing in its neighbourhood relationships, in dollars, over the last ten years?" — and the room goes quiet.

His 2025 briefings have the energy of a chess commentator explaining, with genuine admiration for the technical skill, why a player who is down material is going to lose: "India's diplomatic moves are excellent. India also has weak leverage. Both things are true."

His relationship to triumphalist India narratives is best captured by the old Zen saying — except in reverse: before enlightenment, chop wood, carry water; after reading the triumphalist op-ed, chop wood, carry water, and note that India's neighbourhood relationships have deteriorated on every measurable indicator since 2014.

## Intellectual Allies and Adversaries

Narayanan would find the most natural intellectual companionship with **Dani Rodrik** — the economist who has spent a career arguing that development is path-dependent, that institutional design shapes economic outcomes more than policy intentions do, and that the Washington Consensus mistake was ignoring the specific historical architecture of different economies. Narayanan's reading of 1991 as an incomplete liberalisation that opened markets without dismantling the regulatory architecture that made them dysfunctional is essentially a Rodrikian argument applied to India.

He would also ally with **Shyam Saran** — not just because he quotes him approvingly, but because Saran's realist tradition in Indian foreign policy, which insists on honest accounting of India's actual capabilities and relationships rather than its aspirational positioning, maps precisely onto Narayanan's analytical disposition.

His most productive intellectual disagreement would be with **Parag Khanna**, whose civilisational optimism about Asian connectivity and India's rising regional centrality Narayanan would find insufficiently grounded in the actual institutional investments that meaningful connectivity requires. Khanna sees infrastructure and geography as destiny; Narayanan would insist that political relationships and regulatory architecture are the real determinants, and that India has systematically underinvested in both.

He would also push back against **Thomas Friedman's** tendency to see globalisation's institutional architecture as durable and self-reinforcing — the 2025 piece on America's withdrawal from multilateral institutions is essentially a direct refutation of the Friedman thesis that economic interdependence makes institutional retrenchment impossible.

## Writing and Argument Style

Narayanan builds arguments archaeologically. He typically begins in the immediate — a commodity exchange collapse, a neighbourhood in turmoil — and then excavates backward through institutional history to explain why the present outcome was, in retrospect, overdetermined. This is his characteristic persuasion strategy: by the time he has finished the historical reconstruction, the contemporary failure looks not like an anomaly but like the inevitable expression of a design flaw that was always there.

He handles counterarguments not by refuting them but by complicating them. The Kandivali neighbour's sympathy for Shah is not dismissed; it is treated as evidence of a legitimate human intuition about entrepreneurship that co-exists with Shah's legal culpability, and the tension between them is where the real argument lives. In the geopolitical pieces, he assembles experts who disagree — Shyam Saran's neighbourhood neglect thesis alongside those who would point to external disruptions — and lets the reader navigate the tension rather than adjudicating it prematurely.

At the sentence level, his most characteristic move is the plain declarative that contains a structural claim: "India has made inadequate investments in political relationships." There is no hedging, no diplomatic softening, no attribution to help him escape accountability if the claim proves wrong. This plainness is its own rhetorical strategy — it signals that he has done the work and is prepared to stand behind the conclusion. His occasional wryness — "South Africa simply 'marked the US absent' and moved on" — is deployed sparingly, which makes it more effective. He is not performing analysis; he is conducting it.
