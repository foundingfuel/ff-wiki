# D Shivakumar: Intellectual Profile

## Who Is This Person

D Shivakumar arrives at Founding Fuel carrying something most contributors lack: decades of frontline corporate experience inside some of the most consequential consumer businesses of the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries. A former chairman of PepsiCo India and senior Nokia executive, he writes not as an academic synthesising others' field research but as someone who has navigated the exact terrain he analyses — managing large organisations, watching incumbents collapse, inheriting cultural legacies that either enabled or destroyed performance. At Founding Fuel, he functions primarily as a curator-analyst, producing annual year-end book lists and summer reading recommendations across seven years. This format is deceptively modest. What it actually generates is an intellectual autobiography in serial form — a record of what a serious, experienced business mind finds worth attending to, year after year, and why.

## Dominant Thesis

If there is one idea that runs unbroken through all seventeen pieces, it is this: **character is the substrate on which everything else rests.** Not strategy, not capability, not even culture in the abstract — but the accumulated choices that constitute a person's or organisation's character over time. This conviction surfaces in nearly every article. In his 2019 reading list, he flags Tomas Chamorro-Premuzic's distinction between legitimate competence and mere confidence. By 2023, he is citing Adam Grant's line that "your success is not about your natural talent as much as it is about your character." In his 2025 summer list, Stanley McChrystal's "we are who we choose to be and that lies in our character" receives approving emphasis. Shivakumar does not simply endorse this view; he builds an entire analytical superstructure on top of it. Culture fails when character fails. Leadership fails when ego overwhelms character. Incumbents decline when the character of the organisation calcifies into defensiveness and hierarchy. The thesis is not original — it has deep roots in virtue ethics — but its consistent application across business, leadership, and institutional analysis gives his body of work a genuine coherence.

## India Lens

Shivakumar applies an India-specific filter selectively but with sharpening precision over time. His India observations are never ornamental; they tend to be diagnostic. In *12 Deadly Sins That Bedevil Incumbents* (2023), his sharpest analytical piece, he deploys actual data — the CAGR of India's top fifty MNCs against India's GDP CAGR across 2010–2021 — to argue that Indian incumbents have not dramatically outpaced the broader economy, challenging any complacency about domestic corporate performance. His endorsement of Raghuram Rajan and Rohit Lamba's *Breaking the Mould* in 2023 signals a specific view of India's structural challenge: the country needs not just economic reform but a cultural shift toward openness, irreverence, and merit — qualities that hierarchical Indian corporate culture historically resists. His 2023 observation that "India has the maximum number of listed companies in the world at 5,200, however, our governance needs to get better" is precise and pointed, not a generic governance complaint. Over time, Indian business writing earns progressively more space on his lists — Rahul Bajaj's biography (2022), HCL's origin story (2023), Subbarao's *Just a Mercenary?* (2024), BS Nagesh's *Serve* (2025) — reflecting a genuine deepening of interest in Indian institutional history rather than a fashionable pivot. His Nokia-Samsung rivalry is recalled as specifically lived Indian market experience. The India lens is empirical, not nostalgic.

## Themes and Distinctive Angles

Shivakumar contributes most substantially across three domains. In **Leadership and Organisational Culture**, he is the most rigorous and sustained voice on the platform. His annual lists read as a systematic investigation of why organisations succeed and fail — not through strategy but through the human and cultural conditions that enable or undermine execution. He understands culture as load-bearing, not decorative: GE's collapse in *Lights Out* is attributed squarely to hierarchical rigidity; Uber's dysfunction in 2019 is a "winning at any cost" cultural failure with lasting moral consequences. In **Business and Entrepreneurship**, his distinctive angle is the **incumbent's dilemma** — the structural tendency of successful organisations to protect legacy rather than innovate. His *12 Deadly Sins* piece is the most argumentative writing in the entire corpus, a direct rebuttal to an HBR prize-winning article, deploying his own data analysis to challenge the premise that incumbent longevity equals competitive health. He makes a precise conceptual distinction between longevity and thriving that deserves wider circulation. In **Work and Workplace**, his sustained attention to employee engagement — "more than 70% of employees are actively disengaged at work" (2019) — reflects genuine concern rather than HR-speak, anchored in behavioural science from Milkman, Mercurio, and Buckingham. Communication emerges as an intensifying preoccupation: Charles Duhigg's *Supercommunicators* appears on both his summer 2024 and year-end 2024 lists, and by 2024 he declares communication "one of the super skills to have this decade."

## Mental Models

Several frameworks recur across the corpus with enough persistence to constitute genuine analytical equipment. The **finite versus infinite game** distinction (from Sinek, 2019) shapes his view that competitive obsession distorts strategy. Bahcall's **Loonshots** framework — organisations operating in discrete phases, splitting "soldiers" from "artists" — maps onto his persistent concern about how incumbents balance today's execution against tomorrow's innovation. Coyle's **three levels of group excellence** (safety, shared vulnerability, clear purpose) appear in 2018 and return in 2022. His own coinage, **People, Public-Power, Private Partnerships**, surfaces in 2021 as a proposed evolution beyond conventional PPP models — the only moment he introduces a framework rather than borrowing one. In *A Personal Brand is a Must* (2023), he builds a four-level model of personal brand architecture that reflects the precise social hierarchy of corporate India with the confidence of someone who has navigated it firsthand.

## Evolution

The trajectory across seven years moves from **curation toward argument**. The early pieces (2018–2020) are generous, warm, occasionally impressionistic — strong on synthesis, lighter on critique. The pivot comes with *12 Deadly Sins* (2023), where he drops the curatorial posture entirely and writes as a challenger, picking a direct fight with HBR orthodoxy and deploying his own data. Simultaneously, his India-specific content deepens: early lists could have appeared in any global management journal; by 2024–2025, Indian authors and Indian institutional stories hold prominent positions. His engagement with **bad followership** also evolves meaningfully — in 2019 he observes that bad leaders exist and are worth learning from negatively; by 2024, Kellerman's argument that bad followers enable bad leaders suggests he has arrived at a more systemic view of organisational pathology. Communication as a theme intensifies late in the corpus, possibly reflecting a diagnosis of what specifically ails organisations in the post-pandemic period.

## Distinctive Voice

Shivakumar writes in the register of a senior corporate figure who has earned the right to be plain-spoken and occasionally sharp. His sentences are clean and declarative; he rarely over-explains. The warmth is real but controlled — he reserves genuine enthusiasm for books that illuminate what he actually cares about, and the praise is proportional rather than promotional. His most striking rhetorical move is the quiet personal disclosure: "I have seen a number of these in my career. The only thing I learnt from such people was how not to be a leader" (2019). He does not perform vulnerability; he deposits it as evidence. His bluntness about paid awards ("avoid them") and social media reciprocity ("the mutual back-scratching river overflows... and a lot of that is fake") is atypically direct for a figure of his professional standing, and more credible for it.

## What They Are Skeptical Of

Shivakumar reserves his deepest skepticism for four related targets: management jargon and consulting platitudes ("catchy models and phrases" that create "illusions of control"); paid awards and performative professional networking; cultures that mistake winning for purpose (Uber as cautionary tale); and nationalist economic policy as a substitute for genuine competitiveness — Coggan on nationalism (2020) and Zakaria on tariffs (2024) are both cited with strong approval, suggesting a consistent free-trade, interdependence worldview. He is also quietly skeptical of the all-rounder ideal in talent management, endorsing Buckingham and Goodall's argument that impact comes from spiky differentiation, not well-rounded averageness.

## Where They Fit in FF's Worldview

Shivakumar aligns closely with Founding Fuel's core commitments: entrepreneurship as mindset, transformation as the permanent condition of serious organisations, people and culture as the operational infrastructure of strategy. His systems thinking — particularly on incumbency, innovation phases, and cultural decay — maps naturally onto FF's interest in feedback loops and interdependencies. His distinctive contribution to the platform is **epistemic seriousness about bad leadership and institutional decline**. Most management writing, including much that appears on business platforms, is implicitly optimistic — here is the formula, here is what works. Shivakumar spends at least as much analytical energy on failure, decay, and what enablers allow mediocrity to persist. This gives his work a darker, more realistic undertone that complements FF's otherwise forward-leaning editorial instincts.

## Best Work

*12 Deadly Sins That Bedevil Incumbents* (2023) is the standout piece — original argument, empirical grounding, genuine intellectual risk-taking. *Eight Best Books of 2023* is perhaps his most cohesive annual list, where the character-over-competence thesis achieves fullest articulation. *What You Can Do in Uncertain Situations* (2020) offers a rare lyrical moment — the Sullenberger story as a meditation on experience, judgment, and the limits of technology. *Top Ten Books of 2019* is the best of the early lists, capturing his range at full stretch: Sinek on infinite games, Chamorro-Premuzic on bad leaders, Bahcall on innovation phases. *A Personal Brand is a Must* (2023) stands apart as a piece of applied professional sociology — precise, opinionated, grounded in the specific social architecture of Indian corporate life.

## Memes and Jokes

**"The cake is not a lie — the strategy is. Culture ate it."** A riff on the Peter Drucker aphorism, but with Shivakumar's twist: he would never tire of pointing out that GE had excellent strategy documents right up until the culture devoured them whole.

**Samsung vs Nokia, but make it philosophy**: Imagine a TED talk titled "Why I Watched My Competitor Win and What It Taught Me About Character." Shivakumar would give it sincerely, without a single slide about market share.

**"LinkedIn Detected." [Shivakumar squinting meme]**: His line about mutual back-scratching networks and paid awards is essentially a precise taxonomy of everything wrong with professional social media — delivered by someone who clearly finds the whole performance exhausting.

**The Infinite Regress of Coaching**: The moment he recommends *Trillion Dollar Coach* to "every senior manager who has pretensions of being a coach, to realise where his own shortcomings lie" is peak Shivakumar — a book recommendation designed to make you question whether you should be recommending books.

**"I read the same book twice. It earned it."** Duhigg's *Supercommunicators* appears on two separate lists. No explanation offered. The repetition is the argument.

## Intellectual Allies and Adversaries

Shivakumar's deepest intellectual alignment is with **Aristotle's virtue ethics** — the idea that character is built through repeated choices and that excellence is dispositional, not episodic. He would find strong company in **Alasdair MacIntyre**, who argues that virtues only make sense within practices and traditions — consistent with Shivakumar's view that leadership character is forged in institutional context, not in isolation. He is also clearly aligned with **Alex Edmans** — not just because he recommends *Grow the Pie*, but because Edmans' project of resisting glib redistribution arguments in favour of empirically grounded capitalism mirrors Shivakumar's own anti-slogan instincts.

His most productive intellectual disagreement would be with **Gary Hamel**, whose enthusiasm for radical organisational reinvention and "management innovation" rests on assumptions about creative disruption that Shivakumar's incumbency analysis directly challenges. Hamel tends to write as if culture can be redesigned by sufficiently motivated leaders; Shivakumar's twelve deadly sins suggest the contrary — that cultural decay is systemic, sticky, and resistant to top-down redesign. He would also push back against **Tom Peters**' more evangelical management prescriptions, finding the missionary certainty of *In Search of Excellence* precisely the kind of false comfort he warns against.

## Writing and Argument Style

Shivakumar constructs arguments through **accumulated endorsement** rather than explicit syllogism. His persuasion strategy is primarily curatorial — he selects books, quotes lines, and frames significance, allowing the accumulation of evidence to do the argumentative work. This is effective because the pattern across seventeen pieces is unmistakable: you do not need to agree with each individual recommendation to see the coherent worldview that assembles itself across them. When he turns explicitly argumentative, as in *12 Deadly Sins*, he leads with data before analogy before personal experience — a sequencing that reflects corporate presentation training but also genuine methodological caution. He handles counterarguments by attributing them to others (the HBR article, the prevailing consensus) before disaggregating them with specific data. At the sentence level, his distinctive habit is the **dropped qualifier**: "I loved his fact-based ideology in writing this book." "There are many paid awards — avoid them." "A lot of that is fake." The brevity signals conviction. He does not overwrite strong claims; he underlines them by removing the hedges everyone else keeps in place.
