# Sanjay Handu: The Practitioner-Philosopher of Organisational Navigation

## Who Is This Person

Sanjay Handu writes for Founding Fuel as a senior corporate practitioner who has spent decades inside large, globally structured engineering organisations — navigating matrix restructurings, cross-functional politics, and the slow grind of institutional change from the inside. He is not an academic, a founder, or a policy intellectual. He is something rarer in the FF stable: a reflective insider, someone who has survived the machinery of large organisations long enough to understand how they actually work, as opposed to how they present themselves in strategy documents and town halls.

What distinguishes him immediately is his biographical method. Where other FF contributors typically arrive at insight through research, reportage, or conceptual synthesis, Handu arrives through personal archaeology. He digs into his own past — a motocross event in 1980s Bangalore on a Yezdi 250cc, weekly visits to a family-owned sari shop in the suburbs of what he deliberately calls "Bombay," a sudden organisational restructuring that upended his professional world — and treats these memories not as colour but as evidence. The anecdote is not decoration; it is the argument itself. This makes him unusual and, at his best, unusually persuasive.

He brings to Founding Fuel something the platform needs but rarely gets in undiluted form: the accumulated judgment of a practitioner who has been personally wrong, publicly embarrassed, and professionally outmanoeuvred, and who has reflected carefully on each occasion. His authority is not positional or credentialed. It is experiential.

## Dominant Thesis

The single idea that threads through every piece Handu has written for Founding Fuel is this: the most consequential professional skills are the ones that are systematically misnamed, misunderstood, or dismissed as beneath serious consideration. Negotiation is written off as haggling. Organisational politics is stigmatised as dirty. Preparation is treated as anxiety rather than strategy. Influence without authority is seen as a consolation prize for those who lack real power. Handu's project, taken whole, is the rehabilitation of these undervalued competencies — the argument that what looks like soft, informal, or even compromised professional behaviour is in fact the hard core of how organisations actually function.

This is not a contrarian pose. It is a practitioner's recognition that the gap between how organisations describe themselves and how they actually operate is wide, consequential, and rarely discussed honestly. Handu discusses it honestly.

## India Lens

Handu's India is not the India of GDP projections, startup ecosystems, or policy reform — at least not in this body of work. It is the India of lived biography: the Bombay of the 1980s before it became Mumbai, the weekly market where his grandfather negotiated with shopkeepers, the Bangalore motocross circuit where a young man learned that walking the track before racing it was the difference between confidence and catastrophe.

His most striking India-specific move is the argument in "Negotiating from the Streets" that Indian street commerce contains a sophisticated negotiation philosophy that transfers directly — and perhaps superiority — to corporate settings. The sari shop vendor, the market stall operator, the patient haggler who understands that "the negotiator who tires first is likely to give away more in the interest of an early closure": these are not quaint folk figures but skilled practitioners of a discipline that Western business schools charge handsomely to teach in more abstracted form. This is a genuine counter-narrative, an India-first reframing that refuses to position Western frameworks as the origin point and Indian practice as the local adaptation.

Similarly, when he rehabilitates organisational politics in "Politics a Bad Word," he reaches for India's own intellectual heritage: "For a country that has since time immemorial lived and breathed politics in its history and mythology, it is strange how politics is often seen as a negative factor in an organizational environment." The invocation is implicit but unmistakable — the shadow of the *Arthashastra* tradition, of a civilisation that produced sophisticated statecraft long before Machiavelli. Handu uses India's depth to challenge the squeamishness of a corporate culture that has imported its professional norms from elsewhere and in doing so amputated its own indigenous wisdom.

## Themes

Handu's contribution clusters most substantially around **Leadership and Organisational Culture** and **Work, Workplace, and Employment**. These are not peripheral concerns in his writing — they are the entire territory. Every article in this batch is, at its heart, a meditation on how individuals navigate institutional structures that were not designed with their flourishing in mind.

Within Leadership, his distinctive angle is the rejection of authority-dependence. The progression he describes in "Maze Runner" — from "command-and-control" to "collaborate-coax-convince" — is not presented as loss or as the unfortunate consequence of matrix complexity. It is presented as evolution, as the development of a more sophisticated and ultimately more durable form of professional effectiveness. This puts him in productive tension with leadership writing that fetishises positional authority or that treats influence-without-authority as a workaround rather than the main event.

His engagement with **Organisational Culture** is most acute in "The Emperor's New Clothes," where he identifies what might be his most analytically original contribution: the structural pathology he calls the "process worm," whereby processes designed to serve transformation objectives metastasise into ends in themselves, consuming the very objectives they were created to advance. This is systems thinking in a compact and practically useful form.

**Philosophy and Ethics** appears fleetingly but significantly. In "Politics a Bad Word," he explicitly brackets the ethics of political behaviour — "for the sake of this article, let's assume that the ends are ethical and for the greater good" — and then proceeds to build his case on purely instrumental grounds. This is a deliberate rhetorical choice that creates a latent tension he parks rather than resolves. The question of whether political skill is a neutral tool or an ethically freighted one haunts his argument without being answered. It is the most intellectually unresolved moment in the batch, and arguably the most interesting.

Notably absent: entrepreneurship, technology, policy, macroeconomics, geopolitics. Handu is writing squarely in the register of corporate professional development, and there is no apology for this narrowness. He goes deep where others go wide.

## Mental Models

The most consistent mental model across Handu's work is the **preparation-confidence-performance loop**, introduced most explicitly in "Racing Track" and recurring in modified form across almost every subsequent piece. His argument is not simply that preparation improves outcomes through better information — that would be trivially true. His deeper claim is that preparation changes the *internal state* of the actor: walking the motocross track before racing it does not just tell you where the corners are; it builds the psychological confidence that enables you to commit at speed when the moment comes. Preparation is as much about managing one's own cognition as about managing external variables.

A second persistent model is **relationship as infrastructure**. In "Negotiating from the Streets," he argues that "if every negotiation is a conversation then its foundation is a relationship — even if transactional." In "Politics a Bad Word," he describes relationships as load-bearing structures that enable professional objectives to be pursued at all. Relationships are not social niceties, decorative or optional; they are the substrate through which work actually gets done.

The third model is what might be called **label toxicity** — the observation in "Politics a Bad Word" that "labels limit flexibility and leveraging of relationships towards one's objectives." Classifying colleagues as permanent friends or permanent enemies is emotionally satisfying and cognitively cheap, but strategically catastrophic. "A dogged opponent can also be an ally on a different issue, just as a staunch supporter can withdraw on a new initiative." This is a clean formulation of coalition theory applied to everyday organisational life.

## Evolution

Within this five-article batch, there is a discernible trajectory from the personal-biographical toward the organisational-analytical. "Racing Track" (June 2015) operates almost entirely through metaphor — the motorcycle is the vehicle for everything, including the argument itself. "Negotiating from the Streets" (August 2015) retains the autobiographical frame but begins engaging with technique more systematically. "Politics a Bad Word" (December 2015) is conceptually more ambitious, attempting to rehabilitate an entire category of professional behaviour rather than illuminate a single skill. "The Emperor's New Clothes" (March 2016) is the most structurally analytical piece, naming organisational pathologies directly rather than approaching them only through personal story. "Maze Runner" (May 2016) brings synthesis — personal experience and structural analysis held together in a single frame.

The movement can be described as: **metaphor → technique → concept → structural critique → synthesis**. Handu is progressively more willing to name dysfunction, to move from "here is what I learned" to "here is what organisations persistently get wrong." He has not changed his mind about anything fundamental in this period, but he has become more confident — and more analytically precise — in articulating it.

## Distinctive Voice

Handu writes with a warmth and quiet confidence that is recognisably different from the evangelical urgency common in much business writing, including some that appears on Founding Fuel. The emotional register is avuncular: a senior practitioner sharing hard-won lessons without preachiness, without the performance of urgency, without the implicit promise that reading this article will transform your career. He is self-deprecating at key moments — "much to my embarrassment and the onlookers' amusement" ("Racing Track"), "I would dissolve into giggles" ("Emperor's New Clothes") — which has the effect of disarming any impression of seniority-as-authority. He earns the right to instruct by demonstrating that he has also been wrong.

His sentences are clear and unhurried. He does not reach for jargon. When he coins a phrase — "the process worm," "collaborate-coax-convince," "the ghosts of promises past" — it sticks because it earns its concision through the argument that precedes it. He is a better writer than he may know he is.

## What They Are Skeptical Of

Handu is consistently skeptical of the official accounts organisations give of themselves — and particularly of the missionary communication style that large organisations adopt when launching transformation initiatives. "Build the hype around new initiatives with care," he writes in "Emperor's New Clothes." "Too little and there won't be enough momentum to make progress; too much and the organization could face a credibility gap in future." This is a direct, measured challenge to the evangelical over-promising that is endemic to corporate change management, and it comes with the implicit authority of someone who has watched it fail repeatedly.

He is skeptical of process fetishism — the "process worm" that mistakes procedural compliance for actual progress. He is skeptical of the friend-or-foe binary in professional relationships, and of the cultural squeamishness about organisational politics that, in his view, merely drives political behaviour underground without reducing it. And he is implicitly skeptical of the idea that technical or functional excellence alone constitutes a career strategy, consistently arguing that political astuteness, negotiation skill, and multi-domain exposure are not optional supplements but essential architecture.

## Where They Fit in FF's Worldview

Handu aligns with Founding Fuel's commitment to people and culture as the substrate of strategy, and to the idea that transformation requires not just structural redesign but the development of individual capability and judgment. He is entirely in step with FF's interest in how organisations navigate change, and with the platform's preference for insight grounded in Indian experience rather than imported wholesale from Western frameworks.

What he adds that is distinctive — and that diverges from the platform's more typical register — is the absence of urgency about disruption, technology, or competitive threat. He is writing from inside large, established organisations, not at the frontier of the startup ecosystem. His concerns are durational rather than urgent. This gives his work a quality of steadiness that is unusual at FF, and that serves as a useful counterweight to the platform's more entrepreneurial and forward-looking contributors. He is the voice that says: the fundamentals of human professional behaviour do not change as quickly as we think, and the lessons of the sari shop still apply in the global matrix.

## Best Work

"The Emperor's New Clothes" is his sharpest piece — the one where he moves furthest from biographical comfort and most clearly names a structural pathology. The "process worm" coinage alone is worth the read.

"Politics a Bad Word" is his most conceptually ambitious rehabilitation effort and contains his densest articulation of the relationship-as-infrastructure model. The explicit bracketing of ethics, while unresolved, is intellectually honest in a way that most corporate writing is not.

"Negotiating from the Streets" is his most distinctively Indian piece — the argument that the Bombay market is a better school for negotiation than the business school is quietly radical, and the writing is at its most evocative.

"Racing Track" and "Maze Runner" round out the picture, the former as the purest expression of his preparation-confidence model, the latter as his most direct engagement with the structural experience of global corporate life as lived from the India end.

## Memes and Jokes

The image of Handu on his Yezdi 250cc, walking the Bangalore motocross track in his helmet before the race while other competitors rev their engines impatiently, is essentially his entire philosophy rendered in a single scene. He is, in every piece, the person who already walked the track.

If Handu were a kitchen appliance, he would be a pressure cooker: slow, patient, and building quiet force before the lid comes off.

In "Politics a Bad Word," his advice to stop labelling colleagues as permanent friends or enemies would translate perfectly to a meme of Thanos saying "perfectly balanced" — except Handu's version is about coalition management, not cosmic annihilation. The point is the same: rigid categories are the enemy of flexibility.

He is the colleague who, when the VP of Sales walks into a botched pitch, has already thought through three versions of how this conversation could go and mapped the relationships in the room. He is also the colleague who will later admit, in print, that he once got this completely wrong.

The "process worm" deserves its own emoji: a worm eating a document labelled "original objective," producing a document labelled "compliance report." It is the most quotable coinage in this batch and the most accurate diagnosis of a dysfunction that afflicts every large organisation that has ever run a transformation programme.

## Intellectual Allies and Adversaries

Handu would find a natural ally in **Robert Cialdini**, whose work on influence and persuasion maps closely to Handu's own preoccupation with achieving outcomes without formal authority — particularly Cialdini's insistence that influence is a learnable, ethical discipline rather than manipulation. Both thinkers share a commitment to the idea that understanding how people actually make decisions, rather than how they say they do, is the foundation of effective professional practice.

He would align with **Kautilya** more than he explicitly acknowledges. The *Arthashastra*'s treatment of political skill as a neutral instrument of statecraft, to be evaluated by ends rather than stigmatised by association with power-seeking, is precisely the argument Handu makes in "Politics a Bad Word." The bracketing of ethics ("let us assume the ends are good") is a recognisably Kautilyan move.

His most productive intellectual tension would be with **Gary Hamel**, whose corporate transformation evangelism — the rhetoric of revolution, urgency, and creative destruction — is almost precisely what Handu's "Emperor's New Clothes" warns against. Hamel's case for building momentum through bold vision would, in Handu's framework, risk generating exactly the credibility debt that makes future change harder. Handu would not dismiss Hamel but would insist that the morning-after problem of over-promised transformation deserves at least as much attention as the inspiration problem.

He would be genuinely skeptical of **Clayton Christensen's** disruptive innovation framework, not because it is wrong about technology markets but because it has been applied so indiscriminately as a management philosophy that it has itself become a source of institutional hype — the very pathology Handu diagnoses. He would recognise the "process worm" at work in the way disruption theory gets deployed in corporate strategy workshops.

## Writing and Argument Style

Handu constructs arguments through biographical accumulation rather than logical deduction. He does not state a thesis and then marshal evidence; he tells a story, then another story, then offers a turn — a moment of reframing — and the insight emerges from the contrast between how things appeared and how they turned out. His persuasion strategy is the gradual erosion of the reader's default assumption through the weight of accumulated personal testimony.

He handles counterarguments obliquely rather than directly. He rarely names the position he is pushing back against in explicit terms; instead, he represents it through the naive version of himself that appears at the start of each story ("I had not appreciated...," "much to my embarrassment..."). The strawman is autobiographical rather than hypothetical, which makes it simultaneously more honest and more effective.

At the sentence level, he is fond of the paradox-compressed-into-aphorism: "The negotiator who tires first is likely to give away more in the interest of an early closure." "A dogged opponent can also be an ally on a different issue." "Competition is the essential friction that brings forth progress and performance." These sentences carry more intellectual weight than their apparent simplicity suggests, and they tend to appear at the end of a paragraph as the crystallised residue of a longer narrative passage — which is exactly the right place for them.

His one consistent rhetorical limitation is the ethical bracket. When he parks a difficult question — most visibly in "Politics a Bad Word," where he explicitly suspends judgment on the ethics of political behaviour to make his instrumental case — he is making a legitimate rhetorical choice, but it leaves the reader with an unresolved resonance that his subsequent paragraphs do not address. It is the place where the practitioner's instinct to solve a problem collides with the philosopher's obligation to sit with its full complexity. He consistently chooses the former. It is a reasonable choice. It is also the choice that keeps his work in the register of the useful rather than the profound.
