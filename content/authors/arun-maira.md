# Arun Maira: Intellectual Profile

## Who Is This Person

Arun Maira arrives at Founding Fuel as something genuinely rare in Indian public intellectual life: an insider-critic with institutional credibility on both sides of the establishment divide. He chaired the Boston Consulting Group in India, spent a decade at Arthur D. Little, served as a member of the Planning Commission under Manmohan Singh, and participated in the World Economic Forum's India scenario planning exercises. He was, in other words, present in the rooms where decisions were made — and he spent much of his subsequent writing life examining why those decisions so often failed.

What distinguishes him from other FF contributors is not just his seniority but his refusal to treat his own positions as settled. His columns are argumentative in the precise sense: he is working something out on the page, not delivering verdicts. He brings to FF the perspective of someone who has watched institutional promises made and broken at close range, and who has the intellectual tools — systems thinking, scenario planning, Argyrian epistemology, institutional economics — to diagnose why.

---

## Dominant Thesis

The single idea Maira returns to across 45 articles, in varying registers and with accumulating urgency, is this: *the dominant frameworks for measuring and organizing human activity — GDP growth, shareholder primacy, specialist optimization — systematically produce the problems they claim to solve, because they treat symptoms in isolation rather than the system as a whole.*

This is not a conventional left critique of capitalism, nor a technocratic call for better data. It is a deeper epistemological argument: that reductionist problem-solving, applied to complex adaptive systems, produces fixes that backfire. Jobs disappear while growth statistics improve. Poverty falls while inequality widens. Corporations become more efficient while democracy becomes less functional. The parts are optimized; the whole deteriorates.

His prescription follows from this diagnosis: the solution requires the recovery of integrative thinking — what he calls, reaching for a medical metaphor, the general physician who sees the whole patient rather than the specialists who each optimize a subsystem.

---

## India Lens

Maira's India is specific and structural. He keeps returning to the same coordinates: the employment elasticity collapse (from 0.44 to 0.01 in successive five-year periods straddling 2004–05, which he calls "close to jobless growth"), the approaching water crisis, the fragility of migrant labor revealed by COVID, the regulatory burden crushing small manufacturers, and the persistent gap between GDP growth and human development outcomes.

His historical lens is anchored in 1991. The liberalization reforms are acknowledged as generating real consumer benefit and poverty reduction, but his recurring argument is that they also produced jobless growth, rising inequality, and institutional atrophy — precisely because they were implemented as ideology rather than evidence. The Planning Commission period (2009–2014), when he was a member, functions as his reference point for what good governance process might look like, and its abolition registers as a significant institutional loss.

The WEF 2005 scenario planning exercise — which produced the three-scenario framework of *Flotilla Advances*, *Muddling Along*, and *Falling Apart* — is his predictive infrastructure for India. He cites it across multiple articles and validates it retrospectively: the forecasted decline from 9% to 6% growth, absent institutional improvement, arrived on schedule. CK Prahalad's 2002 challenge about India@2022 is his moral benchmark: when he writes the India@75 piece, the verdict is that the challenge has not been met.

What gives his India writing its distinctive texture is the combination of macro-structural analysis with autobiographical grounding. His father's small factory in Govindpuri — the inspector culture, the regulatory burden, the time diverted from production to compliance — is not merely illustrative; it is the experiential anchor for his arguments about the ease of doing business for the genuinely small rather than the politically connected. His description of his Mashobra neighbors — the cowherd, the carpenter who wants to emigrate to America, the unskilled assistant who doesn't know to turn a screwdriver clockwise — enacts the cognitive and social distance between classes that no GDP statistic captures.

## Themes

Maira's most substantial contributions fall across four intersecting domains.

In **Policy, Governance and Regulation**, he is at his most analytically rigorous, particularly on employment and institutional design. His framing of India's jobs problem as a "5C problem — a Complicated Condition Created by Combinations of Causes" — is precisely the kind of systems diagnosis that resists political capture. His engagement with Singapore's model of "economies of scope" (coordination closer to the ground rather than centralized scale) represents his most developed alternative to both statist planning and market fundamentalism.

On **Philosophy and Ethics**, he makes his most original contributions. The "Ladder of Business Responsibility" — five rungs ascending from shareholder accountability to the political health of society — is his own framework, born from a post-Gujarat riots facilitation exercise he led with Indian business leaders. His sustained engagement with the capitalism-versus-democracy tension, culminating in the ECG (Economics, Capitalism, Governance) diagnostic model, is the most systematized expression of his intellectual project.

In **Work, Workplace and Employment**, the emotional register intensifies over time — from policy concern in 2015 to moral indictment in "The Used and Discarded Workers of India" (2020), where COVID's exposure of migrant labor fragility becomes an indictment of employer mentality and not merely policy failure.

On **Leadership and Organizational Culture**, his consulting experience gives him unusual authority. The McKinsey autopsy, the Pascale story about knowing when a consultant's job is truly done, the contrast between JRD and Ratan Tata eras as culture comparison — these are not theoretical but observed. His concept of "theories-in-use" versus "espoused theories" (drawn from Argyris) is his most precise analytical tool in this domain.

He contributes less to **Technology and Digital Disruption**, **Consumer Behaviour**, and **Brand and Marketing** — not because he ignores them, but because his interest is always in the systemic effects of technology rather than its internal dynamics. Aadhaar interests him as a governance question; automation interests him as a labour question; the internet interests him as a democracy question.

## Mental Models

Maira's intellectual toolkit is identifiable and consistent. Scenario planning, drawn from the Royal Dutch Shell methodology and refined through the WEF India exercises, is his primary framework for holding uncertainty without collapsing into either optimism or despair. The three-scenario structure — with its distinct driving force configurations — disciplines his thinking about India's possible futures without forcing false prediction.

The "General Physician metaphor" recurs at least three times: the integrative thinker who sees the whole system versus the specialists who each optimize a subsystem. This is both a critique of policy fragmentation and an implicit self-description — Maira positions himself as the missing GP.

Argyris's distinction between espoused theories and theories-in-use is his epistemological signature, applied equally to economists defending GDP metrics, corporations claiming social purpose, and governments invoking democratic values. It is a tool for exposing the gap between stated principles and actual behaviour, without requiring bad faith on anyone's part — just unexamined habit.

Fernand Braudel's *longue durée* — surface waves, deeper currents, underlying tides — appears explicitly in "Listening to Them" and informs his treatment of Brexit, Indian political history, and global capitalism throughout. He is consistently more interested in structural forces than in immediate events.

Later in the body of work, game theory (Arrow's Impossibility Theorem, the Prisoner's Dilemma, Kaushik Basu's Greta's Dilemma) enters as a framework — not to endorse rational-choice assumptions but to demonstrate their limits and the consequent need for institutional design that doesn't rely on actors behaving against their individual interests.

## Evolution

The trajectory from 2015 to 2022 is legible and significant. The early writing is primarily policy-analytical: jobs, Make in India, ease of doing business, business responsibility. The personal anecdotes function as illustrations. The tone is that of a concerned expert providing analysis to attentive policymakers.

By 2017, democracy and technology have entered as central concerns — not as subsidiary policy areas but as the frame within which everything else must be understood. The media failure articles and the Brexit pieces signal a broader aperture opening.

The 2018 inflection is philosophical. The five-books essay marks a turn toward first principles: what are economies for, who do economists serve, what is the relationship between knowledge and power? The capitalism-versus-democracy tension, which was implicit earlier, becomes his explicit organizing framework.

From 2019 onward, the climate crisis enters with increasing urgency, and the moral register sharpens. The language of "indictment" — of business models, of consulting ethics, of employer mentality — replaces the language of "concern." Greta Thunberg is cited approvingly multiple times. The ECG model in "Power to the People" (2021) represents the most systematized expression of a framework that has been building for six years.

What has he changed his mind about? He does not announce reversals, but there is a discernible shift in his assessment of the Indian establishment's responsiveness. The early articles assume that better analysis, better-designed processes, and better institutional design can produce better outcomes within the existing system. The later articles are less confident about this. The abolition of the Planning Commission, the COVID migrant crisis, the persistence of jobless growth — these have moved him from reform optimism toward something darker, though he resists despair as an intellectual posture.

## Distinctive Voice

Maira writes like a man conducting a seminar with himself — patient, cumulative, willing to be provisional. His sentences are long but not ornate; his paragraphs build argument rather than accumulate assertion. The emotional register is controlled but not cold: there are moments of genuine indignation (the migrant workers piece, the McKinsey autopsy) and moments of genuine tenderness (the India@75 letter to Viren, the Mashobra neighbours passage). He writes with the authority of someone who has been in the rooms where decisions were made, but without the self-promotion of the memoir-writer.

His most distinctive rhetorical habit is the paired concept: espoused theory versus theory-in-use; economies of scale versus economies of scope; capitalism versus democracy; the doughnut versus the hole. These are not just rhetorical devices but structural moves — he uses binary tensions to locate the productive disagreement, then argues for the neglected term.

He is unusually comfortable citing his own mistakes and limits. The acknowledgment that he was seen as being "on the Davos side" in the capitalism-versus-democracy debate, and his admission that challenging his own side was not easy — this kind of intellectual honesty is rare in public writing of any kind.

## What They Are Skeptical Of

Maira's skepticism is consistent and targets specific intellectual positions rather than political tribes. GDP as the primary metric of social welfare is his most sustained target — he cites Stiglitz and Sen's *Mismeasuring Our Lives* repeatedly and insists the government's sights must be fixed on jobs, not growth rates. Milton Friedman's shareholder primacy doctrine is his most consistent intellectual adversary, named in multiple articles and treated as both intellectually wrong and practically destructive.

He is skeptical of Washington Consensus prescriptions applied to India — trade openness and labour law dilution as "reforms" — treating them as ideology imposed on evidence rather than evidence producing policy. He is skeptical of technology determinism: he cites World Bank research suggesting automation's impact on developing country manufacturing has been exaggerated, while remaining alert to the genuine political dangers of surveillance capitalism and internet concentration.

Most interestingly, he is skeptical of the scaling instinct in social enterprise. "Don't scale up" is his first piece of advice to social change-makers — a provocative inversion of the dominant Silicon Valley logic. His counter-model is network and ecosystem thinking rather than organizational pyramid-building.

## Where They Fit in FF's Worldview

Maira aligns most strongly with FF's commitments to systems thinking, transitions and transformation, and the view that strategy alone is insufficient without culture and people. His India-first lens is consistent and specific.

Where he diverges — or, more precisely, adds something FF's other contributors tend not to provide — is in the depth of his institutional critique. Most FF contributors are, broadly, modernizers: they believe that better entrepreneurship, better technology, better leadership, and better policy can progressively improve India's situation. Maira shares this reformist impulse but adds a structural skepticism that most FF writing lacks: the possibility that the very metrics and frameworks that define "improvement" may be part of the problem. He is the voice that asks whether the ladder is leaning against the right wall before celebrating the speed of the climb.

He is also FF's most consistent voice on the capitalism-democracy tension — a question that most business-focused media, including FF's general orientation, tends to bracket rather than confront directly.

## Best Work

"Capitalism vs Democracy" (2018 or thereafter) is where the master framework is most fully articulated — the moral principles of property rights versus human rights laid out with analytical precision and personal conviction.

"The Used and Discarded Workers of India" (2020) is his most emotionally charged piece and his most morally direct — the COVID migrant crisis used not as policy puzzle but as moral indictment.

"Solving India's Problem of Jobless Growth" represents his most rigorous policy analysis: the employment elasticity data, the 5C framework, the GP metaphor, and the structural alternatives, all in one place.

"India@75: A Nation of Fireflies" is his most personal and most literary work — the letter-to-Viren format, the Rashtrapati Bhavan memory, the grandson who doesn't notice the beggar, the CK Prahalad benchmark. It is the piece where his analytical and moral voices are most fully fused.

"An Ethical Toolkit for Systems Transformers" is where his philosophical project is most explicitly stated — Humpty Dumpty as the fractured world that reductive science has produced, and the ethical requirements for putting it back together. This is Maira at his most ambitious and his most vulnerable to the charge of vagueness, which is itself revealing.

## Memes and Jokes

The appropriate Maira meme is the scene in every hospital drama where the specialist confidently treats the presenting symptom, the patient crashes, and someone finally calls the one doctor who asks: "But what's actually wrong with the whole patient?" Maira is perpetually that doctor, perpetually arriving too late, perpetually right.

If Maira had a LinkedIn bio written by his critics, it would read: "Former BCG Chairman. Former Planning Commission Member. Currently explaining to everyone why all of their metrics are measuring the wrong things." The joke reveals the genuine intellectual provocation: he has insider credentials and uses them to question insider assumptions.

The Humpty Dumpty metaphor he himself deploys is his best self-meme: the scientific revolution broke the world into optimizable parts, and now all the king's horses and all the king's men — the specialists, the economists, the technologists, the consultants — are trying to put it back together again. What they need, but don't have, is a theory of the whole egg.

A fitting image for his relationship to the Davos consensus: the dinner guest who keeps pointing out that the economic model being celebrated is the reason the kitchen staff can't afford rent. He is seated at the table; he is not comfortable at the table; he keeps saying so.

Finally, his "don't scale up" advice to social entrepreneurs plays like a Zen koan delivered at a startup pitch competition. The audience came for hockey-stick growth curves; he offers them a network topology of small, nimble, interconnected nodes. The joke is that he's right, and everyone knows he's right, and nobody knows quite what to do with being right in that particular way.

## Intellectual Allies and Adversaries

Maira's deepest intellectual alliance is with Amartya Sen — not merely on the metrics of development (capabilities over GDP, *Mismeasuring Our Lives*) but on the structural relationship between freedom, democracy, and economic organization. He shares Sen's conviction that development must be evaluated by what it enables people to be and do, not by aggregate output statistics. The Bhagwati/Panagariya versus Sen/Dreze debate is explicitly his frame for competing conceptions of India's economic future, and his sympathies are transparent.

He would find strong allies in Mariana Mazzucato on the public role in value creation and in Kate Raworth on doughnut economics — both of whom appear in his writing approvingly. Joseph Stiglitz, whom he cites repeatedly, is an intellectual companion on IPR maximalism, inequality, and the limitations of Washington Consensus prescriptions.

Chris Argyris is arguably his most important intellectual influence — the theories-in-use versus espoused theories distinction is not merely a borrowed concept but the epistemological foundation of his entire mode of critique. He deploys it to examine economists, corporations, governments, and himself.

His primary intellectual adversary is Milton Friedman, whom he treats as the source of the shareholder primacy doctrine that has corrupted both corporate governance and consulting. But the more interesting adversary is the unnamed class of growth economists — including those advising the Indian government — who defend jobless growth statistics as evidence of success while the labour absorption data points in the opposite direction. His argument with them is not about values but about methodology: he thinks they are using the wrong instruments to measure the wrong things and calling the result rigorous analysis.

He would have productive disagreements with Clayton Christensen (disruption theory applied without systemic consequences analysis), with the scaling advocates of the social enterprise world (networks over pyramids), and with the techno-optimist wing of development economics that sees digital infrastructure as a substitute for institutional reform.

## Writing and Argument Style

Maira's argumentative strategy is consistently inductive: he begins with a concrete observation — a newspaper story, a conversation with a neighbor, a data point from a World Bank report — and builds toward a structural claim. He rarely leads with his conclusion. The effect is that the reader arrives at the argument rather than being handed it, which gives his conclusions more persuasive weight than they would carry if asserted directly.

His persuasion strategy is primarily analogical. The GP metaphor, the Humpty Dumpty image, the ladder of business responsibility, Braudel's tides — these are not decorative but structural. Each analogy does the work of connecting a specific observation to a general framework without the argument collapsing into abstraction.

He handles counterarguments by naming them respectfully and then demonstrating their internal limits rather than their bad faith. When he challenges the shareholder primacy doctrine, he traces it to Friedman's actual reasoning before showing where the reasoning breaks down. When he challenges automation pessimism, he cites the World Bank data before qualifying it. This is a form of intellectual generosity that also functions as rhetorical strategy: an opponent who has been understood is harder to dismiss.

At the sentence level, his writing has the texture of thought rather than performance. He uses the em-dash to introduce qualification and the colon to introduce precision. He rarely uses passive voice. His questions are genuine rather than rhetorical — he often ends a section with a question he doesn't answer, signaling that the analysis is ongoing rather than concluded.

The emotional register never climbs above controlled urgency, except in the migrant workers piece and the India@75 letter, where it briefly becomes something closer to grief. These moments of affective intensity are more powerful for their rarity. He has learned — perhaps from his consulting work, perhaps from scenario planning — that emotion is most persuasive when it is earned rather than performed.
