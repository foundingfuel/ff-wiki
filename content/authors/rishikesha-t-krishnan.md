# Rishikesha T Krishnan: Intellectual Profile

## Who Is This Person

Rishikesha T Krishnan is a management scholar and professor whose contribution to Founding Fuel sits at the intersection of innovation studies, strategy, and India's developmental economics. He brings something rare to the platform: the systematic analytical habits of an academic who has spent years building frameworks, combined with the intellectual restlessness of someone genuinely troubled by a question — why does India keep underperforming on the dimension that matters most for long-run prosperity? His book *From Jugaad to Systematic Innovation* serves as a kind of intellectual spine for his entire body of work at Founding Fuel, and co-authoring *8 Steps to Innovation* with Vinay Dabholkar gave him a diagnostic toolkit he reaches for repeatedly. Among Founding Fuel contributors, he occupies an unusual position: he is the one most consistently willing to do comparative institutional analysis, to hold India up against China or the United States not as an exercise in self-flagellation but as a serious empirical method for identifying structural deficits. He writes as a teacher who has decided the classroom extends beyond the university, and whose students include policymakers, entrepreneurs, and executives who are making consequential decisions on the basis of assumptions he finds inadequately examined.

## Dominant Thesis

The single idea Krishnan returns to across 35 articles spanning five years is this: India's innovation deficit is not a cultural failure but a systemic one, and treating it as the former is itself part of the problem. The deficit is structural — inadequate R&D investment hovering below one percent of GDP while China spends over $162 billion annually, the wrong incentives at the level of firms and research institutions, absent risk capital for deep technology, and path dependencies that lock India into service-sector strength while leaving manufacturing capability underdeveloped. The corollary is equally important to him: India does innovate, but in a register that standard global indices — built around R&D expenditure, high-technology manufacturing, and patent production — systematically fail to capture. Aravind Eye, Narayana Health, Jaipur Foot represent a genuine innovation tradition driven by the imperatives of affordability and accessibility. Krishnan's argument is not that this tradition should be celebrated as India's unique advantage and left to compound on its own. His argument is that India must pursue both tracks simultaneously — the frugal, accessible, bottom-of-pyramid track and the hard, compounding, technologically ambitious track — and that excessive celebration of jugaad gives permission to avoid the harder work.

## India Lens

Krishnan applies ideas to India through the lens of structural conditions rather than cultural deficiency, which distinguishes him from commentators who explain Indian underperformance through national character or democratic dysfunction. When India ranks poorly on the Bloomberg Innovation Index, his response is methodological before it is prescriptive: the index is constructed around R&D intensity and high-technology manufacturing, and given India's economic structure, its poor showing is unsurprising and partly misleading. The more interesting question is whether India is building the conditions for the kind of innovation that will generate supernormal profits and compounding technological capability.

His India lens has several recurring focal points. He treats the waste of human potential — gifted children in villages who never encounter a good school, tier-two city students who never encounter an ambitious institution — as India's defining tragedy rather than a policy problem to be optimised at the margins. He reads Make in India with unusual intellectual honesty, tracking global trends in automation and robotics to argue that India cannot naively replicate the Japan-Korea-China manufacturing-led growth path using labour cost advantage; "making this assumption could be a terrible mistake." He watches Global Capability Centres — the MNC R&D hubs that have quietly displaced CSIR and ISRO as India's largest R&D employers — with a mixture of appreciation and anxiety, recognising that they trained the entrepreneurs who built India's startup ecosystem while simultaneously wondering what protectionism and deglobalisation will do to a model of R&D capability-building that depends on global integration. And he tracks the motorcycle industry with genuine intellectual excitement as evidence that indigenous capability-building is possible: Hero MotoCorp post-Honda, Bajaj's design autonomy, TVS's engineering independence are, for him, not automotive footnotes but proof-of-concept for a self-reliance model that is earned rather than declared.

## Themes

Krishnan contributes most substantially across three clusters: innovation and R&D (his primary expertise), India-specific challenges and opportunities (his primary preoccupation), and business and entrepreneurship understood as organisational systems rather than individual heroics. On innovation, his angle is consistently systemic — he is interested not in the lone inventor but in the designed capability: idea management systems, challenge books, the role of a committed senior champion, the difference between organisations that sustain innovation momentum and those that produce one breakthrough and then revert to routine. On India's structural challenges, he combines the frustration of someone who knows what the data shows with the hopefulness of someone who has visited VidyaGyan and watched village girls solve problems. On entrepreneurship, he is unusual in his insistence that the phenomenon extends far beyond startups — he finds entrepreneurial behavior in C-DOT under Sam Pitroda, in Aravind Eye's institutional design, in IISc's research culture.

His engagement with technology and digital disruption is more anxious than celebratory. He is genuinely concerned that India's digital success in services does not automatically translate into the deep technological capability — in semiconductors, advanced materials, pharmaceutical R&D — that generates durable competitive advantage. His most self-aware piece on technology is the 2019 strategy books article, where he admits to teaching frameworks for two decades that digital disruption has forced into question, and maps the intellectual history of how Porter and others responded to that challenge. This piece has a quality of intellectual honesty — a professor examining the possible obsolescence of his own syllabus — that is rare in management writing.

## Mental Models

Krishnan reaches for several frameworks with notable consistency. The resource-based view and core competencies thinking — explicitly invoking Prahalad and Hamel — organises his questions about where India should direct R&D investment and which capabilities are genuinely difficult to imitate. Path dependence appears explicitly as an explanatory device, deployed to understand why Jagdish Sheth's career followed the trajectory it did, and implicitly throughout his analysis of why India's innovation system has the structural shape it has. The innovation pipeline model from *8 Steps to Innovation* — idea generation, idea management, challenge books, communities of practice, committed champion — functions as a recurring diagnostic lens. He imports Yip and McKern's three-stage model of Chinese innovation (fit-for-purpose to world-standard to global leadership) as a comparative frame for asking where India sits and whether it is moving in the right direction. And systems thinking permeates everything: he almost never discusses a problem at the level of individual actors without zooming out to ask what feedback loops and structural conditions produce the observed outcomes.

## Evolution

The trajectory across five years is from diagnostic to prescriptive, and from solitary to collaborative. The 2015-2016 writing is heavily book-review-driven — Werth on Vertex, Fierstein on Bell Labs, Tett on silos, Ford on manufacturing, Tse on China. Krishnan is assembling a comparative picture, reading the global literature on innovation and testing its applicability to India. The emotional register is frustrated-but-analytical, the work of someone building a case. By 2016-2017 the writing becomes more institution-building focused — the IISc article, the Sam Pitroda piece, the healthcare conference piece — as if having diagnosed the problem he is now searching for existence proofs, cases where India built something that worked. "Hire the best people, give them freedom to do their research, create a meritocracy and get out of the way" reads as prescription extracted from description.

The 2019 pieces carry a sharper digital anxiety. The GCC article worries about what protectionism means for a model of capability-building dependent on globalisation. The strategy books article has unusual self-reflexivity. By 2020, the co-authored articles — on Covid innovation and the motorcycle industry — signal a shift toward collaborative knowledge production and a more explicitly pedagogical register, closer to a teaching note than a discursive essay. One idea he appears to have complicated rather than abandoned is the China comparison: he began using China primarily as a challenge to Indian complacency, but his later writing shows more nuance about China's internal heterogeneity and the limits of the democratic-vs-authoritarian frame.

## Distinctive Voice

Krishnan writes with the discipline of someone who has given the same lecture many times and learned exactly where students get confused. His arguments are carefully staged, his examples are chosen for instructional precision rather than rhetorical effect, and his conclusions are stated clearly rather than implied. This makes him the most legible of Founding Fuel's serious contributors — you always know where he is in an argument — but it also means his writing rarely achieves the aphoristic intensity of contributors who sacrifice clarity for provocation.

His emotional register is cautiously optimistic with a controlled undertone of frustrated urgency. The optimism is always earned through evidence rather than assertion. The frustration surfaces in precise moments — the Sundar Pichai piece has genuine edge, the "I really wonder how many food delivery companies we need" throwaway carries real impatience, "making this assumption could be a terrible mistake" is as close as he gets to alarm. His most pointed sentence — "Indians are smart, ambitious and can communicate well... Most importantly, they can be quite conformist and refrain from rocking the boat" — carries the damning faintness of a professor writing a lukewarm recommendation letter and knowing exactly what he is doing.

## What They Are Skeptical Of

Krishnan's skepticisms are consistent and carefully grounded. He is skeptical of jugaad as sufficient innovation — not through direct attack but through consistent redirection toward the harder, more compounding work that jugaad can substitute for. He is skeptical of age discrimination in entrepreneurship, making the case with specific counter-examples against the VC archetype that treats 27 as the optimal founding age. He is skeptical of the Sundar Pichai celebration as a genuine signal of Indian technological achievement, arguing that Indians becoming technical managers in American companies is a different phenomenon — and a less consequential one — than Indians building globally competitive technology companies. He is skeptical of personalized medicine hype, applying a scientist's caution to claims that are running ahead of the evidence. He is skeptical of India's innovation rankings as complete measures, building a methodological critique rather than simply disputing the conclusions. And he is skeptical of the herd mentality in India's startup ecosystem, particularly the concentration of capital in consumer-facing applications at the expense of deep technology.

## Where They Fit in FF's Worldview

Krishnan aligns strongly with Founding Fuel's India-first lens, its treatment of entrepreneurship as institutional and organisational rather than just startup phenomenon, and its systems thinking orientation. He is perhaps the platform's most rigorous practitioner of comparative analysis — holding India against specific benchmarks and being honest about the gaps. Where he adds something distinctive is in his willingness to apply formal academic frameworks — resource-based view, path dependence, stage-gate models — with pedagogical care, translating scholarly ideas into accessible diagnostic tools without oversimplifying them. Where he occasionally diverges from FF's editorial warmth is in his professorial detachment: he is more comfortable in the analytical register than the inspirational one, and his writing is less interested in the texture of individual human experience than some FF contributors. The VidyaGyan piece is the exception, and notably it is among his most memorable.

## Best Work

Five articles represent Krishnan at his sharpest. The Vertex Pharma piece establishes his entire intellectual programme in miniature: the India innovation question, the systemic analysis, the skepticism about hype, the admiration for sustained institutional commitment. The VidyaGyan piece is his most emotionally direct — "one of the biggest tragedies of India has been the waste of human potential" — and shows that his systems thinking has a moral foundation it does not always make visible. The Make in India manufacturing future piece is his most prescient, anticipating automation-driven reshoring trends that the policy debate was not taking seriously. The Sundar Pichai piece is his sharpest and most pointed, the piece where his controlled frustration escapes the analytical container briefly enough to be genuinely memorable. And the Covid innovation piece, co-authored, is his most pedagogically accomplished — the question "why are they unable to muster even a fraction of this ability in normal times?" crystallises his entire preoccupation with the gap between demonstrated capability and routine performance.

## Memes and Jokes

The first meme is the "Galaxy Brain" progression applied to Indian innovation policy: "Step 1: India has smart, cheap engineers. Step 2: India should become a manufacturing hub. Step 3: Robots now do manufacturing. Step 4: ..." followed by a blank panel — which captures precisely Krishnan's frustration with a development strategy built on assumptions about labour cost advantage that technology is quietly dissolving.

The second is the Drake meme with Drake refusing "Indian CEO at Google" and Drake approvingly pointing at "Indian startup building globally competitive deep tech" — a direct compression of the Sundar Pichai piece's argument about what actually constitutes technological ambition.

The third is the Distracted Boyfriend meme, where the boyfriend is "Indian startups and investors," the girlfriend he is ignoring is "deep technology and R&D capability," and the attractive distraction is "yet another food delivery app" — channelling his "I really wonder how many food delivery companies we need."

The fourth is the "It's the same picture" meme, where the executive holds up photos of "innovation through jugaad" and "permission to avoid systematic R&D investment" and asks if they are the same — which captures his most sustained and productive intellectual tension.

The fifth is Gandalf at the bridge — "You shall not pass" — except Gandalf is the Indian venture capital ecosystem standing between a forty-five-year-old founder and Series A funding, capturing his pointed pushback against age discrimination in entrepreneurship with the mythological gravity he would probably find excessive but accurate.

## Intellectual Allies and Adversaries

Krishnan's closest intellectual ally is C.K. Prahalad — not the bottom-of-pyramid Prahalad alone, but the full Prahalad who believed that Indian firms could build world-class strategic capability and that the developing world's constraints were innovation opportunities rather than merely obstacles. The core competencies framework Krishnan reaches for repeatedly is Prahalad and Hamel's, and his insistence that India can build genuinely competitive technological firms rather than only services exporters echoes Prahalad's ambition for Indian industry.

He would find substantial agreement with Mariana Mazzucato on the state's role in de-risking radical innovation — her argument in *The Entrepreneurial State* that patient public capital is necessary for the kind of deep technology investment that private markets underprovide maps directly onto Krishnan's frustration with India's inadequate R&D spending and his admiration for DARPA-style institutional commitment.

His most productive adversary is probably Clayton Christensen — not because Krishnan disagrees with disruption theory, but because he thinks the disruption framework has been applied to India in ways that make jugaad-style frugal innovation seem like a sufficient response to the technological frontier. Christensen's framework can be read as validating the low-end disruption from below that India excels at; Krishnan's implicit argument is that this reading gives too much comfort and that the frontier itself must be engaged.

He would be skeptical of Tom Peters-style management enthusiasm — the celebration of passion, culture, and individual heroics that sidesteps the harder structural and systemic questions. Krishnan's innovation framework is deliberately depersonalised: it asks organisations to build systems that work regardless of whether a visionary is in the room, which puts him in fundamental tension with the great-man theory of innovation that much popular management writing trades on.

## Writing and Argument Style

Krishnan constructs arguments through staged analytical exposition rather than narrative seduction or provocative assertion. He typically opens with a concrete occasion — a book read, a conference attended, a company visited, a statistic encountered — and uses that occasion as a portal into a structural question. The concrete occasion is not mere decoration; it performs an epistemic function, grounding the analytical claim in observed reality. He then builds through comparison: India against China, India against the innovation index methodology, Indian jugaad against systematic innovation. The comparison is always in service of a diagnostic question, not a ranking exercise.

His persuasion strategy is primarily evidentiary: he accumulates examples, cites data, invokes frameworks, and then draws a measured conclusion. He handles counterarguments through acknowledgement rather than dismissal — he is genuinely interested in why the opposing view has traction and usually grants it partial validity before explaining why it is insufficient. This produces writing that is intellectually honest and occasionally frustrating, because the measured qualification can dilute what deserves to be a sharper point.

At the sentence level, his writing is clear, precise, and occasionally dry to the point of understatement. He rarely reaches for metaphor, and when he does — the automated sushi restaurant as a vision of manufacturing's future — it lands with unusual force precisely because of its rarity. His most effective rhetorical habit is the pointed throwaway: a sentence delivered with professorial neutrality that contains a judgment sharper than its tone suggests. "Indians are smart, ambitious and can communicate well... Most importantly, they can be quite conformist and refrain from rocking the boat" is a masterclass in this register — agreeing with a flattering premise in order to deliver a more damaging assessment through the pivot on "most importantly." It is the prose equivalent of a professor's thin smile.
