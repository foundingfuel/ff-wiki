# G. Venkat Raman: Intellectual Profile

## Who Is This Person

G. Venkat Raman occupies a distinctive niche within Founding Fuel's contributor ecosystem: he is the publication's resident strategic sinologist, a scholar-practitioner who brings sustained, granular knowledge of China to an Indian readership that is simultaneously fascinated, threatened, and confused by its neighbour. His academic base — he is a professor at IIM Indore, specialising in international business and strategy — explains his methodological instincts. He writes like someone who has read the primary sources, spent time in the field, and is deeply impatient with lazy generalisations. What he brings that other FF contributors largely do not is a willingness to sit inside complexity for a long time before reaching for a lesson. His twelve articles, spanning 2019 to 2025, are not opinion pieces in the conventional sense; they are, more accurately, structured analytical dispatches from a mind that has been watching the China-India-US triangle for years and is now compelled to help Indian decision-makers see what he sees.

## Dominant Thesis

The single organising idea that runs beneath all of Raman's work is this: the architecture of global interdependence, which once functioned as a stabiliser and growth engine, has been weaponised — and India, caught between admiration for Chinese state capacity, dependence on American technology platforms, and the political pressure of its own nationalist moment, risks navigating this transformation with emotion rather than analysis. The phrase he borrows from Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman — "weaponised interdependence" — appears explicitly in his 2020 writing and then dissolves into the fabric of everything that follows, present everywhere as an assumption even when unacknowledged. By 2025's "The New Perimeter of National Security," it has become the foundational axiom of his entire worldview: "The interdependence that powered globalisation is now being weaponised." The journey from that borrowed concept to that declarative sentence is, essentially, the intellectual arc of his entire Founding Fuel output.

## India Lens

Raman's application of ideas to India is primarily strategic and prescriptive rather than descriptive or sociological. In his early pieces, India functions as the implied student: what should it learn from Shanghai's environmental governance, from China's O2O innovation model, from the speed with which Beijing is building technological capacity? The tone is almost tutorial — he is briefing a sophisticated reader who needs frameworks before they can act wisely.

The register shifts noticeably after 2021. India moves from observer to actor, and Raman's prescriptions become more direct. By the time of the State vs. Big Tech series, he is framing India's regulatory challenges through comparison with the EU and US, implicitly arguing that India needs to make deliberate choices rather than drift. His 2020 injunction — that India must understand the US-China tech rivalry "guided by facts, reasoning and a healthy dose of pragmatism, rather than by pride or fear, especially during these emotionally charged times" — is his most explicit acknowledgment that Indian nationalism, inflamed by the Galwan moment, poses an epistemic risk. He names the danger without naming the politics.

The most important India-specific insight in the batch arrives in "The New Perimeter of National Security," where Raman argues through cases like Razorpay, PhonePe, and Mundra Port that Indian firms are already inside the geopolitical infrastructure contest whether they recognise it or not. The leap from his 2019 China travel dispatches to this boardroom-addressed warning is substantial. What has changed is not his framework but his audience's readiness — and his impatience with their unreadiness.

## Themes

Raman contributes most substantially at the intersection of geopolitics, political economy, and technology governance. His State vs. Big Tech four-part series (September 2021) represents the fullest expression of this convergence: a comparative anatomy of how China, the EU, and the United States are each reshaping the relationship between state authority and platform power, shaped by their distinct historical memories and ideological inheritances. His observation that "historical experiences of Europe with Fascism and Nazism have led to very stringent privacy protection laws" is characteristic — he is linking regulatory culture to historical trauma in a way that most technology commentators simply do not. On Leadership and Organisational Culture, his contribution is narrower but sharpening: the 2025 piece is a direct intervention into corporate governance, arguing that boards must now integrate geopolitical risk into their strategic architecture. On Science and First Principles, his generational cohort analysis of Chinese Communist Party leadership in "Xi Jinping and the rise of a new party elite" demonstrates an unusually disciplined commitment to building up from evidence rather than from the personality narratives that dominate coverage of Beijing.

## Mental Models

Raman reaches for a consistent toolkit. The centre-province flexibility framework — *Shang you Zhengce Xia you Duice* — introduced in his 2019 Shanghai piece, explains Chinese governance as deliberately elastic rather than rigidly authoritarian, and he returns to its implications whenever Western or Indian commentators misread Beijing's regulatory moves as simple commands from above. His innovation typology distinguishes the Silicon Valley model (build product, hand off logistics) from the Chinese O2O model (orchestrate the entire value chain), and uses this structural difference to explain why Chinese digital platforms generate richer and more strategically useful data. His debt-stimulus-moral hazard cycle, deployed in "China's economic shift: Politics trumps growth," maps how 2008's rescue medicine became 2023's addiction — an Evergrande-scaled demonstration that state bailout expectations distort risk-taking at national scale. Most persistently, he uses the tactical-versus-structural distinction to separate cyclical phenomena (diplomatic warmth, temporary cooperation) from underlying forces that do not respond to summitry. This last framework is his most diagnostic and most frequently deployed.

## Evolution

The trajectory from 2019 to 2025 describes a thinker moving from observation to prescription, and from comparative analysis to applied governance. His earliest piece is the most sensory and curious — "every time I visit China, I am surprised by how fast it's changing" carries the register of a scholar who still finds fieldwork genuinely delightful. The 2020 pieces are urgent but analytical; the 2021 series is clinical and ambitious. By 2022 and 2023, he is deep inside Chinese political economy on its own terms, analysing Xi's ideological project rather than primarily asking what India should learn from it. The 2025 piece marks the most significant tonal break: he is no longer writing for India-watchers of China but for Indian boardrooms. The vocabulary shifts — "strategic exposure," "perimeter," "corporate governance" — and the prescriptions harden.

The most significant intellectual evolution concerns efficiency versus resilience. His early pieces treat economic efficiency and geopolitical strategy as parallel but separate concerns. By 2025, he has made a decisive move: "Efficiency is no longer the sole metric. Resilience — and the sovereignty that underpins it — has become central to long-term competitiveness." This is not merely an updated view of trade policy; it is a revision of what optimisation means in a world of weaponised interdependence.

## Distinctive Voice

Raman writes with a controlled academic authority that distinguishes him from most FF contributors, who tend toward the conversational or the confessional. His sentences are declarative and structurally dense; he is not trying to charm the reader but to compel them. He favours the corrective opening — "Many believe... however" — as a rhetorical structure that establishes both the error he is addressing and his own analytical position in a single movement. His emotional register is consistently cautionary with analytical urgency: never alarmist, but pressing readers toward recognition of what they have been comfortable ignoring. The 2025 piece reaches an elegiac precision in the sentence "That world has quietly vanished" — four words that carry the weight of everything he has been building since 2019.

## What They Are Skeptical Of

Raman's skepticism is directed, consistently, at oversimplification and at the comfort that oversimplification provides. He pushes back against the "China as copycat" narrative — "It would be a grave error to dismiss China as merely a 'near-peer competitor'" — because it enables complacency. He resists personality-driven explanations of Beijing's regulatory moves: the Jack Ma punishment was not about a speech; it was the culmination of structural tensions between platform capital and party sovereignty that long predated any public remark. He is skeptical of peak-China theory, presenting it only to dismantle it. He is skeptical of treating technology as politically neutral — the culminating argument of his entire arc. And, most specifically for an Indian audience, he is skeptical of allowing nationalist emotion to determine strategic judgment, which is as close as he comes to direct political commentary.

## Where They Fit in FF's Worldview

Raman aligns most closely with Founding Fuel's systems-thinking orientation and its insistence on policy intersecting with technology and organisational capability. His state-market-technology triangulation maps directly onto FF's interest in ecosystems and interdependencies. He shares the publication's conviction that Indian organisations — boardrooms, institutions, even governments — need frameworks before they can act wisely, and that the purpose of analysis is ultimately to improve decision-making in India's specific context.

Where he adds something distinctive is in his sustained comparative depth. Most FF contributors are India-first by instinct; Raman is India-first by design but arrives there through extended circuits through Beijing, Brussels, and Washington. He brings the rigour of area studies into a publication that more typically draws from management theory and entrepreneurship. He also operates at a higher level of geopolitical abstraction than most FF contributors, comfortable with international relations frameworks and political economy theory that rarely appear elsewhere in the publication's corpus.

## Best Work

Five articles represent Raman at his sharpest. "The US-China war for tech supremacy" and "Should the world be worried about Chinese tech?" function as a diptych: the first establishes the geopolitical architecture of weaponised interdependence; the second provides the technological substrate — the O2O model, the data generation advantage, WeChat as strategic infrastructure. Together they constitute his most complete theory. "The hidden fault lines behind Jack Ma's fall" is his best single demonstration of structural versus surface analysis; the Alibaba case becomes a window into the uneasy equilibrium between party-state and capital that the regulatory crackdown would eventually shatter. The State vs. Big Tech series is his most ambitious project — a comparative regulatory theory spanning three jurisdictions, grounded in historical memory and ideological analysis. And "The New Perimeter of National Security" is his most practically consequential piece: the moment when six years of China-watching crystallises into direct, named, actionable advice for Indian corporate leadership.

## Memes and Jokes

The phrase "an old joke goes, the only intellectual property the Chinese believe in is speed" — which Raman actually uses in his 2019 piece — captures something essential about his method: he will deploy the light touch on a serious topic, but only once, before returning to rigorous analysis. He is the person at the seminar who smiles at the joke and then dismantles it. Second, one imagines him responding to the "China has peaked" school of thought the way a cardiologist responds to a patient who says their blood pressure is fine because they feel fine — the absence of symptoms is precisely what concerns him. Third, his 2025 boardroom intervention has the energy of the scene in every disaster film where one person has been watching the seismograph for years and finally gets the meeting with the executives: "I know this looks like normal activity to you, but I've been watching the numbers." Fourth, his consistent "many experts are missing a crucial point" move is essentially the academic version of the Columbo "just one more thing" — deceptively mild, structurally devastating. Finally, his career-long argument that technology is not politically neutral would make him a natural ally of the character in any thriller who keeps insisting "it's not a pipeline, it's a weapon" — and keeps being told he is being dramatic — until he isn't.

## Intellectual Allies and Adversaries

Raman's clearest intellectual kinship is with Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman, whose "weaponised interdependence" framework he adopts explicitly and applies with evident conviction. He would also find strong alignment with Yuen Yuen Ang's nuanced institutional analysis of Chinese development — her argument that China's growth was powered by "directed improvisation" rather than either authoritarian command or market liberalism maps closely onto Raman's centre-province flexibility framework. He shares with Dani Rodrik a conviction that the efficiency-maximising logic of globalisation has been underwritten by political assumptions that are now collapsing, and that resilience must replace efficiency as the primary metric of economic governance.

His most productive adversaries would be techno-optimists who treat geopolitics as noise in the signal of technological progress — the Marc Andreessen school, in which software eats the world regardless of which state the servers are in. Raman's entire project is a refutation of this view. He would also push back against Thomas Friedman's "the world is flat" integrationist confidence, not through ideological opposition but through the evidence that flat networks, once built, become the infrastructure of coercion. And he would have sharp disagreements with those Indian commentators who treat the US-China rivalry as a binary choice India must make rather than a structural environment India must navigate — his insistence on pragmatism over pride is a direct challenge to the nationalist frame that reduces strategy to allegiance.

## Writing and Argument Style

Raman constructs arguments the way a good academic constructs a literature review before a findings section: he establishes the dominant view, identifies precisely where it is wrong or incomplete, introduces his structural corrective, and then applies it to cases. The counterargument is not dismissed but located — shown to be a surface reading of a deeper phenomenon. His persuasion strategy is primarily analytical authority rather than narrative warmth or rhetorical provocation. He does not charm; he demonstrates.

At the sentence level, he favours declarative constructions that carry their confidence grammatically — "It would be a grave error," "That world has quietly vanished," "digital platforms are not just pipes." These are not hedged; they are delivered as conclusions from evidence already assembled. His rhetorical habit is the structural contrast: Silicon Valley vs. Chinese model, tactical pause vs. structural competition, efficiency vs. resilience. These binaries are not reductive — he consistently complicates them — but they give his arguments a scaffolding that makes complex material navigable. He is at his best when the case study and the theory are held in balance; at his most academic when theory temporarily overwhelms the specific. The 2025 piece, where named Indian companies anchor abstract geopolitical arguments, represents his most successful integration of these two registers.
