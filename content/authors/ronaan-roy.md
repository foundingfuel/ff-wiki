# Ronaan Roy: Intellectual Profile

## Who Is This Person

Ronaan Roy arrives at Founding Fuel from an unusual angle. Where most contributors to the platform bring expertise in strategy, economics, organizational behavior, or technology, Roy brings something rarer and harder to systematize: a connoisseur's eye trained on cultural production, sharpened by years of close attention to film, music, public art, and the creative life of cities. His connection to Kolkata's cultural ecosystem runs deep — his father ran an advertising agency that commissioned the first Durga Puja awards, a biographical detail he mentions without fanfare that nonetheless explains a great deal about his formation. He is, in the most precise sense, someone who grew up inside the infrastructure of taste-making and learned to see it from the inside.

What Roy brings that other FF contributors do not is a genuine aesthetic education applied to social questions. He does not approach culture as illustration — as a convenient metaphor to dress up arguments about markets or management. He approaches it as evidence. A city's pandal-making traditions tell him something true about that city's economic and psychological condition. An Oscar ceremony's voting patterns tell him something true about the institutional sociology of taste. A jazz musician's harmonic choices tell him something true about how human brains process expectation and reward. This is an unusual analytical posture, and at its best it produces writing that no one else at FF would produce.

## Dominant Thesis

Roy's central preoccupation, visible across every piece he has written, is the tension between **craft and sentiment** — specifically, his insistence that emotional impact achieved through formal rigor is categorically different from, and superior to, emotional impact achieved through audience manipulation. This is not primarily a claim about art. It is a claim about cognition: that genuine reward comes from the surprise that follows pattern recognition, not from the activation of pre-installed sympathetic responses. His Jazz piece states it most cleanly — "all music is about recognising patterns. For this, your brain rewards you. But some patterns razzle-dazzle and subvert expectations" — but the same architecture underlies his contempt for Oscar Bait, his frustration with biopics that show us the genius's output without illuminating the interior condition that produced it, and his repeated praise for films that work against their genre's worst instincts rather than with them.

What makes this more than aesthetic snobbery is that Roy is genuinely interested in *why* the easier path is taken, and what it costs. His CODA critique is not just "this film is mediocre." It is a structural argument: that institutions calibrated to reward sentiment over craft will systematically produce winners that are good enough to win and not good enough to matter. He keeps returning to this because he sees it as a failure mode with implications beyond cinema.

## India Lens

Roy's India lens is geographically concentrated and culturally specific rather than structurally broad. He is not an analyst of Indian media markets or creative industries as ecosystems. He is, above all, a theorist of Kolkata — and his most original contribution to FF's body of India-specific thinking may be the cultural-economic thesis he develops across four consecutive years of Durga Puja writing.

The argument, stated most sharply in the 2022 piece, is this: Kolkata is a post-Partition deindustrialised city that redirected its status signaling from material accumulation to cultural capital. "While other cities peg their socio-economic status on the cars owned, or the newness of their watches, Kolkata pegs it on the number of books read, and the artistic merit of the pandal in their locality." This is a genuine insight — the kind of cultural-economic observation that would hold up under the scrutiny of a serious urbanist or historian. It explains why Kolkata produces world-class public art on budgets that would embarrass a mid-tier Mumbai agency ("What Kolkata does in Rs 20 lakh, Mumbai cannot do in a crore"), and it explains the psychological stakes of the annual competition.

His 2024 Durga Puja piece extends this into overtly political territory, engaging directly with the RG Kar Medical College rape and murder case — carefully, with the disclaimer that "the views in this article are my own, and don't represent any organisation," but unflinchingly. Here he documents how public art absorbs political trauma, how pandals processed collective grief and anger in the months following a crime that had galvanized Bengali public consciousness. This is his most significant India writing — not because it is the most polished, but because it demonstrates that his aesthetic framework can hold weight when the stakes become genuinely serious.

## Themes

Roy's work sits almost entirely within Arts & Culture, which functions as his primary lens on everything else. Within that domain, his most distinctive angle is what might be called **institutional aesthetics** — how powerful institutions (Hollywood, UNESCO, Disney, the Oscar voting body) encounter marginal or subcultural creative forms, and whether they do justice to what they are recognizing or quietly deform it in the act of recognition. His treatment of the UNESCO inscription of Durga Puja, his skepticism about Disney's deployment of Ms. Marvel, and his persistent frustration with the Oscar industrial complex all operate within this frame.

His secondary engagement is with Work and Workplace Culture, approached obliquely. The board games piece argues for play as cognitive development, collective bonding, and emotional intelligence — essentially making a case for unstructured sociality in a culture increasingly organized around productivity. The Jazz piece is partly about learning to appreciate things that don't yield their rewards immediately, which is implicitly a meditation on attention and patience in an accelerating world.

He is largely absent from the platform's core domains of Business, Entrepreneurship, Policy, and Economics — not because he lacks opinions in these areas, but because his intellectual formation has not given him systematic frameworks in them. He applies economic reasoning to culture (the budget-constraint-as-creativity-catalyst intuition is a genuine economic argument) but does not move the other direction.

## Mental Models

Roy's most developed and portable framework is **cognitive reward through pattern recognition**. He articulates it explicitly in the Jazz piece and applies it implicitly everywhere else: brains are prediction machines that generate pleasure when predictions are confirmed and excitement when they are productively violated. Boredom is what happens when you can predict too easily; confusion is what happens when you cannot predict at all. Excellent craft occupies the productive middle — familiar enough to engage, surprising enough to reward. This framework explains his taste hierarchy more coherently than any set of aesthetic preferences could.

His second framework is **genre inversion as quality signal**. His highest praise consistently goes to films that work *against* their genre's worst instincts: RRR for operating outside Hollywood action conventions, Sinners for being a period drama about Black identity that is "unexpectedly attacked, and elevated, by vampires," Mad Max: Fury Road for reversing gender dynamics in a masculine genre. He is interested in what a genre permits when someone refuses to take its permission for granted.

A third framework, never fully articulated but consistently present, is **constraint as creativity catalyst**. He notes it in the Durga Puja series — Covid budget restrictions produced more experimental pandals — and implies it in his film criticism, where the most formally adventurous films are rarely the most expensively produced. He never quite makes this a principle, but it operates as a background assumption throughout.

His most distinctive critical tool is **blocking analysis** — attention to actor placement and mise-en-scène as evidence of directorial intelligence. His comparison of Conclave's staging to Barbie's (where "everyone stands in a row") is precise, visual, and the kind of observation that most cultural critics would not think to make.

## Evolution

Roy's earliest work (2022) is list-organized, occasionally precious in register ("gentle reader," "good reader"), and most comfortable in discursive essay mode — the Jazz piece, which is his most theoretically developed writing of the period, suggests the list format is somewhat imposed rather than natural. By 2023, he is making bolder structural claims: the formulation "what links these films, and elevates them above the usual... it is pathos and consequences" is his clearest theoretical statement of what makes genre cinema work, and it is offered with a confidence that was absent the previous year.

The 2024 pieces mark a significant tonal shift. The RG Kar case changes the emotional register of the Durga Puja writing from appreciative curation to something closer to bearing witness. He does not abandon the aesthetic framework — he uses pandal imagery to track the political emotion — but the stakes are no longer purely aesthetic. This is his most serious writing.

The 2026 Oscar piece represents genuine intellectual escalation. "Oscar 2026: A Study in Extremes" is his most analytical writing — engaging streaming economics, pacing bifurcation, budget-control trade-offs in contemporary filmmaking — and it suggests a thinker moving from connoisseurship toward something more structurally diagnostic. If the trajectory continues, Roy may become the FF contributor best positioned to write about creative industries as systems, not just individual works as objects.

## Distinctive Voice

Roy writes with the register of the **knowing enthusiast**: someone who has spent more time on something than anyone should, earned the right to be opinionated, and wants to share both the pleasure and the rigor without pretending the two are in tension. His enthusiasm is undefended — he does not protect himself with excessive detachment or ironic distance. When he is delighted, he says "gee-willikins and my oh my." When he is exasperated, he says "I honestly don't think Elvis should be nominated." The emotional states are readable and genuine.

He is funnier than most FF contributors, and funnier in a specific way: the humor tends to be structurally embedded in the argument rather than decorative. "Let us all agree then, Monopoly sucks" is a punchline that also sets up his actual claim about what good game design requires. "I'm not saying most Top Gun fans are Islamophobic, but a fair percentage of Islamophobes are likely to be Top Gun fans too" is both a joke and a genuine analytical move about audience self-selection.

His metaphors are precise and physical. He describes Ms. Marvel as a "rockstar that swan-dived into her audience, and instead of catching and celebrating her, they parted like Musa's red sea." The image is specific, kinetic, and slightly heartbreaking — which is exactly the tone he is reaching for.

## What They Are Skeptical Of

Roy is constitutionally skeptical of **sentiment as substitute for craft**, which is his most consistent critical stance. He names CODA as an archetype — a film good enough to win and not good enough to matter — and keeps returning to it as evidence of institutional failure, not just aesthetic disappointment. The Oscar Bait category, which he deploys with genuine contempt, describes films that have learned to trigger sympathy without earning it.

He is skeptical of **auteur mythology** when it licenses bad habits. His critique of James Cameron — "I wish James Cameron was less of a dictator director, and surrounded himself by people who would challenge him... filmmaking is a team sport" — is an argument about the danger of unchecked creative authority. His critique of Bradley Cooper in Maestro makes the same move: Cooper is talented enough to be ambitious but not skilled enough to know when to stop.

He pushes back against **representation as self-congratulation**. The comparison of Ms. Marvel to Slumdog Millionaire — distinguishing representation that serves the represented from representation that serves the audience's exoticizing gaze — is his most pointed version of this skepticism. He wants genuine inclusion, not inclusion performed for the comfort of the includer.

He is deeply suspicious of **critical consensus as quality signal**, most explicitly in his comparison of Ms. Marvel's 96% critical approval rating against its actual viewership numbers. He uses this not to dismiss critics but to argue that institutional taste-making can produce ratings that describe something other than audience connection.

## Where They Fit in FF's Worldview

Roy aligns most closely with FF's commitment to **people and culture** — the conviction that understanding what a community values, celebrates, and mourns is necessary for understanding anything else about it. His Kolkata writing is the fullest expression of this: the pandal competition is not decoration on top of the city's economic and political life; it is a direct expression of it.

He also fits FF's **India-first lens**, though in a narrower geographic register than most contributors. He is not making arguments about India as a national system; he is making arguments about Kolkata as a city, and occasionally using that to say something about how Indian cultural identity works more broadly.

Where he diverges — and adds something distinctive — is his willingness to treat aesthetic experience as **serious intellectual evidence** rather than pleasant illustration. Most FF contributors would use a film as a launching pad for an argument about something else. Roy treats the film as the primary object of investigation, and what emerges from that investigation is something about how human beings process experience, build collective identity, and respond to institutional power. This is an unusual and valuable register for the platform.

## Best Work

**"Notes on appreciating Jazz"** is his most developed theoretical writing, and the piece that most clearly articulates the cognitive framework underlying all his criticism. It is also his most genuinely helpful piece — it changes how you listen.

**The 2022 Durga Puja piece** contains his most original India-specific thinking: the cultural-economic thesis about Kolkata's post-Partition redirection of status signaling toward cultural capital. The sentence "Kolkata is thus a city without the affluence of Mumbai or Delhi, but with the remnant of taste of the once-wealthy" is worth the whole piece.

**The 2024 Durga Puja piece** is his most emotionally serious work. The integration of the RG Kar case into the aesthetic analysis demonstrates that his framework can hold political weight without collapsing into either aestheticism or polemic.

**"Oscar 2026: A Study in Extremes"** is his most analytically ambitious film writing, engaging streaming economics and cinematic pacing as related structural problems. It suggests what his film criticism might become as it matures.

**The Ms. Marvel piece**, despite its analytical thinness on media economics, contains some of his sharpest prose and his most pointed argument about the difference between representation and genuine inclusion.

## Memes and Jokes

The most precise meme for Roy's film criticism is the "Nobody: / Film Twitter: 10/10 masterpiece / Rotten Tomatoes critics: 97% / Actual audiences: [cricket sounds]" format — capturing his persistent frustration with the gap between institutional approval and genuine connection. He would add a third row: "Ronaan Roy: technically accomplished but emotionally hollow, C+."

For his Kolkata writing, the appropriate reference is the Indian uncle who corrects a foreign tourist who praises Mumbai's culture with twelve minutes of counter-evidence delivered at conversational volume. Except in Roy's case, the twelve minutes are impeccably sourced and end with a logistical recommendation about which pandal to visit on which evening to avoid the selfie bottleneck.

His relationship to Oscar season is best captured by the sports fan meme of someone watching a game through a pillow while simultaneously live-texting commentary. The dread is real; the engagement is total; the complaints are exquisitely detailed.

His cognitive reward framework applied to popular music suggests the meme of a music professor discovering that "Sweet Home Alabama" uses three chords while Coltrane's "Giant Steps" uses seventeen, then spending forty minutes explaining why the Coltrane is harder to enjoy but more genuinely rewarding — while acknowledging, without embarrassment, that he does occasionally hum the Lynyrd Skynyrd.

For his skepticism of auteur mythology, the reference is the emperor's new clothes, except Roy is the child who not only points out the nakedness but explains, with specific reference to the weave of the fabric, why the tailor should have hired a collaborator.

## Intellectual Allies and Adversaries

Roy would find a genuine ally in **Robert Pirsig**, whose argument in *Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance* — that quality is not subjective preference but a real property of things that careful attention can recognize — maps almost exactly onto Roy's conviction that craft is distinguishable from sentiment and that the distinction matters. Both share a belief that rigor and enjoyment are not in tension; that understanding *how* something works deepens rather than diminishes the pleasure it produces.

He would find a useful ally in **Pauline Kael**, the American film critic, in her insistence that emotional response is not anti-intellectual and that criticism should be honest about the experience of watching rather than retreating to abstract evaluative language. They share a willingness to be annoyed in public and to say so with precision. Where they would diverge: Kael was suspicious of formal analysis; Roy is most interested in exactly the formal choices Kael tended to pass over.

His most natural adversary would be **Malcolm Gladwell** — not because Gladwell is wrong, but because the strategies are opposed. Gladwell finds surprising patterns and uses them to generate counterintuitive narratives that flatter the reader's sense of discovery. Roy is interested in patterns too, but he is suspicious of the move from "this is surprising" to "this is therefore instructive." His CODA critique would apply with some force to Gladwell: the story is enjoyable; it just feels a little like intellectual Oscar Bait.

He would have a productive disagreement with **auteur theorist Andrew Sarris**, whose director-as-primary-author framework Roy implicitly rejects. Roy's praise tends to be distributed across the filmmaking team — he notices editing, blocking, production design, and performance alongside direction — and his critique of Cameron is precisely that the auteur framework, taken too seriously, licenses the director to stop listening.

## Writing and Argument Style

Roy builds arguments through **accumulation of specific evidence toward a structural claim** — which is the opposite of the move most analytical writers make. He does not announce a thesis and then defend it; he examines several specific things with care and then articulates what they have in common. The Jazz piece does this: he describes several specific musical experiences, explains the cognitive mechanism that makes them pleasurable or frustrating, and then uses that mechanism to explain generational taste gaps and the difficulty of jazz as a genre. The claim arrives after the evidence, which gives it more weight than it would carry if stated first.

His persuasion strategy is primarily **the demonstration of noticing**. He does not tell you that Conclave has superior directing to Barbie; he describes what the blocking in each film looks like and trusts you to draw the conclusion. When he does state conclusions directly, they arrive with the authority of someone who has been paying close attention for a long time, and who assumes you will trust their eyesight if they show you what they are looking at.

He handles counterarguments largely by anticipating and pre-empting them. The move he makes in the Durga Puja pieces — getting ahead of the objection that other Indian festivals are comparable — is typical: he imagines the skeptic and answers them before they speak. This is more effective than addressing counterarguments explicitly because it positions his reader as already persuaded rather than in need of convincing.

At the sentence level, Roy's most distinctive habit is the **embedded concession that sharpens rather than softens**. "CODA was an enjoyable film; it just feels a little like an Oscar cop-out" does not hedge the critique — it makes it more precise. The enjoyment is real; the failure is structural. This is the mark of someone who has thought carefully about what they actually believe rather than what position they want to defend.
