# Vivek Y. Kelkar: Intellectual Profile

## Who Is This Person

Vivek Y. Kelkar occupies a distinctive and largely solitary position within Founding Fuel's contributor ecosystem. Where most FF writers approach global affairs from the vantage point of business strategy, organizational dynamics, or entrepreneurial culture, Kelkar arrives from the opposite direction: he is, at core, a geopolitics and geoeconomics analyst who happens to be writing for a business and leadership audience. His training appears rooted in international relations theory — he cites Keohane's 1984 framework on hegemonic stability, deploys Clayton-Maggiori-Schreger's 2023 work on geopolitical constraint, and is at ease with IR vocabulary like "compellence," "escalation dominance," and "asymmetric cooperation" — yet he consistently translates structural analysis into the language of strategic consequence for executives, policymakers, and informed citizens trying to navigate an increasingly illegible world.

His role at Founding Fuel is, in effect, that of the platform's global systems analyst. Across 22 articles spanning December 2023 to March 2026, he has built what amounts to a sustained intellectual project: mapping the architecture of a world in which the old rules of international order are not merely changing but dissolving. What he brings that other contributors do not is a combination of academic-grade IR rigor, quantified India-specific exposure data, and the patience to work a single thesis across multiple articles, conflict theaters, and analytical registers. He is not a journalist covering events; he is a theorist using events as evidence.

## The Dominant Thesis

The one idea Kelkar returns to with increasing urgency across the entire body of work is what he finally names explicitly in January 2026: the structural absence of endgames. In "A World Without Endgames," he states it with precision: *"The absence of credible endgames — rather than the presence of active conflict alone — is emerging as the defining feature of the global order."* But this insight had been building for two years before he named it. In the Africa piece, neither China nor Europe achieves decisive influence; in the Middle East writing, the US cannot translate military superiority into political outcomes; in the Iran war series, the most technologically advanced military alliance in history cannot convert degradation into resolution. The thesis is not simply that wars are messy — it is that the structural conditions of the current order actively prevent the kind of decisive settlements that once punctuated historical conflict cycles. Hegemonic transition, proxy network proliferation, economic entanglement between rivals, and the weaponization of trade have collectively removed the mechanisms through which conflicts used to end. This is Kelkar's animating intellectual alarm, and by 2026 it has darkened considerably from the more measured risk-mapping tone of his early work.

## The India Lens

Kelkar's application of ideas to India is the most carefully developed and least sentimental dimension of his writing. He uses India not as a feel-good story of strategic cleverness but as a theoretical stress test for what multi-alignment actually costs when instability turns prolonged. His most important formulation appears in "The US–Israel War on Iran: The Risks to India's Strategic Hedging": *"The very region that enables India's strategic autonomy is also the one most capable of constraining it when instability erupts."* This is not a rhetorical flourish — it is the structural paradox that his entire India analysis has been quietly building toward.

He is unusually precise about the material stakes. The Gulf Cooperation Council countries represent India's largest trading partner bloc at approximately $178.56 billion in FY 2024-25; roughly 19 percent of India's total imports arrive from the Middle East; 44 percent of jewellery exports flow to Gulf markets; about 25 percent of auto exports reach GCC buyers. These are structural exposure figures, not decorative statistics. Similarly, his infrastructure analysis — IMEC, INSTC, Chabahar port — names specific projects endangered by Iran instability rather than speaking vaguely about "connectivity."

On India's trade posture, he is particularly pointed. In "India's Trade Deals and the New Meaning of Strategic Autonomy," he writes that *"India did not so much choose this negotiation as accept that refusing accommodation would have carried higher geo-economic costs. Autonomy today is less about refusal and more about calibration."* This is a significant rebuke of the self-congratulatory framing that surrounds Indian foreign policy commentary. He also acknowledges bluntly, in the September 2024 Briefings, that "India lacks significant trade and competitive advantages vis-à-vis ASEAN" — a statement of the kind rarely heard in optimistic India-rising analysis. Atmanirbharta is mentioned alongside US economic nationalism and Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030 as instances of rising "strategic realism and economic nationalism" globally — descriptive, not celebratory. The Modi-Putin photograph that heads Briefing 3 is placed without editorial comment, but its juxtaposition is clearly deliberate.

## Dominant Themes and Distinctive Angles

Kelkar's contribution clusters overwhelmingly at the intersection of three themes: **geopolitics and international relations**, **economics and financial systems**, and **India-specific challenges and opportunities**. These are not separate preoccupations but a single unified field of analysis he calls geoeconomics: the study of how trade, finance, supply chains, and energy flows have become the primary instruments of strategic competition.

On geopolitics, his distinctive angle is structural rather than event-driven. He is uninterested in personalities or diplomatic theater except as symptoms of deeper system conditions. His reading of Trump is analytical rather than polemical: *"Trump's unrelenting transactionalism — viewing every trade move as a one-off, bilateral deal, stripped of strategic context"* is diagnosed as a strategic incoherence, not a moral failure.

On economics and markets, his focus is consistently on trade architecture rather than growth metrics or monetary policy. Tariffs, FTA design, supply chain reconfiguration, sanctions regimes, and technology standard-setting are his instruments. In "Tariffs Are the Noise. Realignment Is the Signal," he argues that a US Supreme Court ruling invalidating Trump's tariffs changes nothing structurally, because the underlying forces driving fragmentation operate far beneath the tariff rate.

On Iran specifically, he has conducted the most analytically sustained case study in his body of work: a five-article arc running from internal protest dynamics through nuclear brinkmanship, energy warfare, India's strategic exposure, and finally the failure of decisive force. It is his most ambitious intellectual project, and Iran functions in it as the ultimate stress test for every thesis he has developed — proxy networks as force multipliers, no-endgame conditions, limits of US compellence, India's fragile hedging.

**Business and Entrepreneurship**, **Leadership and Organizational Culture**, **Learning and Education**, **Consumer Behaviour**, and **Brand and Marketing** are essentially absent from his writing. He is Founding Fuel's geopolitician, not its management thinker.

## Mental Models

Kelkar reaches for a consistent set of frameworks across the body of work. The most load-bearing is **endgame analysis**: not "what is happening" but "what could end this, and why can't it?" This question, applied systematically across Russia-Ukraine, Israel-Hamas, US-Iran, and China-Taiwan, is what distinguishes his analysis from conflict journalism.

His second most important framework is **mesh thinking** rather than bloc thinking. He consistently resists the Cold War II framing because it implies a cleaner bifurcation than actually exists: *"it's a world where the hegemons and, indeed, other nation-states will be entwined with both in a complex web of technology, supply chains, finance and security considerations."* The image of overlapping, mutually constraining dependencies — not rival blocs — is his preferred structural metaphor.

**Chokepoint geography** functions as an analytical anchor throughout: the Strait of Hormuz, Bab el-Mandeb, Red Sea, Suez Canal, and South China Sea appear repeatedly as primary variables rather than contextual background. Roughly a fifth of global oil consumption transiting the Hormuz chokepoint is the kind of anchor fact around which he builds arguments.

**Proxy network analysis** is his most technically detailed framework. The IRGC's architecture — Hezbollah, the Houthis, Kata'ib Hezbollah, the Fatemiyoun Brigade — is mapped across multiple articles as a system of force multiplication specifically designed to offset conventional military inferiority. He treats proxy networks as an asymmetric response to hegemonic power, not as a collection of bad actors.

**Scenario mapping** appears explicitly in "Iran at the Brink: Five Scenarios" and implicitly throughout, as he habitually traces branching outcomes from structural conditions rather than predicting single trajectories.

## Trajectory and Evolution

The intellectual arc across the 22 articles moves in a clear direction, from landscape mapping to system-level alarm. The 2023-2024 pieces — Africa, early Middle East writing — are orienting work, establishing who the players are and what the fault lines look like. The May 2024 three-part hegemony series is the most ambitious theoretical moment of this period: explicitly constructing an "asymmetric cooperation" framework using Keohane and Clayton-Maggiori-Schreger as load-bearing intellectual scaffolding.

The September 2024 Briefings represent a pivot toward pedagogical translation — synthesizing the geopolitical landscape for a business executive audience, connecting structural analysis to corporate strategy consequences. This is Kelkar operating most explicitly as an interpreter between IR theory and management practice.

By early 2025, the writing becomes more scenario-explicit and more attentive to near-term contingencies, particularly around Trump-era trade disruption and the Ukraine minerals negotiation. The January 2026 "A World Without Endgames" represents a qualitative shift in register: he has moved from analyzing specific conflicts to theorizing the systemic condition that prevents their resolution. The Iran war series that follows is the fullest application of this matured framework, and its emotional register is noticeably darker — *"not since the late 1930s has the global order been subjected to such a dense accumulation of stress tests"* signals a level of institutional alarm that his earlier writing held in check.

What has changed, more precisely, is not the underlying thesis but the confidence and urgency with which he states it. In 2023, he was mapping risks. By 2026, he is arguing that the structural conditions generating those risks are themselves becoming permanent.

## Distinctive Voice

Kelkar writes with what might be called **controlled urgency**: the surface is precise, measured, and analytically composed, but the cumulative weight of the argument — no endgames, no coherent US strategy, Iran unresolvable, India's autonomy resting on a fragile regional foundation — is genuinely unsettling if read straight through. He does not editorialize in the manner of opinion writers; he lets structural analysis do the disturbing work.

His rhetorical habits are specific and recognizable. He favors the anchor fact deployed at the start of an analysis — a precise trade figure, a maritime transit statistic, a specific military capability number — before opening into structural argument. He uses diplomatic language ironically: Vietnam's "positive progress" becomes *"diplomatic code for a long road ahead"*; Xi's patronizing comment about a "prosperous United States" gets the single word *"patronisingly added"* inserted without further comment. He occasionally allows himself a phrase of genuine literary economy — *"playing possum"* on China's Middle East positioning, *"powder keg that threatens to explode with just one small miscalculation"* — that punctuates the analytical density without breaking it.

Compared to other Founding Fuel contributors, he is more structurally patient, more willing to defer resolution, and far less interested in actionable prescription. Where FF's editorial culture tends toward "what should leaders do," Kelkar tends toward "here is why the situation cannot be resolved and what that means structurally." This occasionally creates tension with the platform's practical orientation, but it also gives his work a distinctive gravity.

## What He Is Skeptical Of

Kelkar's skepticisms are consistent, specific, and analytically rather than ideologically grounded. He is skeptical of **US coercive capacity**: *"The US' power to induce action and punish has not amounted to much in recent decades"* — a judgment he supports with Afghanistan, Iraq, Russia sanctions, and the Iran war. He is skeptical of **decisive force in asymmetric contexts**, which is essentially the organizing argument of the entire Iran war series. He is skeptical of **China's "friend of the Global South" narrative**, citing Zambia's debt disputes and Kenya's economically unviable BRI highway without sentiment. He is skeptical of **Trump's strategic coherence**, framing it not as malevolence but as a structural inability to think beyond bilateral transactions. He is skeptical of **simple bloc formation**, consistently resisting the Cold War II framing that many analysts find more satisfying.

Most importantly for Indian readers, he is mildly but persistently skeptical of **India's self-congratulatory strategic autonomy narrative**. The paradox he identifies — that India's autonomy is enabled by and endangered by the same regional system — is the most pointed intellectual challenge he directs at his own country's foreign policy self-image.

## Where He Fits in Founding Fuel's Worldview

Kelkar aligns with FF's editorial DNA in his commitment to systems thinking: his analysis of proxy networks, chokepoint geography, and hegemonic transition is precisely the kind of feedback-loop and interdependency analysis that defines FF's intellectual approach. He also shares FF's India-first lens, though his application of it is more structured and more unsentimental than the platform's typical warm treatment of Indian entrepreneurship and innovation.

Where he adds something genuinely unique — and where he most diverges — is in his near-complete absence of interest in FF's core preoccupations with organizational culture, entrepreneurship as mindset, and people management. He is, effectively, a specialist parachuted into a generalist platform, and the discipline he brings from IR theory is not something FF's other contributors provide. He is not writing about how organizations navigate transformation; he is writing about whether the global order itself has any stable structure left to navigate within. This gives his work a different weight and urgency than most of what surrounds it on the platform, but it also means he operates somewhat outside FF's conversational center of gravity. He is Founding Fuel's analyst of last resort — the person the platform needs when the world itself becomes the subject.

## Best Work

**"A World Without Endgames" (January 7, 2026)** is his most important single piece: the moment at which his accumulated case studies crystallize into a structural theory. The argument that uncertainty itself is becoming institutionalized — that the absence of endgames is not a temporary condition but a defining feature of the current order — is his most original contribution.

**"The War in Iran: The Illusion of Decisive Force"** (the concluding article of the Iran series) represents his analytical peak in sustained case-study work. The argument that military superiority does not translate into political resolution, developed across an entire conflict theater, is both rigorous and genuinely counterintuitive against the background of Western triumphalism.

**"The US–Israel War on Iran: The Risks to India's Strategic Hedging" (March 4, 2026)** is his best India-specific piece: the most fully developed statement of his central paradox about Indian strategic autonomy, grounded in specific trade exposure figures and named infrastructure projects.

**"Trade Is the New Geopolitics"** is his clearest statement of the argument that has been building across the entire body of work — that trade architecture, supply chain design, and technology standard-setting have displaced military deployment as the primary instruments of strategic competition. Economical, precise, and genuinely instructive for a business audience.

**"The Future of Hegemony" (May 2024 three-part series)** is his most theoretically ambitious work: the explicit construction of an asymmetric cooperation framework using IR scholarship as scaffolding. It is the piece that most clearly reveals the academic rigor behind the accessible analytical surface.

## Memes, Jokes, and Pop Culture References

If Kelkar were a character in a geopolitics thriller, he would be the analyst in the basement who has read every cable for twenty years and keeps quietly placing memos on his superior's desk that read: *"This cannot end the way you think it will end."* His colleagues find the memos unsettling. They are always correct.

The ideal Kelkar meme is the "expanding brain" format: Panel 1 — "The tariff rate is too high." Panel 2 — "Trade is the new military instrument." Panel 3 — "The architecture of global trade itself is being redesigned." Panel 4 — "The endgame is the absence of endgames." Each panel represents a different level at which the same phenomenon can be analyzed, and Kelkar is always operating at Panel 4.

He is the intellectual equivalent of the Cassandra figure in Greek tragedy: not because no one listens, but because the structural conditions he describes are genuinely resistant to the interventions his audience would prefer to believe are available. His Iran series essentially argues: *the siege will not end because neither side can afford the settlement that would end it.* Cassandra would recognize this argument immediately.

His treatment of India's strategic autonomy recalls the famous Chekhov's gun principle, inverted: if you spend three articles establishing that India's hedging rests on the stability of West Asia, the gun will go off in the fourth article. In "Risks to India's Strategic Hedging," it does.

Finally, Kelkar is the analyst who would appreciate the Dark Forest hypothesis from Liu Cixin's *The Three-Body Problem* — not because he endorses its pessimism, but because it captures the structural logic he identifies in international relations: actors who cannot verify each other's intentions, operating in an environment where cooperation is always available in theory and structurally prevented in practice.

## Intellectual Allies and Adversaries

Kelkar's closest intellectual ally is **Robert Keohane**, whose 1984 framework on hegemonic stability he cites explicitly and deploys as a genuine analytical load-bearer. He would agree with Keohane that international order is a collective good that hegemons provide at cost to themselves, that as hegemonic preponderance declines the institutions it supports become increasingly contested, and that the transition between hegemonic orders is the most dangerous period in international relations. His entire body of work is, in some sense, applied Keohane for the 2020s.

He would also find strong support in **Graham Allison's** work on the "Thucydides Trap" — the structural argument that rising and ruling powers collide not because of individual miscalculation but because the transition itself generates conflict-inducing pressures. Kelkar's treatment of US-China rivalry as "structural and irreversible" is precisely this argument, though he extends it to include trade and supply chains in ways Allison does not fully develop.

On the specific question of trade as geopolitical instrument, he is a fellow traveler with **Fareed Zakaria's** "post-American world" thesis — the argument that unipolarity was always a transient condition — though Kelkar is more structurally rigorous and less comfortable with the optimistic pluralism that sometimes softens Zakaria's analysis.

His most significant intellectual adversary would be **Francis Fukuyama** — not the caricatured Fukuyama of "the end of history," but the more nuanced Fukuyama who has argued that liberal democratic institutions are ultimately more resilient than their current stress tests suggest. Kelkar's "no endgames" thesis is in direct tension with Fukuyama's underlying confidence that liberal international order has self-correcting mechanisms. Where Fukuyama sees institutions bending, Kelkar sees structural conditions that prevent resolution regardless of institutional quality.

He would also push back against **Joseph Nye's** concept of soft power as a meaningful counterweight to hard power in the current moment. Kelkar's analysis consistently shows that reputational influence — America's or anyone else's — has not translated into compellence outcomes. *"The US' power to induce action and punish has not amounted to much in recent decades"* is effectively a challenge to the soft power optimism that Nye represents.

On India specifically, he would be in productive tension with the more enthusiastic proponents of **India's "Vishwaguru" foreign policy framing** — the narrative of India as natural civilizational leader destined to mediate between competing powers. Kelkar's India is a sophisticated and genuinely multi-aligned actor, but one whose autonomy is structurally constrained and whose regional dependencies create vulnerabilities that the self-congratulatory framing consistently underestimates.

## Writing and Argument Style

Kelkar constructs arguments in a characteristic sequence: establish the structural condition, quantify the material stakes, trace the mechanism through which the condition operates, identify the specific points where conventional analysis goes wrong, and conclude with the systemic implication. He rarely inverts this sequence, and he rarely omits any step. This gives his writing a cumulative, architectural quality — each paragraph earns the next.

His persuasion strategy is fundamentally **inductive from evidence to structural claim**. He leads with anchor facts — precise trade figures, specific military capabilities, named infrastructure projects — before moving to interpretive argument. This is not merely rhetorical; it reflects a genuine commitment to grounding structural analysis in material specifics. When he argues that India's strategic autonomy is fragile, he has already given you the $178.56 billion trade figure, the 44 percent jewellery export dependency, and the named corridor projects before he makes the claim. The argument is hard to dismiss because its empirical foundation has been carefully laid.

He handles counterarguments not by directly engaging and refuting them but by **reframing the level of analysis** at which they operate.
