## Core Argument
India's instinct to design friction into systems—not just roads but governance—reflects institutional distrust that slows innovation and growth.

## Key Points
- Speed breakers are symptoms of a deeper governance philosophy based on control through friction rather than enabling movement.
- Bureaucratic and digital systems replicate the same friction-first mindset, suggesting the problem is cultural, not technological.
- Reorienting incentives—rewarding outcomes over process compliance—is required to shift from control-based to flow-based institutional design.

## Open Question
If officials understand the consequences of friction they create, why do systemic incentives remain misaligned—and can incentive redesign alone shift a governance culture deeply rooted in distrust?

## India Angle
India's institutional tendency to build friction into systems—physical speed breakers, bureaucratic approvals, digital controls—reflects a governance mindset rooted in distrust and risk aversion that actively constrains entrepreneurship, innovation, and economic velocity despite improving infrastructure.
