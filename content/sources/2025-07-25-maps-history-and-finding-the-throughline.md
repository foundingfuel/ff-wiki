## Core Argument
Technology adoption succeeds by anchoring to existing habits, not replacing them; maps reveal how geography shapes history and power.

## Key Points
- Competing against trust-based systems (collective memory, local guides) harder than rival products; familiar anchors essential for adoption.
- Same product required three city-specific solutions; India's mapping demanded constant fieldwork, not just satellite data, due to rapid informal urbanization.
- Maps are power narratives—geographic control determines history; geography, not dates, drives economics, wars, and migrations.

## Open Question
How do we reconcile the efficiency of digital mapping systems with the social cohesion and agency that comes from relying on human networks and local knowledge?

## India Angle
India's unique adoption curve for digital mapping—where collective memory and human networks remained stronger competitors than other technologies, requiring localized solutions for Delhi, Mumbai, Bangalore; challenges of mapping informal settlements and remote areas with limited digital infrastructure.
