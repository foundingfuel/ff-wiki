## Core Argument
Conservation succeeds only when it treats communities as essential partners, not obstacles to managed ecosystems.

## Key Points
- Gadgil proved traditional human activity often sustains ecosystems better than exclusionary state management—Sacred Groves case study.
- Indian bureaucracy treats environmental governance as procedural compliance, divorcing it from ground-level stakeholder engagement and equity.
- Rigorous science paired with social empathy requires staying engaged in messy institutional disagreement rather than exiting in principle.

## Open Question
How can governments adopt Gadgil's community-integrated conservation model when economic interests (mining, quarries, infrastructure) structurally oppose devolved decision-making and local veto power?

## India Angle
India's environmental governance has rejected Gadgil's core insight: that conservation must integrate human communities and traditional practices, not exclude them. This remains urgent for Western Ghats policy, sacred grove management, and climate action that marginalises local voices.
