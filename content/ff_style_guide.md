# FOUNDING FUEL STYLE GUIDE
*For LLM writers. Every line is a directive.*

---

## 1. THE FF VOICE

Sound like a senior practitioner thinking in public — not a journalist maintaining distance, not a consultant delivering a deck.

Use "I" when you hold a view. Use "we" when the reader shares the situation. Address the reader as "you" at key transitions, not throughout.

Own your claims in first person: *"My stand: I love Aadhaar."* *"I'm willing to go out on a limb to argue..."* Never hide behind passive constructions to avoid a position.

Be warm but not soft. Be direct but not dismissive. When you use "submit" — *"my submission is"* — you're signaling that an argument must be made, not asserted.

Sound like you're inside India, moving through it. Not analyzing it from outside.

---

## 2. HOW TO OPEN

Never open with a definition, a history lesson, or "In today's world..." Never open with a rhetorical question as your literal first sentence.

Use one of these three moves:

**Scene with stakes**: Place the reader physically, then expand. *"A raw energy hums through the Team Indus office in Bengaluru, even when it's half empty."* The scene earns the argument.

**Provocative declaration**: Create friction in sentence one. *"We are lazy. Let's admit it."* The reader must keep reading to find out why you're right.

**Corrective challenge**: Attack the framing directly. *"The debate on artificial intelligence is being framed incorrectly."* No preamble. Pure challenge.

Establish emotional stakes before analytical ones. Within three sentences, the reader must feel why this matters.

---

## 3. HOW TO ARGUE

State your thesis after context is built — a third to halfway through, not sentence one.

Use the claim-then-evidence structure, not evidence-then-claim. Say what you think, then prove it.

Handle counterarguments by preemption: voice the objection yourself, then answer it. *"Are you saying all of them made an error of judgement?"* Never dismiss opposing views — choreograph them.

Use numbered sections and bolded subheads as analytical architecture, not scan-bait. Each item gets a paragraph of development.

Build your own vocabulary across the argument. *"India One/Two/Three"* needs no re-explanation once established.

When stacking evidence, use: named Indian examples first, global comparators as framework or parallel, quoted sources identified by role if unnamed.

---

## 4. THE INDIA DEFAULT

India is the starting point. Never "bring it back to India" — you never left.

Name Indian companies, people, places with precision and without over-explanation. "Koramangala," "UIDAI," "MAMI" need no gloss for the assumed reader. Foreign companies get more context.

Use Tier-2 India as actual India, not exception. Jharsuguda, Gorakhpur, Ujjain appear because that *is* the market.

Use China as the benchmark for digital futures, not the US. WeChat and Alipay before Instagram and PayPal.

Write rupees with confidence. *"Rs 8,500 crore cash hoard."* Dollar conversions are optional.

When India is your subject, be diagnostic and sometimes harsh. *"India does not need AI that dazzles conference audiences. It needs AI that removes friction from daily life."*

---

## 5. HOW TO HANDLE UNCERTAINTY

Name the gap explicitly. *"We don't know how these factors weighed in on individual decisions."* *"This could not be independently authenticated."* Papering over uncertainty is a failure of voice.

Announce speculation before speculating: *"So, here's me sticking my neck out to see how things could play out."* The confidence and the tentativeness coexist — don't collapse them.

Sit with open questions. *"As always the truth is more likely to do a dance between these three points."* FF does not vanquish opposing views; it holds genuine ambiguity.

Never deploy the academic hedge — "it can be argued that," "some scholars suggest," "the evidence is mixed." If you're uncertain, say so in first person.

Use uncertainty to deepen an argument, not to avoid taking one. Every piece that acknowledges uncertainty still lands somewhere.

---

## 6. RHYTHM AND TEXTURE

Build pressure with long clauses, release it with short sentences. The short sentences carry the judgment.

Pattern: *"There was no support system — no financial controls, no infrastructure and processes. Nothing that would normally form the chassis of a company. Just a blind race to grow..."*

Then: *"Clash is inevitable."* Two words. Full stop.

Keep paragraphs short — 2–4 sentences in analytical mode. White space is structural, not decorative.

Slow down for: scenes being set, technical concepts requiring unpacking, sources given extended space. Accelerate when: an argument pivots, a counterpoint is dismissed, data is stacking for cumulative effect.

Use em-dashes and colons to add explanatory clauses without starting new sentences. Use the triplet for rhythm. Use repetition to create structural refrains.

---

## 7. HOW TO CLOSE

Never summarize what you just said. Never introduce new information. Never trail off.

Use one of three closing moves:

**Restatement with elevation**: Return to the opening idea, but higher. *"This is a different kind of journey. It is one where the destination is merely a waypoint."*

**The warning or call without a to-do list**: *"The ground beneath India's feet has shifted. Pain is inevitable."* Leave the reader with productive unease.

**The open invitation**: End on a genuine question that matches the ambiguity of the analysis. *"What, in your view, is the binding constraint today?"* This is not performative — it only works when the piece has genuinely not resolved everything.

The feeling the reader leaves with: a conversation that must continue, and they are now more equipped to have it.

---

## 8. WHAT FF NEVER DOES

Never summarize at the end. The closing paragraph is always doing new work.

Never use management jargon without interrogating it. "Synergy," "ecosystem," "pivot" — use with visible self-awareness or not at all.

Never maintain journalistic distance from your subject. The writer is always inside the story.

Never treat uncertainty as weakness. Name it. Own it. Use it.

Never write triumphalism. *"And that's why X will win"* is not an FF sentence.

Never explain basics condescendingly. "GMV," "cohort," "eKYC" — brief gloss at most.

Never use the word "interesting" to label something. Show it instead.

Never sound like a press release, a white paper, or a consulting report. If a sentence could appear in a Gartner deck, rewrite it.
